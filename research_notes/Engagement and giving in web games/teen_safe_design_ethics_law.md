## Key question 1 — Which rules in the UK Children's Code, the EU DSA minors guidelines and Irish codes would apply to a school charity website where a play costs €2 and players include under-18s, and which practices do they explicitly discourage?

### Takeaway
The rules that most likely *bind* an Irish school-charity site are the GDPR/Irish Data Protection Act 2018 (digital age of consent 16, with the DPC's "Fundamentals" as the regulator's reading of it), Irish consumer-protection law (the EU blacklist of "direct exhortations to children to buy", plus the 2025 CPC principles on in-game purchases) and Irish gambling law (Key question 2). The UK Children's Code and the Commission's DSA Article 28 guidelines almost certainly do not bind it (UK jurisdiction; DSA Article 28 excludes micro/small enterprises and applies to "online platforms"), and Ireland's Online Safety Code applies only to designated video-sharing platforms — but the three documents are the clearest statements of what regulators now treat as age-appropriate, and they converge on the same list of discouraged practices: engagement nudges (streaks, autoplay, push prompts, reward loops), nudges to hand over data or weaken privacy, low-privacy defaults, profiling/targeted ads, and pressure to spend.

### Inferences
- **Likely applicability for PresLink (opinion, not legal advice):** GDPR + Irish DPA 2018 and the DPC Fundamentals — applies (law + regulator guidance). Irish consumer law incl. the UCPD blacklist and CPC principles — very likely applies to a trader selling plays, although whether a student charity counts as a "trader" is unresolved (Gap). Irish gambling law — applies (Key question 2). DSA Article 28/25 — very unlikely to bind (micro-enterprise exclusion; a leaderboard of initials is at most a "minor and purely ancillary" dissemination feature). UK Children's Code and Online Safety Act — not binding unless the site targets UK users; but the Code is the best-articulated benchmark and costs nothing to follow. Online Safety Code — not applicable (VSPS only).
- **Practices these frameworks explicitly discourage that PresLink should avoid:** streaks/daily-return prompts, push notifications and "come back" emails, autoplay-style "play again" loops, nudges to supply the optional email (pre-ticked boxes, guilt copy), any public display of real names or school emails, profiling or marketing use of data, "buy now"/"get more plays" copy addressed to pupils, hidden fees, bundles framed as virtual tokens with obscure pricing, and a prize for the biggest spender (the current "most plays" spot prize is, in substance, an inducement to buy more plays and the single feature most at odds with the CPC and DSA guidance).
- **Practices they expect:** privacy information in child-readable language at the point of collection (what the email is for, how long it is kept, who sees it); high-privacy defaults (initials only, no public email, opt-out from the board); data minimisation (no date of birth, no names; delete emails after prizes are issued); a DPIA-style note even if short; a named adult contact; and, because the digital age of consent is 16, either not relying on consent from under-16s (e.g. ask for a parent's email for winner contact) or obtaining parental consent for any consent-based processing.
- The Online Safety Code and DSA guidelines use **age assurance** for high-risk content; nothing in them would require ID checks for a €2 skill game, and the DPC Fundamentals warn against collecting extra data just to verify age. A self-declaration plus parent-facing information is the proportionate route (opinion).

## Key question 2 — Is a "stop the light on the mark" timing game with prizes a game of skill or a lottery under Irish and UK law, and what makes the difference?

### Takeaway
**Not legal advice.** In both jurisdictions the line is whether prizes are allocated by **chance**: a reaction-timing game decided by the player's own tap is, on its face, a game of **skill**, which takes it outside "lottery" definitions — but (a) Ireland regulates paid **gaming** as well as lotteries, and its definitions of a "game" include games that are *partly skill and partly chance*, so any randomised element (PresLink's random sweep speed, random mark position and the sub-millisecond random line that decides 999s) gives a cautious reader an argument that chance is present; (b) if the activity were "gaming" or a "lottery", **under-18s would be barred outright** under Irish law, which is why the skill/chance question is the pivotal design decision; (c) a random-draw prize would be a lottery and would need either a permit under the 1956 Act (still the live system, because the GRA 2024's charitable licensing had not opened as of 2025 and reportedly still had not by August 2026) or the GRA 2024 section 92 exemption once commenced (total winnings ≤ €2,000, minimum entry ≤ €5, ≤ 1,500 tickets, no repeat within three months). The UK's "significant proportion" skill test is the clearest statement of the principle and is easy for a timing game to pass if the scoring is deterministic.

### Inferences
- **What makes the difference (both jurisdictions):** (1) payment to enter — present (€2); (2) a prize — present; (3) whether the prize is allocated by **chance** or by **skill**. With (1) and (2) present, everything turns on (3). In Ireland the extra wrinkle is that even a *skill-with-chance* paid game is "gaming", which cannot be promoted for the organiser's benefit without a permit and is closed to under-18s; in the UK the analogue is s.6 "element of chance".
- **Where PresLink stands today (opinion):** the deciding act — tapping when a visible light reaches a mark — is skill. But three design choices hand a sceptic an "element of chance" argument: the random sweep speed per play, the random mark per light, and the random sub-millisecond line position that effectively decides who holds 999 and the "best light" prize. None allocates prizes by chance in the lottery sense, yet each means two equally skilled players face different tasks, which is the kind of variance regulators call chance. Cheap mitigations: one fixed sweep speed; a fixed (or per-day fixed, identical-for-everyone) set of mark positions; quantise the line to the same millisecond grid as taps so a perfect tap is achievable by skill alone; keep the deterministic "earlier score wins" tie-break; measure and disclose device touch latency. These make the "significant proportion prevented from winning by skill" test trivially satisfied and remove the chance argument.
- **Spot prizes:** "best single light" is skill. "**Most plays**" is not chance, but it is a prize for buying the most plays — the design equivalent of a VIP/inducement scheme the GRA 2024 bans for gambling operators and the CPC/DSA guidance discourages for minors; it also undermines the inclusion aim (Key question 4). Replace it with something unrelated to spend (e.g. "most improved", a class/house participation prize, a creative prize) or drop it. Do **not** replace it with a random draw unless the committee is prepared to treat that draw as a lottery (permit under the 1956 Act now; s.92 exemption later) — note that s.92's "≤ €5 minimum entry" is satisfied by a €2 play but the "≤ 1,500 tickets" cap would be hard to reconcile with an open-ended number of plays.
- **Age:** if any part of the arcade is "gaming" or a "lottery" under Irish law, under-18s cannot take part, which would defeat the product. That is the single strongest reason to make the game *purely* skill-based and to say so in the terms, and to have the school's solicitor (or a written answer from the GRAI/Garda licensing office) confirm the position before real money is taken.
- **Prize size:** keeping total prizes well below €2,000 and non-cash (vouchers, experiences) keeps the whole scheme inside the s.92 envelope in case any element is later characterised as a lottery, and is good practice for a youth audience.

### Gaps
- The GRA 2024 section 2 definitions (whether "game" captures *pure*-skill games played for a payment; how "lottery" is defined; whether "gaming" with no chance element is outside the Act) could not be read — this is the critical check and the Act is at the link above.
- Current thresholds under the 1956 Act as amended (e.g. the small "event" lottery exemption) and whether section 92 of the GRA 2024 had been commenced by October 2026 could not be verified.

## Key question 3 — What does research say about spending limits, visible totals and cool-downs on wellbeing and on revenue?

### Takeaway
The evidence base is adult online gambling, not school games, so it transfers only by analogy. Within it: **voluntary limit-setting and personalised spend feedback** are associated with lower spending among the heaviest players (observational, moderate quality); **merely prompting** people to set a limit has tiny uptake (0.7 %) and showed no measurable reduction in net loss in a randomised trial; pop-ups and forced breaks produce small effects. Revenue effects fall mainly on the small tail of highest spenders, which is exactly the tail a charity does not want from under-18s. Experimental work on dark patterns shows the reverse side: aggressive pressure roughly quadruples take-up but produces backlash, mild pressure doubles it without visible backlash — so low-pressure design costs conversions in the short run and buys trust, and the loot-box literature shows that chance-based spend mechanics correlate with problem gambling markers in 16–18-year-olds more strongly than in adults.

### Inferences
- **Translating to a €2 arcade:** the evidence favours *built-in* limits over *prompts to self-limit* (prompt uptake is under 1 %). Concretely: a hard cap on plays per player per day and per campaign; a visible "you have spent €X this campaign" line on the You tab and on the checkout sheet; a short cool-down after a bundle is used up rather than an instant "buy more" button; and no spend-based prize. Because gambling revenue (and loot-box revenue) is concentrated in a small tail, such caps cost little from ordinary players while removing the scenario the committee cannot afford — a child spending €40 in an afternoon — which, in a school, would be a safeguarding and reputational event, not a revenue win.
- **Wellbeing:** no study measures wellbeing effects of caps in children's games; the strongest inference is from the loot-box literature (chance-based spend mechanics are the harmful variant; a transparent, skill-based, fixed-price play is the low-risk variant) and from the DSA/CPC guidance that treats pressure-to-spend as the harm.
- **Revenue:** Luguri & Strahilevitz imply that removing mild pressure (scarcity, social proof, pre-selection) will reduce short-run conversion measurably; the counterweight is reputational — aggressive patterns generate backlash, and a school charity's "customers" are also its community (see Key question 5 on trust surveys).

### Gaps
- No studies on spending caps, visible totals or cool-downs in charity games, school fundraising or teen skill games were found; everything above is adult gambling or general consumer evidence.
- The direction of the Norwegian mandatory-break findings (Auer, Hopfgartner & Griffiths, 2019–20) could not be verified and is omitted.
- Revenue impact of Norsk Tipping's mandatory limits is not documented in a source I could cite.
- Streak research (e.g. Silverman & Barasch 2023, Journal of Consumer Research, on how broken streaks reduce continuation) could not be verified this session; see Key question 5.

## Key question 4 — What do schools and youth organisations recommend about fundraising games (inclusion, pressure, parental consent), and are there Irish Department of Education or school-charity guidelines?

### Takeaway
No Irish Department of Education guidance specific to fundraising games or raffles involving pupils was found. ## Key question 5 — What design patterns keep an under-18 audience's trust (transparency about where the money goes, showing fees, clear scoring, initials-only anonymity, easy sign-out, no dark patterns)?

### Takeaway
Regulators (ICO, DPC, Commission, CPC) and the deceptive-design literature converge on one positive set: real-money prices stated plainly (no opaque virtual currency), no urgency, scarcity or nagging, no confirmshaming or guilt on exit, symmetrical choices, high-privacy defaults with minimal data and child-readable explanations at the point of use, transparent scoring, visible spend totals with caps, and a specific account of where the money goes. The evidence that honesty "pays" is indirect: experiments show aggressive manipulation triggers backlash while mild manipulation is profitable and invisible, Irish surveys show transparency about use of funds is the top driver of charity trust, and the loot-box literature shows chance-based spend is the harmful variant. PresLink already has most of the positive set (initials-only identity, optional email, visible total, published scoring); the gaps are the "most plays" prize, spend caps/visible totals, fee disclosure and child-readable notices.

- [OPINION][U] Platform conventions reputable youth products use: **PEGI/IARC** "In-Game Purchases (Includes Random Items)" descriptors (2020); Apple **Ask to Buy** and Google **Family Link** purchase approval; Epic's post-settlement hold-to-purchase and self-service refunds; Roblox's parent-set monthly spend limits (2024). — [PEGI](https://pegi.info/); [Epic Cabined Accounts](https://www.epicgames.com/site/en-US/news/introducing-cabined-accounts); [Roblox](https://corp.roblox.com/newsroom/2024/11/new-parental-controls)

### Inferences
- **Trust checklist for PresLink (opinion, mapped to the sources above):**
  1. *Money:* price per play and per bundle in euro side by side; the Stripe fee per play and the net to the charity; the "raised so far" counter with the beneficiary named and the charity's registered name/number; a one-page post-campaign report.
  2. *Pressure:* no spend-based prize; a per-player daily and campaign cap with a visible running total; cool-down after a bundle; no scarcity/urgency copy except the true end date; no notifications, streaks or return prompts; neutral exit wording.
  3. *Fairness:* scoring formula explained in plain words at the point of play (already drafted); fixed sweep speed and fixed/quantised marks (Key question 2); disclose touch-latency compensation; deterministic tie-break; a "how to report a problem" link.
  4. *Identity and data:* chosen letters only; opt-out from the public board; email optional and explained (purpose, retention, who sees it); prefer a parent's email for under-16s; no marketing use; delete after prizes; a child-readable privacy note and a parents' page; a named adult contact.
  5. *Leaving:* a visible way to stop (sign out, delete my record, refund on request) that is as easy as paying.
- **Participation vs honesty:** the available evidence suggests honest design will lose some conversions that mild pressure would have captured (Luguri), but in a closed school community the "customers" are repeat players, parents and staff whose trust the charity needs for every future campaign; the Charities Regulator data and the overhead-aversion experiment suggest specific transparency about where money goes is itself a participation driver, not just a cost.

### Gaps
- No experimental evidence specific to teenagers on whether low-pressure design increases or decreases participation in charity or school games was found.
- Figures for Gneezy et al. 2014, Mathur et al. 2019 and the Commission's 2022 behavioural study are from memory and need checking.
- The exact wording of the ICO's position on "features that encourage extended use" and the DPC Fundamentals could not be verified this session.
