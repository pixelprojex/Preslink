# Online donation and micro-payment conversion: evidence notes for a small charity arcade (PresLink Arcade, Cork)

*Evidence tags used throughout: **[STRONG]** = randomised field experiments with large samples, replicated results, or meta-analyses; **[MODERATE]** = a single field experiment or a large observational dataset; **[WEAK]** = lab/scenario studies, small samples, or contested/failed replications; **[PRACTITIONER]** = vendor, platform or charity claims with no published methodology; **[LAW]** = primary legislation or regulator text; **[LAW-COMMENTARY]** = law-firm or trade summaries of the law. Method note: web page fetches were blocked in this environment (DNS/proxy) and the shared search budget ran out before the last round of verification searches, so a few well-known numbers are marked "recalled, verify" and every section's Gaps list says what could not be confirmed.*

## KQ1. Best-evidenced nudges for the share of visitors who pay and the average amount (defaults, ask ladders, social information, matching) with effect sizes

### Takeaway
The best-replicated levers are: (a) a 1:1 match or announced seed money, which raises both participation and revenue (bigger match ratios add nothing); (b) suggested/default amounts, which reliably pull the modal gift to the suggestion but trade participation against gift size (low anchors raise the number of payers and lower the average; high anchors do the reverse; aggregate revenue is often flat); (c) social information about a high-but-plausible prior gift (90th–95th percentile), which lifts average gifts by roughly a tenth; and (d) cheap reciprocity/personalisation touches (a small gift plus a personal message doubled-to-tripled participation). Deadlines by themselves do not change whether people give; reminders do.

### Cited Findings

#### Matching and seed money
- Karlan & List (AER 2007), direct-mail field experiment with over 50,000 prior donors: a match offer raised both revenue per solicitation and the response rate; $2:$1 and $3:$1 matches did no better than $1:$1; the effect was concentrated in "red" states (+55% revenue per solicitation) with little effect in "blue" states — [Karlan & List AER paper (ISPS PDF)](https://isps.yale.edu/sites/default/files/publication/2012/12/AER%2C%202007%2C%20Karlan%2C%20DoesPriceMatter.pdf); [J-PAL evaluation summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1448?lang=en). **[STRONG]** (Recalled from the abstract, not re-verified this session: +19% revenue per solicitation and +22% probability of giving.)
- Karlan, List & Shafir (J. Public Economics 2011), "Small matches": two mailings to nearly 20,000 prior donors testing $1:$1 and $1:$3 matches found only weak evidence that either match worked; active supporters responded positively while lapsed donors were unaffected or put off; a high example gift ($75) combined with a below-1:1 ratio could backfire — [Monash record](https://research.monash.edu/en/publications/small-matches-and-charitable-giving-evidence-from-a-natural-field/); [summary page](https://stafforini.com/works/karlan-2011-small-matches-charitable/). **[MODERATE]**
- Seed money (see KQ2 for detail): raising announced seed from 10% to 67% of the goal produced a nearly six-fold increase in contributions with significant effects on both participation and average gift — [List & Lucking-Reiley JPE 2002 (EconPapers)](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/ucpjpolec/v_3a110_3ay_3a2002_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a215-233.htm). **[STRONG]**

#### Suggested amounts, anchors, ask ladders and defaults
- Goswami & Urminsky (J. Marketing Research 2016), "When should the ask be a nudge? The effect of default amounts on charitable donations": across eight studies plus a large natural field experiment, a low default raised the donation rate (the "lower-bar" effect) but reduced the average gift (the "scale-back" effect); defaults increased revenue in the field study, with the authors warning the two effects can cancel out — [SUNY publication record](https://researchconnect.suny.edu/en/publications/when-should-the-ask-be-a-nudge-the-effect-of-default-amounts-on-c/). **[STRONG]** (Note: the search tool's summary mislabelled this paper as "Jung et al."; the JMR 2016 paper with this title is Goswami & Urminsky.)
- Altmann, Falk, Heidhues & Jayaraman (IZA DP 8680), field experiment on a large charitable donation website: modal contributions in each arm matched the specified default, but defaults had no impact on aggregate donations because they pushed some people up and others down — [IZA discussion paper PDF](https://docs.iza.org/dp8680.pdf). **[STRONG]**
- Tailored anchor grids (Liberali, De Bruyn, Prokopec), field experiment with 50,000 donors: raising the smallest suggested amount or the step-up across the grid increased donation size; only a higher smallest amount reduced the chance of giving; tailoring the grid to past giving increased donation amounts by 22% and net margins by 36% versus control — [ERIM seminar record](https://www.erim.eur.nl/research/events/detail/2036); [ESSEC record (Prokopec & De Bruyn 2009)](https://faculty.essec.edu/en/research/the-impact-of-anchors-on-donors-behavior-a-field-experiment). **[MODERATE]**
- Low anchors cost more than high anchors add: in a pay-what-you-wish museum field test, a one-cent anchor pulled average payment down to $2.47 versus $3.24 with no suggestion, while a $5 anchor barely changed giving relative to no anchor — [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/suggesting-low-gift-amounts-can-drag-down-fundraising-results-study-finds/). **[MODERATE]**
- UK charity A/B test reported by CIM: higher suggested amounts (£20/£80/£150 versus £10/£50/£100) gave about a 45% uplift in giving, with more donors and more varied amounts — [CIM Charity & Social Marketing Group](https://sigs.cim.co.uk/charity-and-social-marketing-group/news/2020/how-powerful-is-anchoring-examples-from-charitable-giving). **[PRACTITIONER]**
- "Even a penny helps" (legitimising paltry contributions, LPC): a meta-analysis (k = 34, n = 3,181) found higher compliance (r = .22) but smaller mean gifts and similar totals — [Arizona record (Communication Research 2017)](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-legitimization-of-paltry-contributions-as-a-compliance-gainin/); a 2016 meta-analysis found compliance rose without a marked fall in per-contributor amount, so totals were higher than control — [Buffalo record (Psychological Reports 2016)](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-legitimization-of-paltry-favors-com/); the technique works for on-the-spot requests but mail-in follow-through collapsed — [Reeves et al. record](https://augusta.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/legitimizing-paltry-contributions-onthespot-vs-mailin-requests/); overview in an EA Forum literature review — [Review: what works to promote charitable donations](https://ea.greaterwrong.com/posts/WTpwDW8dHWzfiaumx/review-what-works-to-promote-charitable-donations). **[STRONG for participation; MODERATE for revenue]**
- Unit asking (Hsee et al., Psychological Science 2013): asking "how much for one person?" before "how much for the group?" raised totals about 90% in an employee study and more than 500% in a 101-student study — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/bringing-in-more-donations-to-the-cause-at-no-extra-cost.html); replicated with smaller effects — [Data Colada](https://datacolada.org/?p=143); replicated across child, animal and environmental causes — [Judgment and Decision Making 2020](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/20/200508/jdm200508.html); a 2023 LSE study found the boost is a one-off rather than something that scales with repeated unit asks — [LSE Research Online](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/119836). **[MODERATE]**

#### Social information
- Shang & Croson, public-radio phone pledge experiments: callers randomly told "another donor gave $75 / $180 / $300" gave more; the most effective reference was at the 90th–95th percentile of prior contributions; renewing members were not influenced; the authors argue social information works when the "right" amount is ambiguous — [Knowledge at Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/raising-the-bar-the-role-of-social-information-in-charitable-giving/); [working paper PDF](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2013/09/1302.pdf); book-chapter excerpt — [Nonprofit Quarterly](https://nonprofitquarterly.org/social-influences-in-giving). A replication found no effect, but its sample was mostly renewing members — [Emerald replication study](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/S0193-230620150000018002). **[MODERATE]** (Recalled, not re-verified: the $300 condition raised average gifts by roughly 12%.)
- Smith, Windmeijer & Wright (Economic Journal 2015), 300,000 donations to more than 10,000 London Marathon pages on JustGiving/Virgin Money Giving: a £10 rise in the mean of past visible donations raises the next gift by about £2.50 (£3.50 in the 2012 working paper); a single visible small donation lowers subsequent gifts by about £5; reactions depend on the ordering of gifts, not their number; donors appear to benchmark against peers rather than infer charity quality — [Bristol CMPO WP 13/302 abstract](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/cmpo/publications/papers/2013/abstract302.html); [University of Bristol press release](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2012/8648.html). **[STRONG observational]**
- Descriptive norms: Agerström, Carlsson, Nicklasson & Guntell (J. Economic Psychology 2016) field experiment — "most others donated" messages raised giving well above a standard altruistic appeal, and local norms beat global ones — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joepsy/v52y2016icp147-153.html). Asking donors to guess the norm before revealing it increased giving further — [IfW Kiel working paper](https://www.ifw-kiel.de/publications/social-comparison-nudges-guessing-the-norm-increases-charitable-giving-23496). **[MODERATE]**
- Downward social information (being told about a lower gift) is examined in a 2025 Experimental Economics article — [Cambridge Core](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/impact-of-downward-social-information-on-contribution-decisions/5B09EA278BFAC7F2DFB0319E13C9D11C). (Pointer only; content not read.)

#### Reciprocity, personalisation, reminders, deadlines, overhead
- Behavioural Insights Team workplace-giving trial with 6,175 bankers: sweets alone 11% donated, a personalised email from the charity CEO 12%, both together 17%, versus 5% in control (effects roughly additive) — [GOV.UK Civil Service Quarterly](https://quarterly.blog.gov.uk/2013/07/12/behavioural-insights-and-charitable-giving); [Civil Society News](https://www.civilsociety.co.uk/news/giving-bankers-sweets-makes-them-donate-more--nudge-unit-research-finds.html); [BIT "Applying behavioural insights to charitable giving" PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/government/uploads/system/uploads/attachment_data/file/203286/BIT_Charitable_Giving_Paper.pdf). Year-two follow-up: first-time sweets recipients more than tripled (3.1% to 9.7%) but giving halved among those receiving sweets a second time — [University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2013/9770.html). World Bank staff replication: 5.4% versus 2.8% one week after a chocolate plus note — [World Bank Development Talk](https://blogs.worldbank.org/developmenttalk/what-motivates-charitable-giving). NPC put the return on the sweets at roughly 30:1 — [NPC](https://www.thinknpc.org/?p=1163). **[MODERATE]**
- Damgaard & Gravert (J. Behavioral and Experimental Economics 2017), "Now or never!": about 53,000 prior donors of a Danish charity received emails/SMS with varying deadlines; deadlines did not change whether people gave, only when ("now-or-never": people give immediately or not at all); the working-paper version found a reminder raised both the likelihood and the amount — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/soceco/v66y2017icp78-87.html); [Aarhus working paper PDF](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/afn/wp/14/wp14_03.pdf); related deadline/procrastination field experiment — [Knowles, Servátka & Sullivan](https://ideas.repec.org/p/otg/wpaper/1501.html). **[STRONG]**
- Overhead aversion: Gneezy, Keenan & Gneezy (Science 2014) — donors told another donor had covered overhead were 80% more likely to give (94% with a match on top), and in another experiment removing overhead raised total donations 64% — [Science article PDF](https://Rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/Science-2014-Gneezy-632-5.pdf); [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/donors-are-80-more-likely-to-donate-if-entire-gift-goes-to-a-cause/); [HBS Working Knowledge](https://hbswk.hbs.edu/item/donors-are-turned-off-by-overhead-costs-here-s-what-charities-can-do). **[STRONG]**

### Inferences
- The €2 / 3-for-€5 / 7-for-€10 grid will function as an ask ladder: most payers will choose a displayed option rather than type their own (Altmann; Goswami & Urminsky). Expect the standard trade-off: a prominent €2 maximises the share who pay (lower-bar) but caps revenue; pre-selecting the €5 bundle is the classic "middle option" play, but direct experimental support for decoy tiers in charity is thin (see Gaps). The evidence says the net revenue outcome is genuinely uncertain, so a two-arm test of "€2 pre-selected" versus "€5 pre-selected" is worth running.
- Any visible social proof should surface a high-but-plausible benchmark (for example, "most parents choose the 7-play bundle" if true, or the latest larger bundle purchase), never a feed dominated by €2 plays: visible small gifts drag later gifts down (Smith et al.).
- A sponsor match is worth seeking and 1:1 is as good as 2:1; a match framed against a high example gift with a weak ratio can backfire (Karlan, List & Shafir). An announced seed from a sponsor is better evidenced than a countdown.
- School push channels (class WhatsApp groups, email, assembly) are reminders, which do move giving; countdown urgency on its own mostly shifts timing.
- "Even one €2 play helps" copy should raise participation among students at little cost to average spend, since the base price is already small.

### Gaps
- The precise Karlan & List effects (+19% revenue per solicitation, +22% response) and Shang & Croson's ~12% uplift are recalled from the abstracts; page fetches were blocked and the search budget ran out before they could be re-verified.
- Reiley & Samek, "Round Giving" (Economic Inquiry 2019; round-number asks in public-TV fundraising) was not retrieved; no sourced effect size.
- No charity-specific field experiment on decoy/asymmetric-dominance tiers was found; decoy evidence comes from consumer pricing and lab contexts.
- The GiveNow (Australia) A/B report on pre-selected $50/$100 defaults was located but its results were not extractable — [GiveNow A/B PDF](https://www.ourcommunity.com.au/files/innovationlab/2018-11-GiveNowAB.pdf).
- Gneezy et al. (Science 2010) on pay-what-you-want with half to charity ("shared social responsibility"), directly relevant to pay-to-play pricing, was not retrieved.

## KQ2. Progress thermometers and goal proximity, including the risk of a stalled bar

### Takeaway
Goal proximity is a real, reasonably well-evidenced driver: contributions rise as a fund nears its target (the goal gradient), most strongly for small goals and near deadlines, and fall once the goal is hit; the mirror image is that a nearly empty bar signals low impact and campaigns that stall in the middle rarely recover, which is why pre-seeding the total matters (announced seed money raised contributions about six-fold in a field experiment).

### Cited Findings
- Cryder, Loewenstein & Seltman (J. Experimental Social Psychology 2013), "Goal gradient in helping behavior": in an internet field study of an online micro-lending site and a large field experiment, funds close to their goal received more donations than funds far from it; a scenario study replicated the effect and mediation analyses showed perceived impact, and the satisfaction it brings, account for it — [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/GoalGradient.pdf); [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/goal-gradient-in-helping-behavior/); German summary — [University of Mannheim](https://www.uni-mannheim.de/forschung-erleben/artikel/spenden-auf-der-zielgeraden/). **[MODERATE–STRONG]**
- Kuppuswamy & Bayus (J. Business Venturing 2017), large Kickstarter field study: support increases as funding approaches the target and drops significantly after the goal is reached; the proximity effect is stronger near the deadline, for smaller goals, and for projects with limited early support — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbvent/v32y2017i1p72-89.html); earlier working-paper summary — [Yannig Roth blog](https://yannigroth.com/2013/02/24/the-dynamics-of-backer-support-in-crowdfunding-findings-from-kickstarter). **[STRONG observational]**
- List & Lucking-Reiley (J. Political Economy 2002): 3,000 households in six groups of 500, each asked to fund one computer; raising announced seed money from 10% to 67% of the goal produced a nearly six-fold increase in contributions with significant effects on participation rate and average gift; a refund guarantee added a more modest 20% — [EconPapers record](https://econpapers.repec.org/article/ucpjpolec/v_3a110_3ay_3a2002_3ai_3a1_3ap_3a215-233.htm); [paper PDF](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreoni/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/8%20List%20Lucking-Riley%202002.pdf); a later Sierra Club mailing found similar seed-money effects — [NBER Reporter 2008](https://www2.nber.org/reporter/2008number4/list.html). **[STRONG]**
- Kannan, Trusov and co-authors (Marketing Science) describe a middle "getting crowded" phase in which most crowdfunding projects stall or fade and need social interaction to re-ignite; once momentum builds, later contributors follow — [Maryland Smith research summary](https://www.rhsmith.umd.edu/research/how-have-successful-crowdfunding-campaign); [INFORMS release](https://www.informs.org/News-Room/INFORMS-Releases/News-Releases/Marketing-Researchers-Identify-the-Three-Most-Powerful-Drivers-of-Effective-Crowdfunding). **[MODERATE]**
- Early momentum: in 669 Kickstarter projects, early backers and early funds mediate the effect of the creator's social capital on success — [Colombo, Franzoni & Rossi-Lamastra PDF](https://re.public.polimi.it/bitstream/11311/861985/2/11311-861985_Rossi.pdf); a multi-platform study names crowd appeal, momentum of contributions and variation in contribution amounts as key success signals — [arXiv 2101.06315](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2101.06315); in 9,425 Ulule campaigns, strong ties among early backers support success — [M@n@gement](https://management-aims.com/index.php/mgmt/article/view/4530/13806). **[MODERATE]**
- Bystander dynamics: on the Taiwanese platform flyingV, a high ratio of "bystanders" (people who follow but do not pay) reduced pledges, consistent with diffusion of responsibility — [NTU Management Review paper](https://review.management.ntu.edu.tw/paper/9192-P.pdf). **[WEAK]**
- Peer effects on JustGiving pages "cannot be explained purely by donor behaviour around fundraising targets" — [Bristol CMPO abstract](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/cmpo/publications/papers/2013/abstract302.html). **[STRONG observational]**
- Practitioner claims about targets are inconsistent: "pages with a target raise 46% more" — [Prostate Cancer Research](https://www.prostate-cancer-research.org.uk/how-to-get-the-most-out-of-your-fundraising-page); [Marie Curie](https://www.mariecurie.org.uk/get-involved/charity-events/justgiving) — versus "17% more" — [St Joseph's Hospice](https://www.stjh.org.uk/support-us/fundraise-as-you-shop-and-sell/fundraising-on-justgiving/); [Beat](https://www.beateatingdisorders.org.uk/support-our-work/fundraise-for-beat/setting-up-a-just-giving-page/); the same guides claim donating to your own page first raises 54% to 84% more — [UNICEF UK](https://www.unicef.org.uk/?p=24828). **[PRACTITIONER; no methodology; likely confounded by fundraiser effort]**
- Perceived personal impact is the mechanism in both the goal-gradient and overhead studies, and "making a real difference" is the trust factor most cited by the UK public (70%) — [Charity Commission, Public Trust in Charities 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/research-into-public-trust-in-charities-and-research-with-charity-trustees-2025/public-trust-in-charities-2025). **[STRONG survey]**

### Inferences
- Show the "raised so far" bar only once it is credibly non-empty: open with a sponsor or parents'-association seed, or pre-sold bundles, so launch day shows a meaningful fraction (the seed-money experiment is the strongest single piece of evidence here).
- Prefer several small sequential goals ("€500 unlocks the prize fund", "€1,000 unlocks game 3") over one large goal: smaller targets and proximity both amplify the effect, and each completion re-creates the "almost there" zone instead of the post-goal slump.
- Plan a mid-campaign re-ignition (match day, class-versus-class week, a new game) because the middle is where campaigns stall.
- If the bar stalls below roughly a fifth, switch the display to absolute plays/totals or a "this week" counter; the proximity literature implies a low percentage actively signals low impact.

### Gaps
- Koo & Fishbach's "small-area hypothesis" (emphasise progress made early and distance remaining late) was not retrieved; verify before relying on it.
- No experiment was found that directly compares a visible stalled thermometer with no thermometer; the inference rests on the impact mechanism and observational crowdfunding dynamics.
- Mollick (2014) on failing Kickstarter projects failing by large margins was not retrieved.
- No study tests a goal bar for micro-payment game plays rather than donations.

## KQ3. Impact statements, beneficiary stories and photos (identifiable victim effect), and the "drop in the bucket" risk

### Takeaway
The classic identifiable-victim result (a named child draws far more than statistics; adding statistics to the story lowers giving) is now contested: the 2016 meta-analysis found r ≈ .05, a 2023 bias-adjusted reanalysis found essentially zero, and a preregistered replication of Small et al. failed. What survives is that concrete, bounded, per-unit framing ("€2 does X") raises perceived impact, while dry effectiveness statistics do nothing on average and reduced giving among small donors in a large field test.

### Cited Findings
- Small, Loewenstein & Slovic (OBHDP 2007), "Sympathy and callousness": participants could give up to $5 of experiment earnings to Save the Children; an identifiable child drew substantially more than statistical victims, and presenting the child together with statistics reduced giving; priming deliberative thought, or teaching people about the effect, reduced giving to the identifiable victim without raising giving to statistical victims — [Stanford GSB Insights](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/increase-charitable-donations-appeal-heart-not-head); [Facing History summary](https://www.facinghistory.org/resource-library/psychic-numbing-genocide); [FORRT replication atlas entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/j.obhdp.2006.01.005/). **[WEAK–MODERATE given replication status]**
- Lee & Feeley (Social Influence 2016) meta-analysis of 41 effects: overall r = .05, small but significant; most reliable with a single victim, a photographed child in poverty, low victim responsibility, and a monetary ask — [SKKU record](https://pure.skku.edu/en/publications/the-identifiable-victim-effect-a-meta-analytic-review/); [Wikipedia summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifiable_victim_effect). **[STRONG meta-analysis, small effect]**
- Maier et al. (2023) reanalysis: moderate evidence of publication bias; bias-adjusted effect r = .002; three of the highest-powered studies (one with 12,802 participants, r = .004) show near-zero effects; a 2024 Collabra: Psychology preregistered replication of Small et al. found no support (ηp² = .000, 95% CI [.000, .003]) — [Wikipedia summary with citations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifiable_victim_effect); [FORRT entry](https://forrt.org/flora-replication-atlas/doi/10.1016/j.obhdp.2006.01.005/); [HKU record](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/348385); a 2024 Judgment and Decision Making replication of Kogut & Ritov's single-victim effect — [Cambridge Core](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/3DA967642DBCD4E6117CCA104F3D6854/core-reader). **[STRONG against a large effect]**
- Karlan & Wood (NBER w20047; Freedom from Hunger direct mail, 21,643 donors): every letter had a beneficiary story; adding scientific impact evidence had no effect on average likelihood or amount; among recent donors, large prior donors gave more and small prior donors gave less — [NBER working paper PDF](https://www.nber.org/papers/w20047.pdf); [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/print/pdf/node/1909). **[STRONG]**
- Proportion dominance / drop in the bucket: people perceive more utility in helping 10 of 11 than 10 of 1,000, and perceived utility mediates giving — [PMC article](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9202475/); Ein-Gar & Give'on (2022): a smaller reference set raises giving (proportion dominance) while perceived global neediness pushes the other way — [Frontiers in Psychology](https://frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.800867/full); the bird-rescue scope-insensitivity example and individual-story-plus-numbers backfire are summarised in — [PMC4747588](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4747588); practitioner synthesis: use proportion language when you can solve a large share of a bounded problem — [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/how-proportion-dominance-gets-in-the-way-of-effective-giving/). **[MODERATE]**
- Perceived impact mediates goal-gradient giving — [Cryder et al. PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/GoalGradient.pdf) — and overhead aversion — [Gneezy et al. Science PDF](https://Rady.ucsd.edu/_files/faculty-research/uri-gneezy/Science-2014-Gneezy-632-5.pdf). **[STRONG]**
- Unit asking is the best-replicated per-unit framing result (see KQ1) — [APS Observer](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/publications/observer/obsonline/bringing-in-more-donations-to-the-cause-at-no-extra-cost.html); [JDM 2020 replication](https://dlab.sauder.ubc.ca/sjdm/journal/20/200508/jdm200508.html). **[MODERATE]**
- Photos: JustGiving-derived charity guides claim pages with pictures or videos raise 14% more per photo — [Prostate Cancer Research](https://www.prostate-cancer-research.org.uk/how-to-get-the-most-out-of-your-fundraising-page). **[PRACTITIONER]**
- Trust drivers (UK public, Charity Commission/BMG 2025): 57% report high trust; "making a real difference" 70%, high ethical standards 66%, maximising spend on the end cause 64% (up 7 points); donations reaching the end cause remains the most important factor — [Charity Commission 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/research-into-public-trust-in-charities-and-research-with-charity-trustees-2025/public-trust-in-charities-2025); [2025 report PDF](https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/6867bd2e2557debd867cbcbd/Public_Trust_in_Charities_2025.pdf); the 2026 edition reports a shift from narrow financial efficiency toward demonstrated purpose and outcomes — [Charity Commission 2026](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/research-into-public-trust-in-charities-and-research-with-charity-trustees-2026/public-trust-in-charities-2026). **[STRONG survey]**

### Inferences
- Use one concrete impact unit tied to the price point ("€2 = one X"; "7 plays = a full Y") and keep it bounded ("€600 completes the Z for the school"); per-unit tangibility and proportion framing are what the surviving evidence supports. Do not put large statistics ("1 in 5 children…") on the same screen as the story.
- A single real, named project or beneficiary with a photo is still worth doing because it is cheap and the meta-analytic effect is positive, but do not expect it to double conversion.
- For a school audience of mostly small payers, effectiveness/evidence copy is not a conversion lever (Karlan & Wood): keep "what the money does" short and vivid and put detail behind a link.

### Gaps
- Exact means from Small et al. 2007 (commonly quoted as about $2.38 identifiable vs $1.14 statistical vs $1.43 combined, out of $5) were not re-verified; treat "substantially more" as the sourced claim.
- Cryder, Loewenstein & Scheines (2013) on "tangibility" and Sudhir, Roy & Cherian (2016) field evidence on sympathy biases were not retrieved.
- No experiment on impact framing for pay-to-play games or child/teen players was found.

## KQ4. Checkout friction: mobile conversion, wallet payments, steps and fields

### Takeaway
Mobile is the majority of donation-page traffic but converts markedly worse (M+R 2025: 8% mobile versus 11% desktop; 4% on mobile for small nonprofits; mobile is 52% of visits, 43% of transactions and 28% of revenue). Stripe's own A/B data show wallets (Apple Pay/Google Pay/Link) lift conversion by roughly 10–14% and cut median checkout time about three-fold. Baymard's survey puts "checkout too long or complicated" at 18% and "didn't trust the site with my card" at 19% of addressable abandonments, so the fixes are a hosted wallet-first checkout, no account creation, and visible trust cues.

### Cited Findings
- M+R Benchmarks 2025: 11% of desktop users who landed on a primary donation page completed a gift versus 8% of mobile users; small nonprofits converted just 4% of mobile visitors; mobile users were 52% of visits but 43% of donation transactions and 28% of revenue — [M+R Benchmarks: website performance](https://mrbenchmarks.com/website-performance/); [2025 edition page](https://2025.mrbenchmarks.com/website-performance.html); secondary summary — [iDonate](https://idonate.com/blog/2025-mr-benchmarks-what-nonprofits-must-know-about-online-giving-and-donation-pages). A UK summary quotes different numbers (11% mobile, 12% desktop) — [Directory of Social Change](https://www.dsc.org.uk/content/six-tips-for-charities-to-optimise-online-donations-and-boost-income/) — treat as an outlier. **[MODERATE industry benchmark]**
- Stripe Sessions 2024 (aggregated merchant A/B tests): adding Apple Pay produced a roughly 10% statistically significant conversion lift; Link produced an average 14% lift in returning-user conversion on merchants with many repeat buyers; Apple Pay, Google Pay and Link checkouts had a median checkout speed about three times faster than other methods — [Stripe Sessions 2024: A look under Stripe's hood](https://stripe.com/sessions/2024/a-look-under-stripes-hood-using-data-to-demystify-payments). Stripe cites studies showing about 2x conversion when Apple Pay is shown early via the Express Checkout Element rather than at the end of checkout — [Stripe blog](https://stripe.com/en-ro/blog/testing-the-conversion-impact-of-50-plus-global-payment-methods). WooCommerce repeats Stripe claims of +22.3% from Apple Pay and +7.4% from each additional relevant payment method — [WooCommerce](https://woocommerce.com/posts/stripe-optimized-checkout-suite/). **[PRACTITIONER/vendor; direction consistent, magnitudes vary]**
- Stripe "State of checkouts": 58% of European checkouts had at least three basic errors (2020) — [Stripe, European checkouts 2020](https://stripe.com/fr-ca/guides/european-checkouts-in-2020); 99% of North American sites had at least five basic errors, such as omitting popular payment methods and not letting customers save details (2022) — [Stripe, North American checkouts 2022](https://stripe.com/en-lv/guides/state-of-north-american-checkouts-2022); the 2022 European guide has a "top mobile optimisation errors" section — [Stripe, European checkouts 2022](https://stripe.com/en-bg/guides/state-of-european-checkouts-2022); Australian top-100 sites: nearly all had five or more errors — [SmartCompany](https://www.smartcompany.com.au/retail/ecommerce-sites-basic-checkout-errors/). **[PRACTITIONER]**
- Baymard: average documented cart abandonment 70.22% (updated 22 September 2025); about 43% of abandoners were just browsing; after removing browsers, reasons reported for 2025 are extra costs 39%, slow delivery 21%, lack of trust with payment information 19%, forced account creation 19%, checkout too long/complicated 18%, unsatisfactory returns 15%, website errors 15% — [Baymard cart abandonment list](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate); [Statista chart of the Baymard survey](https://statista.com/statistics/1228452/reasons-for-abandonments-during-checkout-united-states); secondary reports — [Shiprocket](https://checkout.shiprocket.in/blog/latest-cart-abandonment-statistics-2025/); [eDesk](https://www.edesk.com/blog/shopping-cart-abandonment). Baymard cautions that "too long checkout" should not be read as "a one-page checkout is the fix" — [Baymard](https://baymard.com/lists/cart-abandonment-rate). The reason percentages were seen only in secondary reports and some sites give different figures (e.g., forced account 24%, extra costs 48%, attributed to 2024). **[STRONG for e-commerce; transfer to donations is inference]**
- "Cover the fees" opt-in (vendor data): 40% of donors (FrontStream, June 2016–May 2017) — [FrontStream](https://www.frontstream.com/blog/donors-pay-the-fee-data); 51% on giving forms and 59% on social fundraising campaigns with no statistically significant effect on conversion (GiveCampus, Spring 2026) — [GiveCampus](https://support.givecampus.com/hc/en-us/articles/37844967602839-Donor-Covered-Payment-Processing-Fees); over 70% (Neon One) — [Neon One](https://neonone.com/resources/blog/donor-covered-fees/); almost two-thirds when pre-checked (Classy), one nonprofit's estimate of about one-third, and an A/B test showing no noticeable rise in abandonment; a Blackbaud executive notes the practice has not been quantified industry-wide — [NonProfit Times](https://thenonprofittimes.com/?p=57241); pre-selection mechanics — [Donately](https://support.donately.com/en/knowledge/pre-selecting-donor-pays-fee). **[PRACTITIONER]**
- GoFundMe 2025: 32 million donations to nonprofits (up 39% from 23 million in 2024), average donation $82 (from $77), fundraisers shared more than 72 million times (from 55 million); no mobile/desktop split published in the coverage found — [Fox/GoFundMe 2025 report coverage](https://www.fox7austin.com/news/gofundme-report-2025); the NonProfit Times headline reports Ireland as the most generous country on the platform — [NonProfit Times](https://thenonprofittimes.com/?p=215744). **[PRACTITIONER]**
- Reminders by SMS/email raise both likelihood and amount (see KQ1) — [Damgaard & Gravert working paper](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/afn/wp/14/wp14_03.pdf). **[STRONG]**

### Inferences
- For a phone-first school audience, Stripe Checkout with Apple Pay, Google Pay and Link enabled and the wallet button shown before the card form is the highest-leverage friction fix the data support; expect a relative lift in the 10–20% range, not a doubling.
- Require no account and no field beyond what Stripe needs; collect the leaderboard name/class optionally or after payment (forced account creation is a top-tier abandonment reason).
- Address the 19% "trust with card details" reason with Stripe's hosted page plus the charity's name and registered number and a one-line "where the money goes" beside the pay button.
- A "cover the fees" tick-box is probably net positive and does not appear to hurt conversion, but at €2 the fixed per-transaction fee is a large share of the payment; bundles already improve the fee ratio, which is a reason to nudge toward them.

### Gaps
- Baymard's figure for the average number of checkout form fields (recalled as roughly 11–12 versus an optimal 7–8) could not be verified this session.
- No independent (non-vendor) measurement of wallet-payment lift on donation forms specifically was found; Enthuse/JustGiving/Blackbaud UK-and-Ireland data on mobile and Apple Pay shares were not retrieved.
- No published controlled comparison of Stripe's hosted Checkout versus embedded/custom forms was found.

## KQ5. School and community fundraising: pricing plays and tickets, bundles, and leaderboards/competition for repeat payments

### Takeaway
Pricing and bundle advice for raffles and plays is almost entirely practitioner opinion (a round base price plus two bundle tiers that improve value per euro), while the experimental literature on recognition is solid: every form of public recognition tested increased giving, with the largest effects from naming only the top or only the bottom contributors and from recognition that many can attain; repeat-touch incentives habituate quickly (the sweets effect halved on second exposure), so novelty and fresh goals matter for repeat plays.

### Cited Findings
- Samek & Sheremeta (Economic Inquiry), framed field experiment: all recognition formats tested increased donations relative to baseline; publicly recognising only the top donors (prestige) or only the bottom donors (shame avoidance) had the largest effects — [IDEAS record](https://ideas.repec.org/p/feb/natura/00431.html); [Chapman ESI working paper](https://digitalcommons.chapman.edu/esi_working_papers/175); [MPRA version](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/68054/1/MPRA_paper_68054.pdf). **[MODERATE–STRONG]**
- Yale alumni "giving circles" field test (Dwight Hall; recalled attribution: Karlan & McConnell, "Hey look at me"): mentioning that donors above a threshold would be listed in a published newsletter significantly increased the probability of giving; the specific threshold did not significantly change amounts — [Yale EGC discussion paper PDF](https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/121670/files/cdp1006.pdf); [NBER w17737](https://www.nber.org/papers/w17737.pdf). **[MODERATE]**
- Lab evidence that the possibility of being recognised matters more than the exclusivity of the recognition, implying recognition works best when a large share of participants can earn it — [Bowdoin honours thesis](https://digitalcollections.bowdoin.edu/view/4983/giving-on-the-margin-the-power-of-donor-recognition). **[WEAK]**
- Peer anchoring on public pages: visible larger gifts raise later gifts (+£2.50 per £10 in the visible mean) and a single visible small gift lowers later gifts by about £5 — [Bristol CMPO abstract](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/cmpo/publications/papers/2013/abstract302.html). **[STRONG observational]**
- Habituation of incentives: in BIT's second year, giving among repeat sweets recipients fell by half while first-time recipients tripled — [University of Bristol](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2013/9770.html). **[MODERATE]**
- Raffle and ticket pricing (vendors): Givebutter suggests $1–$2 base entries with bundles that improve value per dollar as size rises (e.g., $5 for 6 entries, $10 for 15) and a "(goal + costs) ÷ expected tickets" floor — [Givebutter](https://givebutter.com/blog/how-to-price-raffle-tickets); RallyUp suggests a single ticket price plus two bundle tiers (e.g., 3 for $10, 10 for $25), argues a 3-for tier "turns a $5 buyer into a $10 buyer", and ties price bands to prize value ($1–$5 tickets for prizes under $500) — [RallyUp](https://rallyup.com/blog/how-to-price-raffle-tickets/); BetterWorld recommends a few clear choices to prevent decision fatigue, whole-dollar prices and early-bird discounts — [BetterWorld](https://betterworld.org/blog/giveaways/best-practices-pricing-raffle-tickets/); PayBee gives a $2-per-ticket worked example and an unsourced claim that 3-for-$5 bundles lift sales 30–50% — [PayBee](https://w.paybee.io/post/ultimate-guide-to-raffle-ticket-prices). **[PRACTITIONER; vendor-interested; no methodology]**
- UK PTA raffle guidance on incidental lotteries and ticket sales at events — [PTA UK](https://pta.co.uk/advice-hub/how-to-guides/running-a-raffle/). **[PRACTITIONER]**
- Small-amount asks: LPC meta-analyses show "even a small amount helps" raises compliance (r = .22) with totals similar or higher — [Arizona record](https://experts.arizona.edu/en/publications/the-legitimization-of-paltry-contributions-as-a-compliance-gainin/); [Buffalo record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/a-meta-analytic-review-of-the-legitimization-of-paltry-favors-com/). **[STRONG]**
- Sharing and the long tail: GoFundMe fundraisers were shared more than 72 million times in 2025 — [Fox coverage](https://www.fox7austin.com/news/gofundme-report-2025); JustGiving-derived charity guides claim about 20% of donations arrive after the event ends (claim appears across several guides; specific origin page not confirmed) — [Demelza JustGiving tips](https://www.demelza.org.uk/news/top-tips-for-using-your-justgiving-page). **[PRACTITIONER]**

### Inferences
- Leaderboard design: run several boards (top score per game, top class/house, most improved, most plays this week) so recognition is attainable by many; calling out classes at the bottom ("3rd Year needs you") is supported by the shame-avoidance result but should be done gently with children.
- Show totals by class or house rather than a feed of individual €2 purchases: team totals are large numbers (upward anchors) whereas an individual feed is dominated by €2 entries (downward anchors).
- Repeat purchases: weekly unlock targets and fresh prizes each week fit the goal-gradient and habituation evidence better than one long campaign with a single prize.
- Pricing: 3-for-€5 and 7-for-€10 match practitioner norms (round base price plus two bundle tiers); the defensible claim is that bundles raise average order value, not that they raise the share who pay.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study of leaderboards in pay-to-play charity games or school fundraising was found; the closest evidence is donor-recognition experiments and loyalty-programme research (Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng 2006 goal gradient; Nunes & Drèze 2006 endowed progress), neither retrieved this session.
- Charity livestream/gaming-marathon research on donation incentives and Lacetera, Macis & Mele's "Viral altruism?" Facebook study were not retrieved.
- No Irish school case studies with numbers on online play or raffle pricing were found; the only Ireland-specific signal is GoFundMe's "Ireland most generous" headline.
- The PayBee 30–50% bundle claim has no identifiable source.

## KQ6. Regulatory notes for Ireland and the UK: skill versus lottery, Gambling Regulation Act 2024 status, prudent disclosures (NOT LEGAL ADVICE)

### Takeaway
In Ireland the 1956 Act's definition of "lottery" expressly includes competitions involving guesses about future events, so a paid "guess the score" is a lottery needing a Garda permit (total prizes up to €5,000, tickets up to €10) or a District Court licence; a paid game of skill with prizes is "gaming" if played for stakes and sits in a grey zone; the Gambling Regulation Act 2024 is only partly commenced and the charitable/philanthropic licence was reported in August 2026 as not opening during 2026, so the old permit routes still apply. In Great Britain a paid competition escapes lottery rules only if skill prevents a significant proportion of entrants from entering or winning; otherwise it needs a genuine free entry route or a registered/incidental lottery. This section is general information, not legal advice.

### Cited Findings

#### Ireland
- Definitions: "'lottery' includes all competitions for money or money's worth involving guesses or estimates of future events or of past events the results of which are not yet ascertained or not yet generally known"; "gaming" means playing a game (whether of skill or chance or partly of skill and partly of chance) for stakes hazarded by the players; "stake" includes any payment for the right to take part in a game — [Gaming and Lotteries Act 1956, s.2 (revised)](https://revisedacts.lawreform.ie/eli/1956/act/2/section/2/revised/en/html); [full revised Act](https://revisedacts.lawreform.ie/eli/1956/act/2/revised/en/html). **[LAW]**
- Lotteries are generally prohibited (s.21) except under a permit (s.27) or licence (s.28) — [McCann FitzGerald, Gambling Law Review Irish chapter](https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/uploads/The-Gambling-Law-Review-2nd-ed-Irish-Chapter.pdf). **[LAW-COMMENTARY]**
- Gaming and Lotteries (Amendment) Act 2019 (lottery provisions in force from 1 December 2020): Garda Superintendent permit where total prize value does not exceed €5,000 (one source says per week), ticket price capped at €10, at least 60 days' notice, local area only; District Court licence for larger lotteries up to €30,000 in prizes per week or €360,000 for a once-a-year lottery; the bar on permit holders profiting personally was removed — [McCann FitzGerald](https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/knowledge/betting-and-gaming/the-gaming-and-lotteries-amendment-act-2019); [Hayes Solicitors](https://hayes-solicitors.ie/news/the-gaming-and-lotteries-amendment-act-2019-dont-leave-it-to-chance/); [Irish Legal News](https://www.irishlegal.com/articles/blog-new-rules-for-promotional-lotteries-what-does-this-mean-for-larger-value-prizes); [LK Shields](https://lkshields.ie/news-insights/publication/small-win-for-product-promotions-but-no-giveaways); [Mondaq](https://webiis06.mondaq.com/ireland/gaming/997588/changes-introduced-by-the-gaming-and-lotteries-amendment-act-2019). A raffle-platform guide instead says permit holders may not take personal profit and a permit is not granted to the same beneficiary more than once in six months — [Galabid Ireland raffle guide](https://galabid.com/raffle/ireland-raffle-rules-permits) — conflict unresolved. **[LAW-COMMENTARY]**
- Promotional/prize competitions: under the 1956 Act prize competitions often count as lotteries under the general ban; organisers avoid it with a genuine free-entry route or a genuine test of skill, and skill tie-breakers are common — [McCann FitzGerald Sales Promotions Q&A Ireland](https://mccannfitzgerald.com/uploads/Sales_promotions_QAndA_Ireland.pdf); Republic of Ireland rules remain stricter than Great Britain's — [Lewis Silkin (2022)](https://www.lewissilkin.com/insights/2022/05/16/no-purchase-necessary-route-for-chance-based-competitions-no-longer-required-in-northern-irelan). **[LAW-COMMENTARY]**
- Gambling Regulation Act 2024: signed 23 October 2024; first commencement order on 4 March 2025 mainly established the Gambling Regulatory Authority of Ireland (GRAI); the Act creates a separate licence for charitable and philanthropic purposes covering games and lotteries for fundraising; lottery licensing provisions were not yet commenced in the sources found — [Mason Hayes & Curran](https://www.mhc.ie/latest/insights/the-gambling-regulation-act-2024); [Matheson](https://www.matheson.com/insights/gambling-regulation-act-signed-into-law); [Arthur Cox (Aug 2025)](https://www.arthurcox.com/knowledge/whats-new-under-the-gambling-regulation-act-2024/); [McCann FitzGerald, GRAI becomes operational](https://www.mccannfitzgerald.com/knowledge/betting-and-gaming/and-theyre-off-the-grai-becomes-operational); [CMS expert guide, Ireland](https://cms.law/en/int/expert-guides/cms-expert-guide-to-online-gambling-regulation-in-europe/ireland). GRAI licensing guidance (July 2025): lottery permit and licence applications continue to be assessed by the District Courts and An Garda Síochána until new arrangements are in place; charity licence applications unlikely to be processed in 2025 — [GRAI guidance notice](https://www.drugsandalcohol.ie/43690); [Public Gaming summary](https://publicgaming.com/news-categories/regulatory-issues/14620-irish-gambling-regulator-publishes-licence-application-guidelines). August 2026 reporting: the charitable and philanthropic licence is not open and is not expected to open during 2026; Garda permit and District Court routes still apply — [Connaught Telegraph (20 Aug 2026)](https://www.con-telegraph.ie/2026/08/20/what-the-new-gambling-law-means-for-your-club-lottery/); [Galabid (Aug 2026)](https://galabid.com/raffle/ireland-raffle-rules-permits). One commentary says old-law licences run to 30 June 2026 with the new regime from 1 July 2026 — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/ireland/gaming/1667930/whats-new-under-the-gambling-regulation-act-2024) — not reconciled with the August 2026 reports. **[LAW-COMMENTARY; verify with GRAI]**
- A small charitable lottery exemption under the new regime was summarised as: no personal profit, total winnings not over €2,000, entry not more than €5, no more than 1,500 tickets, and no lottery in the previous three months — reported in secondary summaries ([Galabid](https://galabid.com/raffle/ireland-raffle-rules-permits); [Beauchamps](https://www.beauchamps.ie/publications/21843)); not verified against the Act text. **[UNVERIFIED]**
- Fundraising disclosures: the Charities Regulator's "Guidelines for Charitable Organisations on Fundraising from the Public" (2017, under the Charities Act 2009) require honesty (no misrepresentation of the charity, its need for funds, or how money will be used), communications donors can easily understand, and no implication that general donations are restricted; "must" marks a legal/regulatory requirement and "should" expected good practice; trustees must be able to justify departures — [Charities Regulator news item](https://www.charitiesregulator.ie/ga/faisnéis-don-phobal/ár-nuacht/2017/september/charity-regulator-s-fundraising-guidelines-to-protect-charities); [The Wheel summary](https://wheel.ie/funding/guidance/fundraising-essentials/guidelines-charities-fundraising); [Dóchas resource page](https://dochas.ie/resources/institutional-funding/guidelines-for-charitable-organisations-on-fundraising-from-the-public); [guidelines PDF copy](https://downsyndrome.ie/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/Charities-Regulator-Fundraising-Guidelines.pdf). **[REGULATOR GUIDANCE]**

#### United Kingdom (Great Britain unless stated)
- Prize competitions fall outside the Gambling Act 2005 only where success depends on skill, judgement or knowledge that prevents a significant proportion of entrants from entering or from winning; questions whose answers are widely known or obvious fail the test, and the more questions or the more obscure the subject, the more likely it is a genuine competition — [Gambling Commission free draws and prize competitions guidance (council-hosted copy)](https://www.orkney.gov.uk/media/cb3bec40/free_draws_and_prize_competitions_guidance.pdf); [Trafford Council summary](https://www.trafford.gov.uk/business-licences-and-consumers/licences-and-permits/entertainment-and-events/apply-licence/gambling-gaming-and-lotteries/lotteries-and-society-lottery-registration/prize-competitions-and-free-draws); [CMS legal update](https://cms.law/en/gbr/legal-updates/prize-competitions-and-free-prize-draws-new-guidance-1); "significant proportion" has never been precisely defined — [Osborne Clarke marketing law](https://marketinglaw.osborneclarke.com/marketing-techniques/gambling-commission-reveals-all-on-prize-promotions/). **[LAW/REGULATOR + COMMENTARY]**
- Free draws are not lotteries where there is no payment, or where a free route is as easy to use as the paid route, clearly publicised and gives the same chance of winning; the Commission does not license free draws or prize competitions but expects clear free-entry rules — [Lottery Daily on the 2022 Commission statement](https://lotterydaily.com/2022/01/28/news/ukgc-clarifies-position-on-free-draw-prize-competitions/); [Pinsent Masons guide](https://www.pinsentmasons.com/out-law/guides/running-a-competition); [Swan Turton](https://swanturton.com/official-guidance-on-free-draws-and-prize-competitions-the-gambling-act-2005/); [Lewis Silkin 2024](https://www.lewissilkin.com/en-ie/insights/2024/06/18/digitalcommerce-creative-101-running-prize-promotions-in-the-uk); [Sprintlaw overview](https://sprintlaw.co.uk/articles/uk-online-competitions-legal-rules-for-free-prize-draws-competitions/). Northern Ireland removed the no-purchase-route requirement for chance-based promotions in 2022, provided no payment is required to enter — [ASA](https://www.asa.org.uk/news/running-chance-based-prize-promotions-in-northern-ireland.html). **[LAW-COMMENTARY]**
- Raffles: an incidental non-commercial lottery (e.g., a school fete) needs no licence provided tickets are sold at the event, no more than £100 is deducted for expenses, no more than £500 is spent on prizes (donated prizes extra), and there are no rollovers; larger raffles run as small society lotteries registered with the local authority — [Gambling Commission "Running a lottery" (council-hosted copy)](https://www.dumfriesandgalloway.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2025-04/Gambling_Commission_-_Running_a_Lottery.pdf); [Gambling Commission quick guide (Stroud copy)](https://stroud.gov.uk/media/gg3ojaya/running-a-lottery-quick-guide-gambling-commission.pdf); [NE Lincolnshire lotteries advice](https://www.nelincs.gov.uk/assets/uploads/2021/10/Lotteries-advice-for-promoters.pdf); [PTA UK](https://pta.co.uk/advice-hub/how-to-guides/running-a-raffle/). **[REGULATOR GUIDANCE]**

### Inferences
- A paid "guess the score" is squarely a lottery in Ireland: either obtain a Garda lottery permit (with its caps) or run it as a genuinely free side-game with no purchase required, and take advice first.
- A €2 arcade play where prizes go to top scores is a game of skill, but Irish "gaming" covers skill games played for stakes, and "stake" includes any payment to take part. The prudent structure is: frame the €2 as a donation to the charity, have prizes donated by sponsors rather than funded from play fees, publish the prize rules and closing date, provide a free play route for the prize competition, and get a short legal check (GRAI and the Charities Regulator are the contacts). This is not legal advice.
- Prudent on-site disclosures that align with the Charities Regulator's "must/should" guidance and the UK trust drivers: charity name and registered charity number, who operates the site, exactly where the money goes (including whether Stripe fees are deducted or covered), prize terms and draw date, age guidance (students pay via parents), a privacy note, and a contact.
- If the site is ever opened to players in Great Britain, run the prize element as a free draw or a genuine skill competition and do not sell raffle entries online without a society-lottery registration.

- Whether a charity pay-to-play skill game is "gaming" requiring a Garda gaming permit under the 1956 Act as amended in 2019 was not confirmed from primary text; no Irish case law on charity skill games was found.
- Age limits for online lottery/gaming participation in Ireland (recalled: 18 under the 2019 Act) could not be verified this session.
