## Key question 1: Which mechanics have the best evidence for increasing replays and retention in casual games, and what are typical effect sizes and benchmark numbers (D1/D7/D30, sessions per day)?

### Takeaway
The best-evidenced retention levers are (a) visible progress toward a goal (goal-gradient and endowed-progress effects, STRONG), (b) intact streaks shown in a log (STRONG consumer-research evidence, supported by Duolingo's correlational data), (c) immediate, low-latency feedback and instant restart (STRONG for latency perception; PRACTITIONER for "one more go"), and (d) difficulty that errs on the easy side when the game assigns it (MODERATE, large online experiments). Near-miss and variable-reward effects are far more contested than game-design folklore suggests, and leaderboards help some players while demotivating others. Current mobile benchmarks: top-quartile games retain ~27% of installs at Day 1 and ~7–8% at Day 7; three-quarters of games keep under 3% at Day 28. Historically, "timing" games were the best-retaining hyper-casual sub-genre at Day 1 (~44% for top performers).

### Cited Findings

For the least successful 25% of projects, D1 Retention is at 10-11.5%. On iOS, the top 25% of projects have a higher average D1 Retention - 31-33% compared to 25-27% on Android." — [GameAnalytics 2025 Mobile Gaming Benchmarks (PDF via InvestGame)](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2025-GameAnalytics-Mobile-Gaming-Benchmarks.pdf) (verified); summarised at [GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-gaming-benchmarks) (verified).
- Same report: "The median D7 Retention across all projects ranges from 3.42% to 3.94%... lower than the 2023 results (4-5%). For the top 25% of projects, this indicator is at 7-8%, while for the weakest 25% of projects, it barely reaches 1.5%." and "75% of projects have a D28 Retention lower than 3%." Median playtime in 2024 was about 22 minutes; average session length was ~5 minutes in Africa/Asia and 6.85 minutes in Oceania (longest). — [GameAnalytics 2025 benchmarks](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2025-GameAnalytics-Mobile-Gaming-Benchmarks.pdf) (verified). Caveat: the report uses "classic" rather than rolling retention, and is all-genre, not casual-only.
- GameAnalytics 2019 benchmarks: industry-average D1 ≈ 25%, D7 just under 6%; the top 25% of games averaged D28 ≈ 4%; "only the best games achieve a Day 1 retention of 35% and Day 7 retention of 11% or higher." — [App Developer Magazine summary](https://appdevelopermagazine.com/mobile-gaming-industry-benchmarks-offer-fresh-look-on-industry/) (verified); [GameRefinery summary of the H1 2019 report](https://www.gamerefinery.com/5-key-takeaways-from-gameanalytics-h1-2019-mobile-benchmarks-report/) (verified).
- GameAnalytics' hyper-casual guidance: a hyper-casual game "with a day 1 retention rate lower than 40% probably isn't doing well"; in its 2020 hyper-casual sub-genre breakdown of top performers, **timing games had the highest Day 1 retention at 44%**, with other sub-genres around 40%; at Day 7, shooting led at 17% and the rest sat just below 15%. — [GameAnalytics, "The metrics behind hyper-casual games"](https://www.gameanalytics.com/blog/the-metrics-behind-hyper-casual-games-industry-report) (verified). Note the thresholds (35% vs 40%) differ between GameAnalytics documents.
- Tenjin/GameAnalytics publish quarterly hyper-casual benchmarks (D1/D7 for top 2%, top 25%, median by platform); figures were not in the retrieved snippets. — [Tenjin Hyper-Casual Benchmark Report Q4 2022](https://resources.tenjin.com/2022-benchmark-q4) (verified link only).

**Progress and goal mechanics (STRONG)**
- Goal-gradient: customers holding coffee reward cards accelerated purchases as they neared the free coffee, and "illusionary" progress (a 12-stamp card with 2 pre-stamped) was completed faster than a plain 10-stamp card; effort dipped after the reward ("post-reward resetting"). — Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng (2006), *Journal of Marketing Research* 43(1), [doi:10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39](https://doi.org/10.1509/jmkr.43.1.39) (URL from memory). Classic.
- Endowed progress: car-wash customers given a 10-stamp card with 2 "free" stamps completed it at 34% vs 19% for an 8-stamp card with none, and completed it faster. — Nunes & Drèze (2006), *Journal of Consumer Research* 32(4), [doi:10.1086/500480](https://doi.org/10.1086/500480) (URL from memory). Classic.

**Streaks (STRONG academic + WEAK/MODERATE company data)**
- Across seven studies, "intact streaks highlighted via behavioral logs increase consumers' subsequent engagement in that behavior, relative to when broken streaks are highlighted"; the effect "comes from the log's representation, because consumers consider maintaining a logged streak to be a meaningful goal in and of itself"; it is amplified when the break is self-attributed and attenuated when the streak can be "repaired". — Silverman & Barasch (2023), *Journal of Consumer Research* 49(6), [University of Delaware repository](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb/full) (verified).
- Duolingo's own blog states that learners who reach a 7-day streak are "3.6 times more likely to complete their course" (correlational; no methodology given) and that a streak-milestone animation raised the odds that a brand-new learner was still using the app seven days later by +1.7% ("modest but meaningful across millions of users"). — [Duolingo blog, "How the Duolingo streak builds habit"](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit) (verified via search summary; page itself could not be opened).
- Jorge Mazal (former CPO) writes that leaderboards, push-notification and streak changes, with other product/marketing efforts, "helped grow DAU by 4.5x over four years"; by mid-2018 DAU had been growing at a single-digit annual rate and the team prioritised retention because all acquisition was organic. Per-feature percentages are in the essay but were not retrievable here. — [Lenny's Newsletter, "How Duolingo reignited user growth"](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth) (verified link; contents partially verified).

**Difficulty and challenge (MODERATE: large online experiments)**
- Three experiments over 20,000+ play sessions of an online educational game: "Moderately difficult levels were most motivating when self-selected; yet, when difficulty was blindly assigned, the easiest games were most motivating." Moderate novelty was optimal; suspense in "close games" was beneficial. The paper notes "recent experiments have shown that even moderate difficulty can reduce player engagement" (referring to the same group's 2013 test of the inverted-U hypothesis). — Lomas et al. (2017), CHI 2017, [doi:10.1145/3025453.3025638](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025638) (verified); [CMU HCII preview](https://hcii.cmu.edu/news/chi-preview-researchers-hcii-believe-difficulty-can-be-overrated-games) (verified); Lomas et al. (2013) "Optimizing challenge in an educational game using large-scale design experiments", CHI 2013 ([PDF mirror](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/463-663/project-part-one/Optimizing%20Challenge.pdf), verified link only).

**Gamification in general (STRONG that effects exist; MODERATE on size)**
- Meta-analysis of gamification in learning: small-to-medium effects on cognitive (g ≈ 0.49), motivational (g ≈ 0.36) and behavioural (g ≈ 0.25) outcomes, with high heterogeneity. — Sailer & Homner (2020), *Educational Psychology Review*, [doi:10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w) (URL from memory).
- Early review: gamification "does work" but effects depend heavily on context and users; many studies are short-term and methodologically weak. — Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa (2014), HICSS, [doi:10.1109/HICSS.2014.377](https://doi.org/10.1109/HICSS.2014.377) (URL from memory).

**Leaderboards (MIXED: MODERATE evidence of both benefit and harm)**
- In a brainstorming task "the mere presence of a leaderboard motivated participants to perform at similar levels to participants in difficult and impossible goal-setting groups", interpreted as players setting a goal to be at or near the top. — Landers, Bauer & Callan (2017), *Computers in Human Behavior* 71, [doi:10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.008](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.chb.2015.08.008) (finding verified via search snippet; attribution/URL from memory).
- A semester-long comparison of two sections of one course: students in the gamified section (leaderboard + badges) "showed less motivation, satisfaction, and empowerment over time" and had lower final-exam scores; ~80 participants. — Hanus & Fox (2015), *Computers & Education* 80, [doi:10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compedu.2014.08.019) (finding verified via search; [Hanus profile](https://osu.academia.edu/MichaelHanus) verified).

**Self-determination theory (MODERATE: large correlational samples; theory widely used but often shallowly)**
- Four studies found that in-game satisfaction of competence and autonomy predicted enjoyment, game ratings and preference for future play, and that relatedness mattered in multiplayer. — Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski (2006), *Motivation and Emotion* 30, [doi:10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8) (URL from memory). Classic.
- Review of SDT in HCI games research found it is mostly used as a measurement instrument (PENS/needs scales) rather than tested as a theory, and calls for more rigorous use. — Tyack & Mekler (2020), CHI 2020, [doi:10.1145/3313831.3376723](https://doi.org/10.1145/3313831.3376723) (URL from memory).

**Habit formation (MODERATE)**
- In 96 volunteers, automaticity of a new daily behaviour plateaued after a median of 66 days (range 18–254); missing a single day did not materially derail habit formation. — Lally et al. (2010), *European Journal of Social Psychology* 40(6), [doi:10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) (URL from memory).

**Variable-ratio reward (PRACTITIONER + classic operant psychology; WEAK direct evidence in casual games)**
- The canonical designer text applying Skinnerian schedules (fixed/variable ratio and interval) to game pacing. — Hopson (2001), "Behavioral Game Design", Gamasutra, [gamedeveloper.com](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/behavioral-game-design) (URL from memory). Classic, practitioner.
- The "Hooked" model (trigger → action → variable reward → investment) is a practitioner framework without controlled evidence. — Eyal (2014), *Hooked*, [nirandfar.com](https://www.nirandfar.com/hooked/) (URL from memory). PRACTITIONER.

### Inferences
- For PresLink, replay is a purchase (€2), so the honest retention goal is "return visits and willing re-plays", not maximised session length. The mechanics with the strongest evidence are also the least ethically fraught: show progress toward a visible goal (next rank, personal best, campaign milestone), log and display days-played streaks for free activity, and make feedback instant.
- Benchmarks are for free-to-play installs; a paid-play charity site with a school community will behave differently (strong social pull, short campaign window). Use the numbers only to set expectations: if D7 of players who made a first play is above ~10%, PresLink is already in top-quartile territory by mobile standards.
- The "timing" sub-genre's historically high D1 (44% among top performers) suggests the stop-the-light core loop is inherently retentive when execution is good; retention then hinges on fairness and feedback, not on adding systems.
- Lomas' results argue against making the light so fast that most players average well under their aspiration level; when players cannot choose difficulty, easier-feeling versions kept more players. A speed that lets a typical first-timer land 600–800/1,000 with visible room to improve fits the evidence better than a brutal default.

### Gaps
- No benchmark for **sessions per day** or for replay rate within a session was found for casual/hyper-casual games in the retrieved results; the 2019 and 2025 GameAnalytics reports contain session-count data that could not be opened.
- Per-feature results in Mazal's Duolingo essay (leaderboards, streak-saver notifications, streak freeze) and Duolingo investor-letter figures on streaks could not be retrieved; the "+0.38% DAU from allowing two streak freezes" figure appears only on secondary sites and is unverified.
- No benchmark exists for paid-per-play web arcade games; the closest analogues (redemption arcade machines) publish no retention data.
- The full results of Lomas et al. 2013 (the shape of the engagement–difficulty curve, with numbers) could not be read.

## Key question 2: What does research say about near-miss effects in skill/timing games, and when do they motivate versus frustrate?

### Takeaway
Near-misses reliably raise arousal, frustration and the momentary urge to continue (MODERATE), but the claim that they increase actual persistence is not robust: a 2019 review plus three new experiments found no replicable near-miss effect on continued play. The one fMRI study that found increased motivation found it only when players felt personal control, and its lead author explicitly notes that in genuine skill tasks a near-miss is informative feedback. For a skill game, the design implication is to make near-misses truthful and actionable (show exactly how far off and in which direction) rather than engineered, and never to pair them with a paid "try again" nudge aimed at teenagers.

### Cited Findings
- fMRI study (15 non-gambler volunteers): near-misses "were less pleasant than full misses but raised the desire to keep playing, and only when players had personal control over arranging the gamble"; anterior cingulate activity varied with personal control. — Clark, Lawrence, Astley-Jones & Gray (2009), *Neuron* 61(3), [PMC2658737](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2658737/) (verified). MODERATE (small N).
- Clark, in press coverage, distinguished skill from chance: "for some tasks, such as learning to kick a soccer ball into a goal or firing an arrow at a target, near-misses are informative. But in gambling, almost winning has no effect on the next pull of the lever." — [ScienceDaily, 2009](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2009/02/090211122130.htm) (verified).
- Review and three experiments (pigeons and humans, resistance-to-extinction designs): results "failed to support the near-miss effect hypothesis"; the authors "found problems and inconsistencies in the previously cited evidence"; near-miss events affect brain activity and other measures "but there seems to be no conclusive, replicable evidence that they encourage gambling persistence." — Pisklak, Yong & Spetch (2019/2020), *Journal of Gambling Studies*, [PMC7214505](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7214505) (verified); [University of Alberta news](https://www.ualberta.ca/en/science/news/2019/september/gambling-behaviour-near-misses.html) (verified). STRONG for "effect on persistence is unproven".
- Candy Crush field-lab study: 60 avid players played 30 minutes; "Near-misses were more arousing than losses as indexed by increased heart rate and greater subjective arousal. Near-misses were also subjectively rated as the most frustrating of all outcomes. Most importantly, of any type of outcome, near-misses triggered the most substantial urge to continue play"; the authors suggest near-misses "may contribute to players playing longer than intended." — Larche, Musielak & Dixon (2017), *Journal of Gambling Studies* 33(2), [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10899-016-9633-7) (verified); [Waterloo thesis version](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/handle/10012/12522) (verified). MODERATE.
- Classic slot-simulator experiment: a ~30% near-miss density produced the greatest persistence in extinction compared with 15%, 45% and 0%. — Kassinove & Schare (2001), *Psychology of Addictive Behaviors* 15(2), [doi:10.1037/0893-164X.15.2.155](https://doi.org/10.1037/0893-164X.15.2.155) (URL from memory). WEAK–MODERATE; part of the literature Pisklak et al. found inconsistent.
- Theoretical origin: Reid (1986) proposed that near-misses persist because in skill domains "nearly" succeeding signals skill acquisition, which gambling machines exploit. — Reid (1986), *Journal of Gambling Behavior* 2, [doi:10.1007/BF01019932](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01019932) (URL from memory). Classic.
- Celebration can masquerade as success: in multi-line slots, "losses disguised as wins" (payouts below the stake, with win sounds/animations) produced skin-conductance responses as large as real wins in 40 novices, both larger than for plain losses. — Dixon et al. (2010), *Addiction* 105(10), [Waterloo PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/reasoning-decision-making-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/DixFugetal_10c.pdf) (verified). MODERATE. (Relevant because a "so close!" fanfare on a poor light is the same pattern.)

### Inferences
- In PresLink's game a near-miss is real information (the tap was 15 ms early), so the ethically clean and evidence-consistent presentation is **diagnostic**: show the offset direction and size, the points lost, and (optionally) a tip ("you tend to tap early; wait a beat"). This leverages the "informative near-miss" that Clark describes and the competence need in SDT without manufacturing false hope.
- Near-misses motivate when (1) the player attributes the outcome to a controllable action, (2) a retry is cheap and immediate, and (3) the feedback suggests a concrete adjustment. They frustrate when retries cost money, when the player suspects the game is rigged, or when "so close" is engineered (e.g., a light that slows near the target or a scoring curve that inflates near-misses). With a €2 retry price, the Candy Crush "urge to continue" becomes a spending prompt; the ethics sections below treat that as a line not to cross for under-18s.
- Because the persistence effect is unproven, there is no business case for engineering near-misses; the downside (frustration, perceived rigging, regulatory risk) is better evidenced than the upside.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests near-miss feedback specifically in reaction/timing games (as opposed to slots or match-3 puzzles); the inference about diagnostic feedback rests on Clark's comment and general feedback-learning principles, not direct experiments.
- No evidence was found on near-miss effects when each retry costs money in a non-gambling, skill-based context.

## Key question 3: How should a "stop the moving light on the mark" timing game present its three-round structure, averages, personal bests and rank changes to maximise the urge to play again?

### Takeaway
The evidence points to: instant per-light verdicts (sub-100 ms), a visible 1-of-3 → 3-of-3 progress structure that creates suspense around the running average and the personal best ("you need 870 on light 3 to beat your best"), end-of-play summaries that lead with the best thing that happened (peak-end), scores on an expanded scale (out of 1,000 rather than 100) so improvements look larger (unit effect), salient reference points (PB, next rank, round-number "clubs"), and relative rather than global leaderboard framing to limit the demotivation that leaderboards cause for lower-ranked players.

### Cited Findings
- Unit effect: "Attribute differences appear larger on scales with a higher number of units" (e.g., a 7- vs 9-year warranty is judged a smaller gap than 84 vs 108 months); reminding people of alternative units eliminates the effect. — Pandelaere, Briers & Lembregts (2011), *Journal of Consumer Research*, [RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-659000.html) (verified); [ScienceDaily summary](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2011/02/110214163114.htm) (verified); game-design commentary at [Psychology of Games, "The Unit Effect and Player Perceptions"](https://psychologyofgames.com/2011/03/the-unit-effect-and-player-perceptions) (verified). STRONG (replicated; see Cadario et al. 2016 replication noted in the same search).
- Numerosity heuristic: people infer quantity from the number of units presented. — Pelham, Sumarta & Myaskovsky (1994), *Cognitive Psychology* 26(2), [doi:10.1006/cogp.1994.1004](https://doi.org/10.1006/cogp.1994.1004) (URL from memory). Classic.
- Suspense: in Lomas et al. (2017), suspense "was studied in 'close games,' where it was found to be beneficial" to intrinsic motivation. — [CHI 2017, doi:10.1145/3025453.3025638](https://doi.org/10.1145/3025453.3025638) (verified). MODERATE.
- Peak-end rule: retrospective evaluations of an episode are dominated by its most intense moment and its ending, not its duration. — Kahneman, Fredrickson, Schreiber & Redelmeier (1993), *Psychological Science* 4(6), [doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.1993.tb00589.x) (URL from memory). Classic, STRONG.
- Reference points: marathon finishing times bunch just under round numbers (e.g., 3:59 vs 4:01), showing people exert extra effort to beat salient round-number goals. — Allen, Dechow, Pope & Wu (2017), *Management Science* 63(6), [doi:10.1287/mnsc.2015.2417](https://doi.org/10.1287/mnsc.2015.2417) (URL from memory). STRONG (very large field dataset).
- Goal-gradient and endowed progress (see KQ1): effort accelerates with proximity to a goal and with any head-start — Kivetz et al. 2006; Nunes & Drèze 2006 (URLs from memory). STRONG.
- Leaderboards act like difficult/impossible goals for those who chase the top (Landers et al. 2017) but reduced motivation and satisfaction over a semester in a classroom (Hanus & Fox 2015) — see KQ1 citations (verified/URL from memory). MIXED.
- Streak/log representation drives continuation; "repairable" breaks attenuate the drop-off; Barasch advises that after a break, messages highlighting the failure "typically doesn't work" and suggests offering a new goal instead. — Silverman & Barasch (2023), [UDel repository](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb/full) (verified); [CU Boulder news](https://www.colorado.edu/business/news/2023/04/20/research-streaks-marketing-tech-barasch) (verified). STRONG.
- Celebration animation measurably moved retention: Duolingo's streak-milestone animation, +1.7% odds of D7 activity for new learners. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit) (verified via search summary). WEAK–MODERATE (company A/B, no method detail).
- Feedback timing thresholds for touch buttons (see KQ7): perceived quality drops once feedback lags more than ~70–100 ms (tactile/audio) or ~100–150 ms (visual). — Kaaresoja, Brewster & Lantz (2014), *ACM Transactions on Applied Perception*, [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/104653) (verified). STRONG for the specific question.
- Zeigarnik effect (unfinished tasks stay mentally active) is a classic from 1927 whose replications are mixed; a modern variant found unfulfilled goals cause intrusive thoughts and impair performance until a concrete plan is made. — Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), *JPSP* 101(4), [doi:10.1037/a0024192](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192) (URL from memory). WEAK for the original effect; MODERATE for the plan-making version.

### Inferences
A presentation design consistent with the evidence above (each item maps to a finding):

1. **Per-light verdict in under 100 ms** (Kaaresoja): the light freezes where the tap landed; the target band stays visible; an offset readout ("−14 ms, early") and a points number appear in the same frame. Sound/haptic (if available) fire within ~20–50 ms.
2. **Three pips, one running average** (goal-gradient, suspense in close games): after light 1 show "Light 1: 812 · Average so far 812"; after light 2 show the average and the exact number needed on light 3 to beat the personal best or to reach the next rank ("Need 905 to beat your best 846"). This makes the third light a "close game" when it genuinely is one, and makes the averaging rule transparent.
3. **Lead the end screen with the peak** (peak-end): if the average is poor but one light was excellent, the first line is "Best light: 968 — your highest ever single light", then the average. Never celebrate a poor average as if it were good (losses-disguised-as-wins).
4. **Count-up and big numbers** (unit effect, numerosity): keep the 0–1,000 scale (do not present as a percentage or a 0–10 rating), animate the count-up briefly (≤ 1 s), and express improvement as points ("+37 on your best") rather than as a fraction.
5. **Reference points** (round-number goals, PB): show PB and the next round-number "club" (800, 900, 950) with distance to each; show rank movement as "Up 3 places to 14th · 11 points behind 13th". Show distance to the next place, not to first place, unless the player is near the top (goal-gradient works only when the goal is reachable).
6. **Relative leaderboard** (Hanus & Fox; Christy & Fox on social comparison): default view is the player's class/school/year group and the five places either side; global position is secondary. Offer "Most improved" and "Best single light" boards so late or weaker players have a reachable goal.
7. **Broken-streak and bad-play framing** (Silverman & Barasch): never say "You lost your streak"; say "New week, new goal" and offer a fresh target. For a bad play, offer a diagnostic ("both misses were early") and a free practice light rather than "Play again for €2".
8. **Do not add a between-lights timer or countdown pressure**; the suspense comes from the score maths, not from urgency (ICO advises against pressure/FOMO devices for children, see KQ6).

### Gaps
- No experiment was found comparing "average of three" against "best of three" or "sum of three" on replay or perceived fairness; the choice to average is a variance-reduction decision (leaderboard integrity), and its effect on motivation is untested.
- No controlled evidence was found on animated count-ups, odometer effects or rank-change animations in games; this is PRACTITIONER territory.
- No study was found on relative (nearby-rank) versus global leaderboards in casual games; the recommendation is an inference from social-comparison findings.

## Key question 4: What is known about streaks, daily limits and scarcity (e.g., limited plays) increasing value and return visits?

### Takeaway
Streaks have the best evidence of any return-visit mechanic (STRONG lab/field studies plus company data), but their power comes from loss-framing, which is exactly what EU child-protection guidance now says should be off by default for minors. Deliberate scarcity (Wordle's one puzzle a day) is well-documented as a design choice with a happy, non-pushy user relationship, but its causal effect on retention is not measured in any source found. Habit research suggests return visits become automatic only after roughly two months of consistent cues, and that a missed day does not undo progress.

### Cited Findings
- Intact logged streaks increase subsequent engagement; broken ones cause drop-off; self-attributed breaks hurt most; repairability attenuates (the mechanism behind Duolingo's "streak freeze"). — Silverman & Barasch (2023), [UDel repository](https://udspace.udel.edu/items/42ce576b-8e1f-429a-8541-e29e48dbcfbb/full) (verified). STRONG.
- Duolingo: 7-day streak holders "3.6 times more likely to complete their course" (correlational); milestone animation +1.7% D7 odds (A/B). — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit) (verified via search summary). WEAK (3.6x: correlational) / MODERATE (1.7%: experiment, but no method detail).
- Wordle: Josh Wardle told the New York Times the game "encourages you to spend three minutes a day... And that's it"; while other games send notifications hoping you'll come back throughout the day, Wordle "doesn't want an intense relationship"; one puzzle is released daily and every player gets the same word. — [NYT via Boston.com](https://www.boston.com/news/national-news/2022/01/04/he-made-wordle-for-his-partner-now-its-an-online-hit/) (verified); [NME](https://www.nme.com/?p=3129495) (verified); [Wikipedia summary of design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wordle) (verified). PRACTITIONER (design rationale); no retention data published.
- Habit automaticity plateaus after a median 66 days (18–254); one missed day does not materially derail it. — Lally et al. (2010), [doi:10.1002/ejsp.674](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674) (URL from memory). MODERATE.
- Habits are cue-driven responses formed by repetition in stable contexts; interventions work best by shaping context cues and friction rather than by persuasion. — Wood & Rünger (2016), *Annual Review of Psychology* 67, [doi:10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417](https://doi.org/10.1146/annurev-psych-122414-033417) (URL from memory). STRONG (review).
- Scarcity: a meta-analysis of scarcity tactics found an overall positive effect on purchase intentions, moderated by the type of scarcity cue. — Barton, Zlatevska & Oppewal (2022), *Journal of Retailing*, [doi:10.1016/j.jretai.2022.06.003](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jretai.2022.06.003) (URL from memory; exact moderators not recalled with confidence). MODERATE.

- EU: the Commission's July 2025 DSA guidelines recommend "disabling by default features that contribute to excessive use, like communication 'streaks,' ephemeral content, 'read receipts,' autoplay, or push notifications, as well as removing persuasive design features aimed predominantly at engagement", because minors "are particularly vulnerable to practices that can stimulate addictive behaviour". — [European Commission, Guidelines on the protection of minors (14 July 2025)](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/library/commission-publishes-guidelines-protection-minors) (verified); [Taylor Wessing analysis](https://www.taylorwessing.com/en/insights-and-events/insights/2025/07/rd-european-commission-guidelines-on-protection-of-minors-under-the-digital-services-act) (verified). Regulatory guidance (non-binding benchmark; applies to online platforms except small/micro enterprises).

Regulatory guidance.

### Inferences
- A paid-play streak ("play every day to keep your streak") is a daily spending prompt aimed at teenagers and conflicts with both the EU minors guidelines and the ICO's FOMO guidance. If PresLink uses streaks at all, tie them to **free** actions (a daily free practice light, visiting the leaderboard, a daily "lights challenge" with a shared seed), make them repairable, and keep them off by default or opt-in for under-18s.
- A Wordle-style **daily cap** on paid plays (e.g., at most 2–3 plays per person per day) is likely to *raise* perceived value and reduce regret, create a natural "come back tomorrow" rhythm without loss-framing, and double as a consumer-protection control. It is also the single clearest answer to the "manipulative" critique: the site visibly refuses to let anyone chase.
- Campaign-length matters: with ~66 days to automaticity, a 4–8 week campaign will not create durable habits; design for *social* return cues (class challenges, teacher announcements, weekly reset of a "this week" board) rather than individual habit loops.
- "Limited plays" as scarcity has a plausible value effect (MODERATE in purchasing contexts) but no game-retention evidence; treat it as a fairness and wellbeing feature first and a value signal second.

### Gaps
- No empirical measurement of Wordle's retention or of the effect of its one-a-day limit was found; only the designer's stated intent.
- No study was found that tests daily play caps in games on return visits or spend.
- Duolingo's streak-freeze causal numbers and investor-letter streak statistics could not be verified.

## Key question 5: What does the evidence say about celebration (confetti, sounds, haptics) and perceived quality, and about overdoing it?

### Takeaway
"Juice" is strongly held practitioner doctrine with thin but growing academic support: controlled studies now exist on visual embellishments (2019), audio embellishments (2024) and vibrotactile embellishments (reported as improving player experience), and feedback-latency research shows perceived quality collapses when feedback lags by more than ~100 ms. The clearest evidence on overdoing it comes from gambling research: celebratory sounds and animations on outcomes that were actually losses raise arousal and make people overestimate how often they won, which is both ineffective as honest feedback and a textbook dark pattern.

### Cited Findings
- Juiciness defined and tested: "visual embellishments (VEs) are design elements that support information already conveyed by other means. In games, this concept is known as juiciness, and refers to the provision of redundant feedback in situations where a single player action triggers multiple non-functional reactions"; the authors note "empirical evidence to back the assumption is lacking" and ran a study to test it. — Hicks, Dickinson, Holopainen & Gerling (2019), CHI PLAY 2019, [doi:10.1145/3311350.3347171](https://doi.org/10.1145/3311350.3347171) (abstract verified; **results not accessible in this session**).
- Follow-up: a CHI PLAY 2024 paper found "principles of juicy design also apply to audio and sound design". — Hicks et al. (2024), [Staffordshire eprint PDF](https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/8527/1/3677084.pdf) (verified existence; results not read). MODERATE (single study).
- Haptics: a University of Waterloo Haptic Experience Lab study is titled "Juicy Haptic Design: Vibrotactile Embellishments Can Improve Player Experience in Games". — [Waterloo project page](https://uwaterloo.ca/haptic-experience-lab/projects/juicy-haptic-design-vibrotactile-embellishments-can-improve) (verified existence; results not read). MODERATE (single study).
- Latency and perceived quality of virtual buttons (0–300 ms delays, tactile/audio/visual): points of subjective simultaneity were 5 ms (tactile), 19 ms (audio), 32 ms (visual); perceived quality fell noticeably between 70–100 ms for tactile/audio and 100–150 ms for visual; 300 ms buttons were rated lowest. Earlier keypad work found a constant 18 ms tactile delay with minimal jitter was fastest with fewest errors, and satisfaction dropped when delay variation exceeded ~72 ms; longer tactile delays made buttons feel "heavier". — Kaaresoja, Brewster & Lantz (2014), [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/104653) (verified); [Kaaresoja PhD thesis (2016)](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/7075) (verified); 2011 keypad study via [Springer Professional](https://www.springerprofessional.de/playing-with-tactile-feedback-latency-in-touchscreen-interaction/3732310) (verified). STRONG for this specific question.
- Overdoing it / dishonest celebration: slot "losses disguised as wins" (celebrated sub-stake payouts) produced skin-conductance responses as large as real wins (40 novices). — Dixon et al. (2010), [Waterloo PDF](https://uwaterloo.ca/reasoning-decision-making-lab/sites/default/files/uploads/files/DixFugetal_10c.pdf) (verified). With sound on, 96 gamblers overestimated their number of wins by 24% versus 15% with sound off, and skin-conductance responses were higher with sound. — Dixon et al. (2013/2014), *Journal of Gambling Studies*, [University of Waterloo news](https://uwaterloo.ca/news/news/music-gamblers-ears) (verified); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/07/130702100348.htm) (verified). MODERATE–STRONG.
- Company evidence that a celebration animation moves behaviour: Duolingo's streak-milestone animation, +1.7% odds of D7 activity among new learners. — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit) (verified via search summary). WEAK–MODERATE.
- Practitioner canon: "Juice it or lose it" demonstrates layering tweens, particles, screen shake and sound on a Breakout clone to transform feel. — Jonasson & Purho (2012), talk video [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Fy0aCDmgnxg) (URL from memory). PRACTITIONER. Steve Swink's *Game Feel* (2008) frames feel as real-time control + simulated space + polish, with response perceived as instantaneous. — [game-feel.com](https://www.game-feel.com/) (URL from memory). PRACTITIONER classic. Jan Willem Nijman's "The Art of Screenshake" (2013) catalogues hit-stop, screen shake, bigger impacts and sound as cheap feel multipliers. — [YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=AJdEqssNZ-U) (URL from memory). PRACTITIONER.
- Sound and psychophysiology: sound (and music) in a first-person shooter changed arousal and self-reported experience measurably. — Nacke, Grimshaw & Lindley (2010), *Interacting with Computers* 22(5), [doi:10.1016/j.intcom.2010.04.005](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intcom.2010.04.005) (URL from memory). MODERATE.
- A preliminary study titled "Good Feedback for Bad Players? A preliminary Study of 'juicy' Interface Feedback" (Juul & Begy, FDG/DiGRA 2016) exists; its findings could not be verified in this session. — no verified URL. WEAK (unread).

### Inferences
- Do juice the moment of the tap: freeze-frame, a short hit-stop (~50–80 ms), a flash on the target band, a rising tick sound and (where supported) a haptic tap. Keep every element within the ~100 ms window; a celebration that arrives after the number feels disconnected (Kaaresoja).
- Scale celebration to achievement, not to spend: a new PB or a 950+ light earns confetti; an average play earns a clean, quiet summary. Celebrating a 600 average with fanfare is the LDW pattern and will erode trust with sharp-eyed teenagers.
- Keep celebrations short (≤ 1.5 s), skippable by tap, and never blocking the "what next" information; peak-end means the *last* thing on screen should be the most positive truthful fact.
- Haptics on the mobile web are unreliable: the Vibration API is not supported in iOS Safari (URL from memory, [MDN Vibration API](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Vibration_API)), so design the feel to work with visuals + audio alone, treating vibration as a bonus on Android.
- Sound must be opt-in or at least mutable, and because iOS unlocks audio only after a user gesture, preload and unlock the audio context on the first tap (PRACTITIONER).

### Gaps
- The actual results of Hicks et al. 2019 (whether more juiciness monotonically improves experience or plateaus/backfires), and of the 2024 audio and Waterloo haptic studies, could not be read; the "overdoing it" question therefore rests on the gambling LDW literature plus practitioner opinion.
- No study was found on confetti/celebration frequency and habituation in casual games.
- No evidence was found on haptics in *web* (browser) games specifically.

## Key question 6: Where is the line between engaging design and manipulative "dark patterns", especially for teenagers? Main critiques and regulatory guidance (UK Age Appropriate Design Code, EU, Ireland)

### Takeaway
Academic and regulatory sources converge on a test: a mechanic is a dark pattern when it is designed to work against the player's own interests or without their informed consent — typically by exploiting loss aversion, FOMO, social pressure, hidden odds or children's limited commercial literacy. For teenagers, regulators in the UK (ICO Children's Code and 2023 games guidance), the EU (DSA Article 25 and 28, the July 2025 minors guidelines, consumer-law enforcement on games) and Ireland (digital age of consent 16; the Gambling Regulation Act 2024, which licenses "games" and lotteries and gives charities a specific regime) all point the same way: no engagement features that rely on pressure or loss, transparent costs and odds, and defaults that favour the child's wellbeing. For PresLink, the paid-play-plus-prizes structure additionally needs a legal check under the Irish gambling regime.

### Cited Findings

**Academic critiques**
- Dark game design patterns are features "a creator uses deliberately to produce experiences that work against players' interests, usually without their consent"; categories include *temporal* (grinding, playing by appointment), *monetary* and *social-capital-based* patterns. — Zagal, Björk & Lewis (2013), FDG 2013, via [Deceptive Design](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/dark-patterns-in-the-design-of-games) (verified); [Chalmers record](https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/177148) (verified). Foundational.
- Heuristic evaluation of popular casual mobile games catalogues grinding ("forces the players to sit through repetitive mechanics to make progress") and play-by-appointment. — Dahlan & Susanty via [Deceptive Design](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/finding-dark-patterns-in-casual-mobile-games-using-heuristic-evaluation) (verified).
- Building an ontology of dark game patterns "is itself far from an easy task" and effects depend on individual characteristics. — Aagaard, Doherty, Knudsen & Bækgaard (2022), "A Game of Dark Patterns", [dokk.org record](https://dokk.org/library/a_game_of_dark_paterns_2022) (verified).
- Children's free-to-play apps: a framework for critically considering "dark design" aspects aimed at children. — Fitton & Read (2019), IDC 2019, [doi:10.1145/3311927.3323136](https://doi.org/10.1145/3311927.3323136) (URL from memory).

### Inferences
A practical line test for PresLink, derived from the above (each clause traces to a source):
- **Transparent, deterministic scoring with no hidden operator settings** (Key Master cases; UCPD on misleading practices): publish the scoring formula and light speed; never vary speed or scoring per player or per time; show the tap offset on every light.
- **No loss-framed or appointment mechanics for under-18s** (EC 2025 guidelines; Silverman & Barasch; Zagal "playing by appointment"): no paid-play streaks, no "your rank expires tonight", no countdown timers on the purchase step.
- **Spending guardrails** (CPC principles; ICO FOMO guidance): show the price in euro on the play button, keep a visible running total of what the player has spent this campaign, cap plays per day, and require a deliberate second step to pay rather than a one-tap "play again".
- **Honest celebration** (Dixon LDW research): celebrate genuine bests only.
- **Social comparison with dignity** (Hanus & Fox; Christy & Fox): nicknames, class/year-relative boards, opt-out from public boards, no "bottom of the table" messaging.
- **Data minimisation and age-aware consent** (ICO code; DPA 2018 s.31; DPC Fundamentals): no behavioural profiling of students; parental consent flows for under-16s where consent is the basis; no reward for sharing data.
- **Legal check on the game/prize structure** (Gambling Regulation Act 2024): €2 per play plus prizes is a "game" played for money with a prize. Whether a timing game is treated as pure skill (outside the Act) or "partly skill and partly chance" (potentially requiring a charitable gambling licence or structuring as an exempt charitable lottery) is a question for an Irish solicitor; the 1956 Gaming and Lotteries Act's language, which the 2024 Act replaces, covered games "whether of skill or chance or partly of skill and partly of chance", so the skill defence should not be assumed. Any suggestion of adding random draws or chance-based bonuses would make this risk worse.

### Gaps

## Practical guidance (cross-cutting): feedback timing, sound and haptics, score presentation, and making scoring feel fair rather than rigged

### Takeaway
Feel "instant" means feedback within tens of milliseconds, with the drop in perceived quality starting around 70–100 ms and the classic 100 ms rule as the outer bound; browsers add their own latency, so a timing game must measure taps with high-resolution timestamps at `pointerdown`, compute the light's position from time rather than frame count, and show the player exactly where their tap landed. Perceived fairness comes from determinism, transparency and consistency, and from the absence of anything that looks like an operator-adjustable "win rate".

### Cited Findings
- Perceived simultaneity and quality thresholds for touch feedback: PSS 5/19/32 ms (tactile/audio/visual); quality drop at 70–100 ms (tactile/audio) and 100–150 ms (visual); jitter above ~72 ms hurts satisfaction. — Kaaresoja et al. (2014) [Glasgow eprint](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/104653) (verified); [thesis](https://theses.gla.ac.uk/7075) (verified). STRONG.
- The "0.1 second" limit for feedback to feel instantaneous (with 1 s and 10 s as the next thresholds) derives from Card, Robertson & Mackinlay's 1991 analysis and is popularised by Nielsen. — Card et al. (1991), CHI '91, [doi:10.1145/108844.108874](https://doi.org/10.1145/108844.108874) (URL from memory); Nielsen, "Response Times: The 3 Important Limits", [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/response-times-3-important-limits/) (URL from memory; host was policy-blocked in this session). Classic.
- Direct-touch latency perception: lab studies found people can detect dragging latency down to the low single-digit milliseconds and tapping latency in the tens of milliseconds. — Ng et al. (2012), UIST, [doi:10.1145/2380116.2380174](https://doi.org/10.1145/2380116.2380174); Jota et al. (2013), CHI, [doi:10.1145/2470654.2481317](https://doi.org/10.1145/2470654.2481317); Deber et al. (2015), CHI, [doi:10.1145/2702123.2702300](https://doi.org/10.1145/2702123.2702300) (all URLs from memory; exact thresholds not recalled with enough confidence to quote). MODERATE–STRONG.
- Google's RAIL model: respond to input within 100 ms; produce each animation frame within ~16 ms (10 ms of work) to hold 60 fps. — [web.dev RAIL](https://web.dev/articles/rail) (URL from memory). PRACTITIONER guideline grounded in the HCI literature above.
- Unit effect, numerosity, reference points and peak-end: see KQ3 citations.
- Rigging and trust: Key Master lawsuits and manual-based "win threshold" settings (verified; see KQ6).
- Losses disguised as wins and sound-driven win overestimation (verified; see KQ5).
- Apple's Human Interface Guidelines on haptics advise using haptics consistently, sparingly and in sync with visual/audio feedback. — [Apple HIG, Playing haptics](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/playing-haptics) (URL from memory). PRACTITIONER.

### Inferences
- **Timing architecture**: drive the light from `performance.now()` so position is a pure function of time; record the tap timestamp at `pointerdown` (not `click`, not `touchend`); render the freeze-frame on the next animation frame (≤ 16 ms) and fire the sound/haptic immediately; keep the whole verdict under ~50 ms on mid-range Android phones. Lower frame rates on cheap phones must not change the *score*, only the smoothness of the moving light.
- **Fairness through transparency**: publish the formula (e.g., points = 1,000 − k × |offset ms|, with k and the target band stated); show the measured offset on every light; use the same speed for everyone within a campaign; validate scores server-side (replay the timestamps) rather than trusting client totals; display a "how scoring works" page and a short fairness statement ("same speed for everyone; no hidden settings; your tap time is measured on your phone and checked on our server").
- **Device fairness**: because touch and display latency differ across phones, consider a one-time per-device calibration light (unscored) or an explicit note that the score is relative to the player's own device, and provide a free practice light so the first paid play is not the first touch of the game.
- **Score presentation**: keep 0–1,000; show per-light scores and the average; show PB, next round-number club and next leaderboard place with distances; brief count-up; celebrate only genuine bests; end on the most positive true fact.
- **Sound and haptics**: short, pitched "tick" per light that rises with score; a distinct sound for a new PB; everything mutable; haptics as an Android bonus, never relied upon; keep audio latency low by using the Web Audio API with preloaded buffers unlocked on first tap (PRACTITIONER).

### Gaps
- No study was found on perceived fairness of skill scoring in mobile web games or on calibration approaches for device latency; the fairness recommendations are inferences from the rigged-machine cases and latency research.
- Exact JND values for tapping latency from Jota/Deber could not be re-checked and are deliberately not quoted.
