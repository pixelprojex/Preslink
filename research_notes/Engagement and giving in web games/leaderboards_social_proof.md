# Leaderboards, competition, social proof and social features as drivers of engagement and giving

*Scope and method note for the report writer.* These notes cover six key questions plus one section on the specific design choices named in the brief (resets/seasons, ties, badges/titles, spot prizes, multiple boards). Research was done with web search only: in this session the page-fetch tool could not resolve any host and the shared search budget ran out, so most findings rest on abstracts, repository records and search-engine summaries of the papers rather than full texts. Where a figure comes from a secondhand summary rather than the primary paper this is said explicitly. Every bullet carries an evidence grade:

- **[Strong]** randomised field or lab experiment in a peer-reviewed journal, or a meta-analysis
- **[Moderate]** peer-reviewed but small, quasi-experimental, survey or qualitative
- **[Weak]** secondhand report of a study, failed/unclear replication, or unread full text
- **[Practitioner]** industry blog, vendor, journalist or designer opinion; vendor numbers are unaudited

Target product (for the inferences): PresLink Arcade, a small charity arcade website used by Irish secondary-school students, teachers and parents, with three boards (Everyone, Students, Teachers), spot prizes (best single light, most plays), initials-only identities, a live "raised so far" ring, an end date and end-of-campaign prizes.

## Q1. What does peer-reviewed research say about leaderboards' effects on people at the top, middle and bottom, the "discouragement" effect, and designs that mitigate it?

### Takeaway
Leaderboards reliably raise short-run effort because the displayed scores act as self-set goals, but in longer-running classroom studies absolute, public boards energise the top while the middle and bottom report rising pressure and disengagement; the best-supported mitigations are relative ("around-me") boards, anonymity options for low-ranked players, smaller comparison groups, self-progress feedback, and effort-based as well as performance-based recognition.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong, lab]** Landers, Bauer & Callan (2017, *Computers in Human Behavior*) randomly assigned participants to do-your-best, easy, difficult or impossible goals, or to a leaderboard whose displayed scores matched those levels; the leaderboard alone pushed performance to levels similar to the difficult and impossible goal conditions, implying people spontaneously set goals near the top of the board, and goal commitment moderated leaderboard effects much as it moderates assigned goals — [University of Minnesota record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/gamification-of-task-performance-with-leaderboards-a-goal-setting/); [UniversityXP summary](https://universityxp.com/research/2021/1/25/gamification-of-task-performance-with-leaderboards-a-goal-setting-experiment)
- **[Moderate, replication]** Kratochvíl et al. (2025, *Learning and Individual Differences*) extended the Landers design: leaderboards improved speed-based (quantitative) performance but not accuracy-based (qualitative) performance; self-reported performance landed between the easy and impossible goal conditions; the effect was stronger among experienced learners — [Masaryk University record](https://www.muni.cz/en/research/publications/2495997)
- **[Moderate, poster]** Garcia, Jha, Bauer & Landers (APS 2017) found gamification had effects independent of trait competitiveness, with no interaction, suggesting leaderboards suit competitive and non-competitive people about equally — [APS 2017 poster](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/conventions/archive/2017annual/paper/7459/)
- **[Strong, field RCT]** Chen, Harper, Konstan & Li (2010, *American Economic Review* 100(4):1358–1398), MovieLens: telling users the median member's rating count made below-median users increase monthly ratings by 530% while above-median users decreased by 62%; both groups converged on the median (conformity to a newly created norm), the effect was far stronger below the median (interaction of conformity and competitive preferences), and in a separate "net benefit vs average" treatment above-average users contributed 94% of new database updates — [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/social-comparisons-and-contributions-to-online-communities-a-fiel/); [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v100y2010i4p1358-98.html)
- **[Moderate, course study]** Bai, Hew, Sailer & Jia (2021, *Computers & Education* 173:104297, "From top to bottom: How positions on different types of leaderboard may affect fully online student learning performance, intrinsic motivation, and course engagement"): the absolute leaderboard intensified students' sense of comparison and competitiveness more than the relative leaderboard; students in the bottom third preferred anonymous comparison on an absolute leaderboard but public comparison on a relative one — [LMU Munich record](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/96762); [dblp listing](https://dblp.dagstuhl.de/pid/270/6034.html)
- **[Moderate, conference]** A companion 2021 IEEE ICET study of absolute vs relative leaderboards in online learning reported no statistically significant difference in learning gain between the two settings, and that most students preferred the relative leaderboard (author field blank in the record, so the link to Bai/Hew is unconfirmed) — [HKU repository](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/307985)
- **[Moderate, course study]** Bai & Hew (2025, *Educational Technology & Society*): in an absolute-leaderboard group the top-ranked were energised by public visibility, while middle- and bottom-ranked students reported more peer pressure as the course went on; the authors recommend relative leaderboards and limiting public display of the bottom ranks — [HKU repository record (identity of record inferred from search result)](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/368255)
- **[Moderate, quasi-experiment]** Hanus & Fox (2015, *Computers & Education* 80:152–161): two sections with the same curriculum, one with a leaderboard and badges; the gamified section showed less motivation, satisfaction and empowerment over time, and the effect on final exam scores was mediated by intrinsic motivation; secondary sources say the authors argue gamifying an already interesting course can feel "controlling"; a 2018 corrigendum exists and its contents were not checked — [UCLA SMHP brief hosting the paper](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf); [Hanus publication list](https://osu.academia.edu/MichaelHanus)
- **[Weak, classroom anecdote]** The same UCLA-hosted document describes a class in which bottom-of-leaderboard students disengaged because they felt they could never catch the leaders, and the class voted to replace the system at week six — [UCLA SMHP brief](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)
- **[Weak, n=45]** Malayao et al. (*JOTSE*), mathematical-physics students: higher performers improved alongside rankings while lower-ranked students lost motivation; authors call the effect context-dependent — [JOTSE article](https://jotse.org/index.php/jotse/article/view/3321)
- **[Moderate, quasi-experiment n=89]** An engineering-education study (ESPOL) of leaderboard gamification in an intro programming course found significant learning gains in the gamified group and no significant interaction effect after background controls — [ESPOL PDF](https://www.cti.espol.edu.ec/sites/default/files/docs_pdf/Gamification%20through%20leaderboards_An%20empirical%20study%20in%20engineering%20education.pdf)
- **[Review]** An RWTH Aachen review concludes poorly designed leaderboards discourage lower-ranked students while well-designed ones promote healthy competition, i.e. design, not the mechanic, decides the sign of the effect — [RWTH publication](https://publications.rwth-aachen.de/record/1010913/files/1010913.pdf)
- **[Moderate, survey]** Jia, Liu, Yu & Voida (CHI 2017, "Designing Leaderboards for Gamification"): perceptions of leaderboards varied with the respondent's rank position, the application domain (most favourable in fitness, least in social networking) and personality; more extraverted people reported more positive leaderboard experiences regardless of rank — [IUPUI author manuscript](https://scholarworks.iupui.edu/bitstream/1805/17645/1/Jia_2018_designing.pdf)
- **[Strong, meta-analysis]** Sailer & Homner (2020, *Educational Psychology Review* 32:77–112): gamification of learning shows small positive effects on cognitive (g = .49, 19 studies), motivational (g = .36, 16) and behavioural (g = .25, 9) outcomes; combining competition with collaboration (and adding game fiction) was particularly effective for behavioural outcomes, with a tentative similar pattern for motivation; heterogeneity is high — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w); [open-access PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109056/109056.pdf)
- **[Strong, lab + archival]** Garcia & Tor (2009, *Psychological Science*, "The N-effect"): told the top 20% would win $5, participants competing against 10 others finished a quiz in 29 s vs 33 s when competing against 100; average SAT scores fall as the number of test-takers at a venue rises; the effect runs through social comparison and is strong among people high in social-comparison orientation; the smallest group size at which it appears is unknown — [Garcia & Tor PDF](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~smgarcia/pubs/n-effect.pdf); [University of Michigan news](https://news.umich.edu/?p=7511); [Discover coverage](https://www.discovermagazine.com/mind/does-having-more-competitors-lower-the-motivation-to-compete)
- **[Moderate, field]** Zuckerman & Gal-Oz (2014, *Personal and Ubiquitous Computing*, "Deconstructing gamification"): a "quantified" walking app (daily goal, real-time progress) significantly raised walking time; versions adding virtual rewards and social comparison performed only as well as the quantified version, i.e. social comparison added nothing over self-progress feedback — [paper PDF](https://runi.ac.il/media/whrh3e33/deconstructing-gamification.pdf)
- **[Review]** Koivisto & Hamari (2019) reviewed 16 comparison studies of gamified physical activity: positive results overall, but less optimistic with more rigorous designs — [Tampere University record](https://researchportal.tuni.fi/en/publications/gamification-of-physical-activity-a-systematic-literature-review-/)
- **[Exists, unread]** "Gamified double-edged sword: exploring the different social comparison..." (Copenhagen Business School) studies upward/downward social comparison in gamified systems; no findings retrieved — [CBS record](https://research.cbs.dk/en/publications/gamified-double-edged-sword-exploring-the-different-social-compar/)
- **[Exists, unread]** Höllig, Tumasjan & Welpe (HICSS 2018), "The Interaction of Trait Competitiveness and Leaderboard Design", DOI 10.24251/hicss.2018.146; abstract not retrievable — [TUM record](https://mediatum.ub.tum.de/1473494)
- **[Practitioner]** A gambling-industry guide claims a single global leaderboard motivates roughly the top 5–10% and can demoralise the rest, recommending segmented "micro" leaderboards; its cited research could not be traced — [Blask](https://blask.com/knowledge/leaderboard/)
- **[Practitioner]** A sales-gamification vendor argues winner-takes-all and never-refreshing boards make the top feel out of reach and demotivate the majority — [myhubble](https://www.myhubble.money/blogs/leaderboards-that-work-gamification-without-the-gimmicks)

### Inferences
- The two mechanisms in the strong evidence point in opposite directions for a weeks-long campaign: top scores act as goals (Landers) and a closable gap to a reference point drives large effort increases (Chen et al.), but a gap that is not closable produces the disengagement seen in the classroom studies. For PresLink the "best single light" board is a pure performance board; players far below the top should be shown a reachable reference (next place, next tier, their own best) rather than only the top score.
- Splitting into Everyone / Students / Teachers reduces N and matches the reference group, which the N-effect and the relative-leaderboard studies both favour; the separate boards also stop adults' or outliers' scores from making the student board look unreachable.
- Kratochvíl's finding that leaderboards move quantity (speed) more than quality (accuracy) suggests a "most plays" board will respond more strongly to ranking than a "best single light" board, which is useful because plays are the revenue driver.
- The bottom-third preference for anonymity on absolute boards, plus Bai & Hew's advice to limit public display of bottom ranks, supports showing a public top-N with each player's own rank shown privately ("you are 41st of 120") rather than a full public list.
- Hanus & Fox is the main caution: the arcade games must be fun in their own right, with the board as an optional layer, not a controlling evaluation; a charity frame ("every play raises money") is a plausible protective factor but untested.

### Gaps
- No randomised study was found that directly compares a top-N display with an "around-me" display on the same product; the relative-vs-absolute evidence is from online university courses, not games or secondary pupils.
- Nearly all classroom evidence is university students; I found no leaderboard study specific to 12–18-year-olds in a school fundraising context.
- No peer-reviewed study of separate role-based boards (e.g., student vs teacher) was found.
- Christy & Fox (2014, *Computers & Education*, "Leaderboards in a virtual classroom", on stereotype threat and women's maths performance) is a frequently cited paper on who is harmed by leaderboards; searches did not return it, so it is not cited here.
- Full texts of Bai et al. 2021/2025 and Höllig et al. 2018 could not be read; effect sizes for position-by-board-type interactions are therefore missing.

## Q2. What is the evidence on showing "rank gap" information (points to the next place) and on rank-change notifications prompting replays?

### Takeaway
No experiment was found that isolates a "points to next place" display or a "you've been overtaken" notification; the adjacent evidence says personalised distance-to-reference-point information is powerful when the gap is closable (MovieLens, goal-gradient), and practitioner systems (Duolingo zones, Strava "lost KOM" emails) use it with only anecdotal evidence of replays, while reminder/notification fatigue has measurable costs.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong, field RCT]** Personalised social comparison information (your count vs the median) produced a 530% increase in contributions from those below the reference point and a 62% decrease from those above it; the authors describe the result as conformity to a norm plus competitive preferences — [Chen et al. 2010](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/social-comparisons-and-contributions-to-online-communities-a-fiel/)
- **[Strong, lab]** Displayed leaderboard scores function as goals and performance tracks the displayed level, moderated by goal commitment — [Landers et al. 2017](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/gamification-of-task-performance-with-leaderboards-a-goal-setting/)
- **[Strong, field + lab]** Cryder, Loewenstein & Seltman (2013, *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*, "Goal gradient in helping behavior"): contribution rates increase as recipients approach their fundraising goals (Kiva robot study), funds close to their goal received more donations in a large field experiment, and increased perceived impact of late-stage contributions mediates the effect — [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/goal-gradient-in-helping-behavior/); [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/GoalGradient.pdf)
- **[Strong, lab]** Competitive motivation is driven by social comparison and weakens as the number of rivals grows — [Garcia & Tor 2009](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~smgarcia/pubs/n-effect.pdf)
- **[Practitioner]** Duolingo matches each learner weekly with people of similar study habits and time zone; competition spans all courses; the top 10 in the Diamond league can enter a multi-round tournament — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-leagues-leaderboards/); a fan-run teardown describes leagues as weekly 30-person groups ranked by XP over seven days (group size not confirmed by Duolingo) — [Deconstructor of Fun: Duolingo leagues](https://duolingo.deconstructoroffun.com/mechanics/leagues)
- **[Weak, anecdote]** A cyclist's blog describes receiving two automated Strava "lost KOM" emails, planning a dedicated ride to retake one, and regaining it by one second — [Loving the Bike, "Strava Attack"](https://lovingthebike.com/strava-attack/); [Part II](https://lovingthebike.com/strava-attack-part-ii/)
- **[Weak, anecdote]** A rider who lost her last London QOM says segments motivate her more than solo intervals, and that losing a QOM is hard to accept even though they cannot be kept forever — [Fit Is a Feminist Issue](https://fitisafeministissue.com/2020/07/26/losing-the-last-of-my-london-qoms/)
- **[Weak, forum]** Forum users report lost-KOM notifications for KOMs they did not know they held, KOMs from erroneous GPS data, and confusing or inaccurate alerts — [Singletrack forum](https://singletrackworld.com/forum/bike-forum/losing-a-kom-you-didnt-know-you-had/); [TrainerRoad forum](https://www.trainerroad.com/forum/t/strava-kom-qom-hunting/9560?page=3)
- **[Practitioner/vendor]** Localytics reported push-enabled app users were more likely to return a month after install, 62% vs 32% (correlational vendor data) — [Mobile World Live](https://www.mobileworldlive.com/old_latest-stories/push-notifications-boosts-app-engagement-retention-localytics/)
- **[Moderate]** A peer-reviewed trial protocol/paper on the Drink Less app notes that the immediate causal effect of a push notification on engagement had not been established — [PMC article](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10337295)
- **[Practitioner]** Guidance on push experiments warns that over-notifying drives uninstalls and recommends a holdout group — [Statsig](https://www.statsig.com/perspectives/push-experiments-engagement)
- **[Strong, field]** In a Danish charity experiment with about 53,000 prior donors, shorter deadlines and a reminder increased requests to be removed from the mailing list, even though the reminder raised both likelihood and amount of giving — [Aarhus University news](https://law.au.dk/en/current/allnews/news/artikel/longer-deadlines-make-people-donate-more-money); [working paper](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/afn/wp/14/wp14_03.pdf)
- **[Design proposal, no data]** A 2018 technical disclosure proposes re-engagement messages built from real-time leaderboard standings showing nearby users at the player's level — [TD Commons](https://www.tdcommons.org/dpubs_series/1749)

### Inferences
- Showing "12 points to 5th place" is most defensible when the gap is small; the MovieLens pattern suggests the display should always be relative to a point the player can plausibly reach (next place, next tier, own best), not to the overall leader.
- "You've been overtaken" messages are plausible replay triggers (the Strava anecdotes show exactly that behaviour) but there is no causal estimate; if used, cap frequency, make them opt-in for students, and A/B with a holdout, because reminder fatigue has measurable opt-out costs.
- "You are top of the board" notices are safer than "you have dropped" notices for under-18s: they carry recognition (see Q5) without the loss framing.

### Gaps
- No A/B results on rank-gap displays or on rank-change notifications were found in peer-reviewed or industry sources.
- Garcia, Tor & Gonzalez (2006, "Ranks and rivals: a theory of competition") on proximity to the top rank or to a cutoff intensifying competition is directly relevant but was not retrieved, so it is not cited.
- Duolingo has not published the effect of its promotion/demotion-zone notifications.

## Q3. How do Duolingo leagues, Strava segments, Wordle sharing, Peloton and arcade high-score tables use competition, and what have they published about effects?

### Takeaway
All five use small, time-bounded or locally scoped comparison; Duolingo's own account is that leaderboards were one of three levers behind a 4.5x DAU increase (the widely quoted 17% learning-time / 3x highly-engaged figures are secondhand), Strava added an effort-based "Local Legend" award because speed leaderboards were unreachable for most riders, Wordle's growth is credited to a spoiler-free shareable result grid, Peloton's evidence is a motivation survey rather than experiments, and arcade initials date from 1979 hardware constraints.

### Cited Findings
**Duolingo**
- **[Practitioner, primary]** Duolingo describes leagues as weekly groups of learners matched on similar study habits and time zone, with competition across all courses and a multi-round tournament for the Diamond top 10 — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-leagues-leaderboards/)
- **[Practitioner, primary]** Former CPO Jorge Mazal writes that he joined as Head of Product in late 2017, DAU growth had slowed to single-digit annual rates by mid-2018, and that leaderboards, a refocus on push notifications and streak optimisation (with other work) grew DAU 4.5x over four years; the first phase focused on retention through gamification — [Lenny's Newsletter: How Duolingo reignited user growth](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth); [leaderboards section anchor](https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/i/104096876/leaderboards-vector)
- **[Weak, secondhand]** Secondary write-ups attribute to Mazal's article the figures that leaderboards (modelled on FarmVille 2) produced a 17% increase in overall learning time and tripled the number of highly engaged learners; I could not open the article to confirm — [Lakhiani Substack](https://marishalakhiani.substack.com/p/breaking-down-duolingos-growth-model); [Medium summary](https://medium.com/@theniteshknows/how-duolingo-reignited-user-growth-a-masterclass-in-gamification-strategy-2bc2d40e1876)
- **[Practitioner, unsourced]** A vendor blog claims Duolingo's gamification cut churn from 47% to 28%, with no source — [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/blog-gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo)
- **[Exists, unread]** A 2026 University of Essex paper (Springer, s10212-026-01157-3) on Duolingo engagement covers leaderboards and friend challenges; results not retrieved — [Essex repository PDF](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/43461/1/s10212-026-01157-3.pdf)

**Strava**
- **[Moderate, qualitative]** Barratt (2017, *Health & Place* 46:328–336, "Healthy competition") studies how Strava-style gamification instigates and maintains motivation to exercise and compete in everyday cycling, and its negative consequences — [Staffordshire repository](https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/2684); press coverage of the related presentation says apps like Strava encourage cyclists to ride faster, further, more often and less carefully, with "anti-social" riders disrupting club rides — [BikeBiz](https://bikebiz.com/research-claims-strava-encourages-riders-to-cycle-anti-socially/)
- **[Practitioner, primary product]** Local Legends (2020) awards the rider who completes a segment most often in a rolling 90-day window, alongside the speed-based KOM/QOM boards; Strava's stated motivation was concern over how hard it is to get onto leaderboards in some areas; there are overall and women's boards; full access was for premium subscribers; reaction was mixed, with some praising recognition for persistence rather than ability and others calling it a feature for obsessives — [BikeRadar](https://bikeradar.com/news/strava-local-legend); [DC Rainmaker](https://dcrainmaker.com/2020/06/strava-legends-feature.html); [road.cc](https://road.cc/content/news/strava-launches-local-legends-heres-how-become-one-275471); [iMore](https://www.imore.com/strava-announces-local-legends-new-way-compete-segments)
- **[Weak, forum]** A 2012 forum thread describes segments encouraging riders to push harder on specific sections, with one user calling them a good training motivation tool — [TrainerRoad forum](https://www.trainerroad.com/forum/t/strava-kom-qom-hunting/9560?page=3)

**Wordle**
- **[Journalism]** TechCrunch reports Wordle brought "tens of millions" of new users to the New York Times and credits the ease of sharing the emoji results grid for its virality; Wardle says a single daily puzzle, rather than a random word per player, was essential — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2022/05/04/wordle-new-york-times-user-growth)
- **[Journalism]** The share feature was added in December 2021 after Wardle saw players already posting coloured-square results — [Know Your Meme](https://amp.knowyourmeme.com/memes/subcultures/wordle)
- **[Practitioner opinion]** A design analysis argues the grid reveals the guess pattern but not the words, so it can be posted anywhere without spoiling the puzzle, making a boast socially safe and acting as a peer recommendation; causal share of growth unquantified — [Dinogame analysis](https://dinogame.gg/blog/why-is-wordle-so-popular/); [Beast of Traal](https://beastoftraal.com/?p=19327)

**Peloton**
- **[Moderate, survey]** Davies, Aicher, Newland & Hungenberg (2024, *Journal of Applied Sport Management* 16(1)) surveyed 663 Peloton users and identified three motivation clusters (Wellness, Physique, Social Performance), concluding connected-fitness brands should create social engagement and opportunities to better one's own past performance — [JASM article](https://trace.tennessee.edu/jasm/vol16/iss1/1)
- **[Journalism/essay]** Anne Helen Petersen describes instructors steering riders away from leaderboard obsession and riders filtering the board to people nearby or in their age group — [Culture Study](https://annehelen.substack.com/p/the-counterintuitive-mechanics-of)
- **[Journalism]** Reporting on Peloton's Lanebreak game says Peloton experimented with motivators ranging from social cooperation to competition, without outcome data — [Input](https://www.inputmag.com/gaming/pelotons-working-on-a-game-for-its-connected-exercise-bike)
- **[Practitioner]** A behavioural-science teardown of Peloton's leaderboard exists but could not be fetched — [Kristen Berman, Product Teardowns](https://kristenberman.substack.com/p/how-is-peloton-so-good-at-getting-089)

**Arcade high-score tables**
- **[History]** Space Invaders (1978) was the first arcade game to display the previous highest score; Exidy's Star Fire (1979) first let players save initials beside a score, stored in RAM and wiped whenever operators unplugged machines; Asteroids and Star Fire offered top-10/top-20 tables with three-character initials, which became the norm — [Wikipedia: Score (video games)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_(video_games)); [Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/games/first-video-game-high-score-invented-history); [Arcade Blogger](https://arcadeblogger.com/2021/01/31/anatomy-of-arcade-high-score-tables/)
- **[History]** Developers used default high-score tables to hide their own initials as credits when companies kept names secret — [Waxy.org](https://waxy.org/2023/05/1980s-arcade-game-designers-hid-their-names-in-high-score-tables); [Set Side B](https://setsideb.com/dev)

### Inferences
- The industry pattern is "small group + short cycle + matched peers": Duolingo (about 30 learners, one week, matched by habits), Strava Local Legends (90-day rolling window, local segment), Wordle (one shared puzzle per day). PresLink's end date already gives one cycle; a weekly sub-cycle would mirror the practice.
- Strava's Local Legends is the closest industry precedent for PresLink's "most plays" prize: an effort award added explicitly because the performance board excluded most participants.
- Wordle's lesson for a share feature: share a result image that boasts without exposing identity or spoiling others; for PresLink that could be a "my best light / my rank on the Students board" card with initials only.
- Arcade initials are a strong genre convention, so initials-only identity will read as authentic rather than as a privacy compromise.

### Gaps
- No primary Duolingo data on leagues beyond Mazal's narrative; the 17%/3x numbers are secondhand.
- Strava has published no outcome data for segments or Local Legends.
- No controlled evidence on Wordle sharing's contribution to growth.
- Peloton evidence is survey-based; no leaderboard experiments published.

## Q4. What does research on social proof and goal progress (thermometers, "N people have already given", recency of activity) say about participation rates and amounts?

### Takeaway
Strong field-experimental evidence shows that visible information about others' giving raises amounts (anchoring on a high reference point, around the 90th–95th percentile, works best for first-time givers), that local/peer norms beat global ones, that giving accelerates as a goal gets close, and that public recognition raises giving; the same literature shows the mechanism cuts both ways, since small visible donations and minority norms lower subsequent giving.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong, field]** Shang & Croson (2009, *Economic Journal* 119:1422–1439): in public-radio drives, telling callers that another donor had just given a specified amount raised contributions; the most effective reference level was the 90th–95th percentile of previous contributions; the effect held for new members but not renewing members; later analysis found no crowd-out of future contributions — [paper PDF](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/27%20Shang%20Croson%202009.pdf); [Knowledge@Wharton summary](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/raising-the-bar-the-role-of-social-information-in-charitable-giving/); [UMN record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-field-experiment-in-charitable-contribution-the-impact-of-socia/)
- **[Weak, failed replication]** A later replication attempt (NVSQ, 2019) found no evidence that social comparisons affected donation decisions; its sample was mostly renewing members, the group the original study also found unaffected — [SAGE abstract](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0899764019869537)
- **[Strong, field]** Croson & Shang (2008, *Experimental Economics*): renewing donors shown a reference donation below their previous gift reduced their giving ("downward social information") — [Cambridge Core](https://core-cms.cambridgecore.org/core/journals/experimental-economics/article/impact-of-downward-social-information-on-contribution-decisions/5B09EA278BFAC7F2DFB0319E13C9D11C)
- **[Strong, field]** Frey & Meier (2004, *American Economic Review* 94(5):1717–1722): 2,500 randomly selected University of Zurich students told the share of students who contribute to two social funds; giving rose on average when told that many others contribute, with the effect depending on past contribution behaviour and some types unaffected — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/aea/aecrev/v94y2004i5p1717-1722.html); [paper PDF](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreoni/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/23%20Frey%20Meier%202004.pdf)
- **[Strong, field]** Agerström, Carlsson, Nicklasson & Guntell (2016, *Journal of Economic Psychology* 52:147–153): embedding a descriptive norm in a charity brochure substantially increased university students' giving versus a standard altruistic appeal, and local norms (about their own university) were more effective than global norms — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joepsy/v52y2016icp147-153.html)
- **[Moderate]** Bartke, Friedl, Gelhaar & Reh (Kiel, 2016): people who had to guess the descriptive norm before seeing it donated significantly more, attributed to attention — [Kiel Institute](https://ifw-kiel.de/publications/social-comparison-nudges-guessing-the-norm-increases-charitable-giving-23438)
- **[Moderate]** Andersson, Erlandsson & Västfjäll ("Norm avoiders"): participants were more likely to donate when shown that a majority donates than when shown a minority does; about half chose to reveal the norm when given the option (link mapping from search results uncertain) — [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10219411)
- **[Strong, large observational]** Smith, Windmeijer & Wright (2015, *Economic Journal*, "Peer effects in charitable giving: evidence from the (running) field"): 300,000 donations to 10,000+ London Marathon JustGiving/Virgin Money Giving pages; a £10 increase in the mean of past visible donations raised a subsequent donation by about £2.50 (£3.50 in the 2012 working paper); a single small donation lowered subsequent amounts by about £5; donors appear to use earlier donations to judge what is appropriate rather than as a signal of charity quality; the 2012 version found no role for fundraising targets — [CMPO abstract 13/302](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/cmpo/publications/papers/2013/abstract302.html); [working paper PDF](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/media-library/sites/cmpo/migrated/documents/wp302.pdf); [Bristol press release](https://www.bristol.ac.uk/news/2012/8648.html)
- **[Strong, field + lab]** Goal gradient in helping: contribution rates rise as a fund approaches its goal, driven by perceived impact of late-stage gifts — [Cryder et al. 2013](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/goal-gradient-in-helping-behavior/); [Foundation Beyond Belief summary](https://foundationbeyondbelief.org/node/2043.html)
- **[Moderate, observational]** Kuppuswamy & Bayus (Kickstarter): backer support has a "bathtub" shape (heavy in the first and last weeks); projects reaching about 30% of goal early are more likely to succeed; final days draw backers who see their contribution as pivotal; publication year cited variously as 2013–2018 — [NTU review PDF](https://review.management.ntu.edu.tw/paper/9192-P.pdf); [CMF commentary](https://cmf-fmc.ca/?p=69881); [Groth summary of working paper](https://yannigroth.com/2013/02/24/the-dynamics-of-backer-support-in-crowdfunding-findings-from-kickstarter)
- **[Weak, secondhand]** A vendor article describes an experiment mailing flyers with a thermometer filled to 10%, 66% or 88% (plus none); the 88% version drew more donations and online giving rose as progress grew; the original study could not be located — [Donately](https://blog.donately.com/fundraising-thermometers)
- **[Practitioner]** Fundraising-platform blogs attribute thermometer effects to goal gradient, social proof and loss aversion, recommend seeding the bar with money already raised, raising the target once passed, and auto-updating thermometers — [Fundraise Up](https://fundraiseup.com/blog/fundraising-thermometer/); [Bloomerang](https://bloomerang.com/blog/ask-an-expert-does-sharing-a-campaigns-fundraising-goal-drive-more-donations)
- **[Strong, field RCT]** Bond et al. (2012, *Nature* 489:295–298): a Facebook "social" message showing friends who had voted, sent to 61 million users, increased self-expression, information seeking and validated turnout by about 340,000 voters; the spread through close friends exceeded the direct effect — [paper PDF](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/bond_et_al._2012_-_a_61-million-person_experiment_in_social_influence_political_mobilization.pdf); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/09/120912152831.htm)
- **[Strong, field]** Landry, Lange, List, Price & Rupp (2006, *QJE* 121:747–782): door-to-door drive to about 5,000 households; lottery treatments raised considerably more than voluntary contribution treatments; a re-analysis reports a 17.1% contribution rate for the single-prize lottery vs 9.5% for the standard voluntary mechanism — [NBER working paper](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11611/w11611.pdf); [re-analysis (arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.02276); companion lab/field work finds single and multiple prize lotteries dominate voluntary contributions in dollars and in number of contributors — [Lange, List & Price](https://econpapers.repec.org/paper/febartefa/00381.htm)
- **[Strong, field]** Hamari (2017, *Computers in Human Behavior* 71:469–478): two-year before/after field experiment (N = 1,410 then 1,579) on a peer-to-peer marketplace; badges made users significantly more likely to post, transact, comment and use the service; an earlier analysis found the gain concentrated among users who actively monitored their own and others' badges — [Turku record](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/27336812?lang=en_US); [2013 interim results](https://gamification-research.org/2013/05/1-5-year-experiment-on-gamification-surprising-results/)
- **[Practitioner]** Games Done Quick livestreams run live donation totals with "incentives" unlocked at thresholds and bid wars; one nonprofit blog reports a single incentive (saving the animals in Super Metroid) raised over $350,000, more than a fifth of the event total — [Soapbox Engage](https://www.soapboxengage.com/blog/281-what-awesome-games-done-quick-teaches-nonprofits); [Cornell College blog](https://digitalwriting.cornellcollege.edu/hit-point/?p=236); [non-academic data analysis](https://minimaxir.com/2016/01/agdq-2016/)
- **[Secondhand definition]** Nielsen Norman Group defines social proof as people referencing others' behaviour to guide their own, with users reading reviews, likes, shares and press coverage as legitimacy signals; NN/g's own article could not be fetched — [OpenClassrooms citing NN/g](https://openclassrooms.com/en/courses/4556196-apply-psychology-to-design/5150916-design-for-social-dynamics-and-trust)

### Inferences
- The "raised so far" ring is well supported, with two provisos from the goal-gradient and Kickstarter evidence: seed it (do not launch at 0%), and make the end-of-goal visible, because contributions accelerate close to the target and participants want to be the one who tips it over.
- A "recent activity" feed that shows amounts is double-edged: visible high amounts raise later gifts (Smith et al.), but a visible €1 play lowers them; show counts and initials ("A.B. just played", "N plays today") rather than tiny amounts, or show amounts only for larger gifts.
- "N people have already given" works when N is a majority or a large number; a low count can signal a minority norm and backfire (Andersson; Frey & Meier). Prefer local framing: "63% of 5th years have played" beats a global figure (Agerström).
- Social proof is strongest when it comes from identifiable close ties (Bond et al.); in a school this suggests class- or year-level counters and friend-visible activity rather than anonymous global counters.
- Spot prizes structured as raffles (one entry per play) have the best evidence for raising participation rates; the GDQ pattern of threshold-unlock incentives is a practitioner analogue for "when the ring hits 50% we unlock X".

### Gaps
- The thermometer flyer experiment is secondhand and unlocated; no peer-reviewed evidence specifically on live-updating thermometers or on "N plays today" counters was found.
- Salganik/Watts (music lab) and Muchnik/Aral/Taylor (2013, upvote herding) are the standard references on popularity counters; neither was retrieved in this session.
- NN/g's social-proof guidance could not be read directly.

## Q5. Evidence on identity and display (initials vs full names, avatars, anonymity) and prosocial behaviour; implications for a school setting with under-18s

### Takeaway
Being identifiable or publicly recognised reliably increases prosocial behaviour and giving (image motivation), but low-ranked students prefer anonymity on absolute boards, children's privacy regimes in Ireland and the UK define a child as under 18 and prioritise best interests and high-privacy defaults, and arcade convention makes initials a natural, low-risk middle ground that preserves most of the recognition value.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong, field + lab]** Karlan & McConnell (2014, *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization*): among 4,168 prior donors to a Yale service club, the prospect of being publicly listed in tiered "giving circles" (donors over $100 named in the alumni newsletter) raised giving; a lab study with 94 students found no support for an altruistic "to encourage others" motive, pointing to social image — [NBER WP 17737](https://nber.org/papers/w17737); [J-PAL summary](https://www.povertyactionlab.org/evaluation/public-recognition-and-fundraising); [IPA](https://poverty-action.org/publication/hey-look-me-effect-giving-circles-giving)
- **[Strong, lab]** Ariely, Bracha & Meier (2009, *American Economic Review* 99(1):544–555): visibility isolates image motivation, which is an important part of prosocial motivation; monetary incentives are less effective, and more likely counterproductive, when prosocial behaviour is public than private — [Boston Fed working paper](https://www.bostonfed.org/publications/research-department-working-paper/2007/doing-good-or-doing-well-image-motivation-and-monetary-incentives-in-behaving-prosocially.aspx); [IZA DP 2968](https://repec.iza.org/dp2968.pdf)
- **[Moderate]** In a public-goods game with a behavioural study and computational model, identifiable groups contributed more and earned higher collective returns than anonymous groups — [arXiv 2506.06032](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.06032)
- **[Moderate]** Repeated prisoner's-dilemma experiments found identifiable ("onymous") partners produced more cooperation than anonymous ones, with prosocial actions linked to better rankings under identifiability — [NWPU record](https://pure.nwpu.edu.cn/en/publications/onymity-promotes-cooperation-in-social-dilemma-experiments)
- **[Moderate]** Showing participants a photo of their partner improved public-good contributions (Pradiptyo & Wibisana, 2022) — [JIEB](https://journal.ugm.ac.id/v3/jieb/article/view/4523)
- **[Moderate, children]** A Dalhousie dissertation found 5-year-olds shared about the same whether anonymous or identified, while school-aged children preferred to share as, and receive from, identified donors — [Dalhousie](https://dalspace.library.dal.ca/items/a4c98833-54e6-40e2-a4d1-6ff6dfa1544d/full)
- **[Registered, no results]** An AEA-registered online experiment on how public awards and rankings affect prosocial behaviour ("Awards and Social Image") describes the design only — [AEA RCT Registry #8901](https://www.socialscienceregistry.org/trials/8901)
- **[Strong, field RCT]** The Facebook turnout effect came from a message showing the faces of friends who had acted, with spread concentrated among close friends — [Bond et al. 2012](https://sparq.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj19021/files/media/file/bond_et_al._2012_-_a_61-million-person_experiment_in_social_influence_political_mobilization.pdf)
- **[Moderate, course study]** Bottom-third students preferred anonymous comparison on an absolute leaderboard but public comparison on a relative leaderboard — [Bai et al. 2021](https://epub.ub.uni-muenchen.de/96762); Bai & Hew (2025) recommend limiting public display of bottom ranks — [HKU record](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/368255)
- **[Moderate, survey]** Extraverts report more positive leaderboard experiences regardless of rank; social-networking contexts rated least favourable for leaderboards — [Jia et al. 2017](https://scholarworks.iupui.edu/bitstream/1805/17645/1/Jia_2018_designing.pdf)
- **[History]** Three-character initials on arcade tables date from Star Fire (1979) and became the genre norm — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_(video_games)); [Arcade Blogger](https://arcadeblogger.com/2021/01/31/anatomy-of-arcade-high-score-tables/)
- **[Regulatory, Ireland]** The Data Protection Commission's "Children Front and Centre: Fundamentals for a Child-Oriented Approach to Data Processing" (finalised December 2021) applies to services directed at, intended for or likely to be accessed by children, including educational providers; a child is anyone under 18; the best interests of the child must be the primary consideration in any processing decision — [Goodwin summary](https://www.goodwinlaw.com/en/insights/blogs/2022/01/the-irish-dpc-publishes-final-version-of-its-childrens-fundamentals); [draft summary](https://www.lexblog.com/2021/01/15/irish-dpc-publishes-draft-fundamentals-for-a-child-oriented-approach-to-data-processing/)
- **[Regulatory, Ireland]** In May 2022 the DPC issued three child-facing guides for 13–18-year-olds and wrote to schools suggesting they share them with pupils — [Inside Privacy](https://www.insideprivacy.com/childrens-privacy/irish-dpc-publishes-child-facing-privacy-guides/); [DPC letter (ACCS)](https://www.dataprotection.ie/sites/default/files/uploads/2021-11/ACCS.pdf)
- **[Regulatory, UK, comparator]** The ICO Age Appropriate Design Code defines a child as under 18, sets 15 standards, requires high-privacy defaults, and Standard 13 bars nudge techniques that lead children to provide unnecessary personal data or weaken privacy protections; the final version narrowed an earlier broader draft; one summary says the code also discourages nudges that extend children's use of a service — [ICO code PDF](https://ico.org.uk/media/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/childrens-information/childrens-code-guidance-and-resources/age-appropriate-design-a-code-of-practice-for-online-services-2-1.pdf); [TIGA summary](https://tiga.org/?p=9917); [Consumer Protection Connection](https://www.consumerprotectioncxn.com/2020/02/uk-ico-finalizes-rules-for-childrens-content/); [SuperAwesome](https://www.superawesome.com/?p=7322)

### Inferences
- Initials-only is a good compromise: within a school, initials plus the Students board are often identifiable enough to carry image motivation among friends (the group where social influence is strongest), while not publishing full names of minors on a public web page; the Dalhousie finding that school-age children prefer identified giving suggests students will want to be recognisable to peers.
- Keep the public board to the top N and show lower ranks privately or in a relative view, consistent with the bottom-third anonymity preference; let students choose their initials (or a pseudonym of three characters) and never require real names.
- Avoid avatars or photos for students; the gains from photos in lab games do not justify the data-protection cost under the DPC Fundamentals' best-interests standard.
- Because public recognition is itself a reward, pair it with intrinsic framing (the cause) rather than cash-like incentives; Ariely et al. suggest monetary rewards can undercut public prosocial behaviour, which argues for modest, fun spot prizes rather than large cash prizes on a public board.
- The Teachers board carries different norms: adults can opt in to full names, and a visible teacher/parent board doubles as social proof for students ("the adults are playing too").

### Gaps
- No study directly compares initials vs full names vs avatars on a leaderboard.
- Christy & Fox (2014) on leaderboards and stereotype threat was not retrieved.
- The DPC Fundamentals text itself was not read; the specific treatment of publishing pupils' names or initials on school websites was not found.
- Irish "digital age of consent" (16 under the Data Protection Act 2018) was not verified in this session and is therefore not cited.

## Q6. Evidence about timing: live-updating boards, end-of-campaign deadlines and "final days" effects

### Takeaway
Deadlines on their own do not reliably increase whether people give; giving clusters immediately after each prompt and at the very end of a campaign (the Kickstarter "bathtub" and year-end giving), reminders raise both participation and amounts but also raise opt-outs, and no evidence was found on live-updating versus delayed boards.

### Cited Findings
- **[Strong, field]** A Danish natural field experiment emailed and texted about 53,000 prior donors with varying deadlines: no effect of deadlines on the propensity to give; almost all donations arrived within two to three days of the message regardless of deadline ("now or never"); a reminder increased both the likelihood of donating and the amount; shorter deadlines and the reminder increased mailing-list opt-outs; the authors declined to generalise to other industries where deadlines curb procrastination; note the university news headline frames the result as longer deadlines raising money, which could not be reconciled without the full text — [Aarhus University news](https://law.au.dk/en/current/allnews/news/artikel/longer-deadlines-make-people-donate-more-money); [working paper wp14_03](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/afn/wp/14/wp14_03.pdf); [KU record](https://test01.ku.dk/:obvius/pureproxy/3974268/en/publications/now-or-never-the-effect-of-deadlines-on-charitable-giving-evidenc)
- **[Exists, results not retrieved]** Knowles, Servátka, Sullivan & Genç ran a field experiment on deadlines, procrastination and inattention/forgetting in charitable tasks (Otago WP 1501; MPRA 69621/83694), and a related *Economic Inquiry* 60(2):706–720 article titled "Procrastination and the non-monotonic effect of deadlines on task completion" — [IDEAS WP](https://ideas.repec.org/p/otg/wpaper/1501.html); [Knowles RePEc profile](https://ideas.repec.org/e/pkn61.html)
- **[Moderate, observational]** Kickstarter backer support is concentrated in the first and last weeks, and final days attract backers who see their contribution as decisive — [Kuppuswamy & Bayus via NTU review](https://review.management.ntu.edu.tw/paper/9192-P.pdf); [Groth summary](https://yannigroth.com/2013/02/24/the-dynamics-of-backer-support-in-crowdfunding-findings-from-kickstarter)
- **[Strong, field + lab]** Giving accelerates as a goal approaches, mediated by perceived impact — [Cryder et al. 2013](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/goal-gradient-in-helping-behavior/)
- **[Practitioner data]** CanadaHelps reports donations from 28–31 December accounted for about 9% of annual revenue for the charities it studied — [CanadaHelps](https://www.canadahelps.org/en/charity-life/every-email-your-charity-should-send-at-the-end-of-the-year1/); Mozilla notes US donors' growing urgency before 31 December because of tax deductions — [Mozilla Foundation blog](https://blog.mozilla.org/foundation-archive/?p=328)
- **[Practitioner]** Fundraising vendors recommend thermometers that update automatically so donors see their contribution land — [Donately](https://blog.donately.com/fundraising-thermometers)
- **[Practitioner]** Strava's Local Legend uses a rolling 90-day window so status lapses unless the rider keeps completing the segment — [BikeRadar](https://bikeradar.com/news/strava-local-legend); Duolingo leagues run on a weekly cycle — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-leagues-leaderboards/)
- **[Practitioner]** Live-updating donation totals with threshold incentives are the core format of Games Done Quick marathons — [Cornell College blog](https://digitalwriting.cornellcollege.edu/hit-point/?p=236)

### Inferences
- The end date matters less as a deadline than as an occasion for prompts: plan a launch push, a mid-campaign push and a "final 3 days" push, each expected to produce a spike within 2–3 days, and use the goal-gradient effect by pairing the final push with the gap left on the raised-so-far ring.
- Expect a bathtub curve of plays and donations; a mid-campaign weekly reset or a mid-point spot prize is a plausible way to fill the trough, though this is inference from Kickstarter patterns rather than tested.
- Keep reminders few and valuable; the Danish opt-out finding suggests one or two reminders rather than daily nudges, especially to student accounts.
- Live updating of the board and ring is a reasonable default (it is universal in the industry cases), but there is no evidence that real-time versus hourly updates changes behaviour.

### Gaps
- No study comparing live-updating with delayed or batched leaderboards was found.
- The fresh-start effect literature (Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014) on temporal landmarks, relevant to weekly resets and "new term" launches, was not retrieved.
- Results of the Knowles et al. deadline experiments were not retrieved.

## Q7. Which other design choices matter: resets and seasons, ties, badges and titles, spot prizes, personal bests and multiple boards?

### Takeaway
Resets, tiers and effort-based awards are near-universal practitioner remedies for discouragement with supportive but mostly indirect evidence; raffles have strong evidence for raising participation; badges have mixed evidence (positive in a marketplace field experiment, negative when bundled with a leaderboard in a classroom); no evidence on tie handling was found.

### Cited Findings
- **[Practitioner]** Yu-kai Chou recommends weekly, monthly or seasonal resets to give every user a level playing field and cites League of Legends' partial rank resets as re-engaging players who had given up — [Yu-kai Chou](https://yukaichou.com/advanced-gamification/how-to-design-effective-leaderboards-boosting-motivation-and-engagement/)
- **[Practitioner/vendor, unverified]** Coverage of fitness-app resets cites a leaderboard vendor's claim that competition on scheduled-reset boards runs at 3.4 times the level of all-time boards, with top-10% places changing hands eight times as often — [TechBuzz Ireland](https://techbuzzireland.com/2026/10/02/why-fitness-apps-keep-resetting-their-leaderboards/); a competing vendor piece argues for hybrid designs (weekly resets inside a longer challenge) — [NextBee](https://blog.nextbee.com/2025/12/30/real-time-leaderboards-vs-seasonal-challenges-which-works-better/)
- **[Strong, lab]** Smaller competitor pools raise competitive motivation — [Garcia & Tor 2009](https://public.websites.umich.edu/~smgarcia/pubs/n-effect.pdf)
- **[Strong, field]** Local reference groups are more persuasive than global ones for giving — [Agerström et al. 2016](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/joepsy/v52y2016icp147-153.html)
- **[Practitioner]** Peloton riders filter the leaderboard to people nearby or in their age group — [Culture Study](https://annehelen.substack.com/p/the-counterintuitive-mechanics-of); Strava provides overall and women's Local Legend boards — [BikeRadar](https://bikeradar.com/news/strava-local-legend)
- **[Practitioner, product]** Strava added a persistence award (most completions in 90 days) because speed boards were out of reach for most riders — [BikeRadar](https://bikeradar.com/news/strava-local-legend); [road.cc](https://road.cc/content/news/strava-launches-local-legends-heres-how-become-one-275471)
- **[Moderate, replication]** Leaderboards shifted quantity more than quality of performance — [Kratochvíl et al. 2025](https://www.muni.cz/en/research/publications/2495997)
- **[Moderate, field]** Self-progress feedback (daily goal and real-time progress) alone raised activity as much as social comparison did — [Zuckerman & Gal-Oz 2014](https://runi.ac.il/media/whrh3e33/deconstructing-gamification.pdf); Peloton users value opportunities to better their own past performance — [Davies et al. 2024](https://trace.tennessee.edu/jasm/vol16/iss1/1)
- **[Strong, field]** Badges increased posting, transacting and commenting in a two-year marketplace field experiment, concentrated among users who watched the badges — [Hamari 2017](https://research.utu.fi/converis/portal/detail/Publication/27336812?lang=en_US); a leaderboard-plus-badges classroom lowered motivation and satisfaction — [Hanus & Fox 2015](https://www.smhp.psych.ucla.edu/pdfdocs/gamil.pdf)
- **[Strong, field]** Tiered public recognition (named giving circles) raised giving — [Karlan & McConnell 2014](https://nber.org/papers/w17737)
- **[Strong, field]** Single-prize lotteries roughly doubled household contribution rates relative to plain asks (17.1% vs 9.5%) and raised more gross revenue — [Landry et al. 2006 (NBER)](https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w11611/w11611.pdf); [re-analysis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.02276); an all-pay auction in a Dutch door-to-door study drew substantially lower participation than lotteries — [Tinbergen paper](https://papers.tinbergen.nl/11070.pdf)
- **[Strong, meta-analysis]** Competition combined with collaboration was particularly effective for behavioural outcomes in learning gamification — [Sailer & Homner 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-019-09498-w)

### Inferences
- Run the campaign as one "season" with weekly mini-boards (weekly top light, weekly most plays) feeding the overall boards: the weekly cycle gives latecomers a fair start, the overall board keeps the end-of-campaign prize meaningful.
- Keep "most plays" (effort) and "best single light" (performance) as separate honours with separate titles (e.g., "Local Legend"-style naming for the plays champion), since effort boards include far more people and respond more to ranking.
- Add a collaborative layer (class or year-group totals toward the raised-so-far ring) because competition plus collaboration is the best-supported configuration and it gives low-ranked individuals a team outcome to care about.
- Spot prizes should be raffle-style (each play is an entry) rather than only rank-based, to raise participation among those who will never top a board; an "all-pay" framing (only the top wins) has the weakest participation evidence.
- Personal-best feedback ("your best light: 4.8 — beat it?") is cheap and as effective as social comparison in the one field test that separated them; show it on every result screen.
- Ties: no evidence; the common convention (earliest to reach the score ranks higher, or shared rank with the next rank skipped) is a product decision, and publishing the rule up front avoids disputes among students.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed test of reset cadence or season length; the strongest numbers are vendor claims.
- No evidence on tie-breaking rules.
- No evidence on badges or titles specifically for secondary-school pupils in a charity context.
