# Colour, visual design, typography and readability: effects on engagement, trust, attention and readability (websites, apps, casual games)

Scope note for the report writer. These notes cover the six key questions assigned. Evidence is graded inline as **[Strong]** (replicated, large-sample or multi-study peer-reviewed / eye-tracking work with numbers), **[Moderate]** (a single peer-reviewed study or an NN/g study with reported numbers), **[Weak]** (small sample, second-hand summary, or unverified metrics) and **[Practitioner]** (platform guidance or designer opinion without published data). Method caveat: the research environment could not open most web pages directly (only GitHub-hosted documents were fetchable, and the web-search budget ran out part-way), so most academic and NN/g findings below come from search-result page summaries of the primary sources rather than full-text reads; where a number could not be confirmed against the primary text this is flagged. Primary W3C WCAG "Understanding" documents, Google's Material motion/typography docs, Apple's Human Interface Guidelines (via a GitHub mirror) and Google's web.dev motion page were read in full. Audience context (a small Irish charity arcade site used by secondary-school students, teachers and parents, mostly on phones) is reflected in the Inferences sections only; the Cited Findings report the general evidence as found.

## Key question 1: Colour and emotion/arousal (red/orange urgency, green go/success, gold reward, purple prestige), colour consistency and brand recognition

### Takeaway
The best-controlled evidence says colour effects on feelings and behaviour are real but small, fragile and context-dependent: red reliably raises perceived arousal, but whether that arousal reads as "exciting" or "threatening" depends on the setting; the marketing associations red-excitement and blue-competence/trust are the best-supported, while gold-reward and purple-prestige rest on convention rather than studies, and the widely quoted "colour increases brand recognition by 80%" statistic has no verifiable primary source.

### Cited Findings
- **Colour-in-context theory (Elliot & Maier, 2012)** holds that colour meanings come partly from repeated pairings with concepts and experiences and partly from biologically rooted responses that learning then shapes; the same colour means different things in different contexts, e.g. in achievement settings red links to danger/caution and triggers avoidance, whereas in romantic settings it links to attraction and triggers approach. [Strong as a framework; see critiques below] — [Elliot 2015 review (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383146/); [Buechner & Maier 2016, PeerJ](https://peerj.com/articles/2515)
- **Red raises arousal independent of context; valence is context-dependent.** Buechner & Maier (2016) used a 2 (red vs blue) x 2 (test-relevant vs neutral pictures) between-subjects design; red increased perceived arousal of the pictures irrespective of their valence, while for test-relevant pictures red also increased perceived negativity. The paper's title summarises it as "direct effects of red on arousal but context-dependent moderations on valence". Sample size could not be confirmed from the summaries. [Moderate — one lab study] — [PeerJ 4:e2515](https://peerj.com/articles/2515); [PDF](https://peerj.com/articles/2515.pdf); [PMC copy](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045874/)
- **The competing "arousal theory"** holds that longer wavelengths such as red are stimulating in themselves; one study reported more hand tremor under red than green. [Weak — older physiological work] — [PMC5045874](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5045874/)
- **The field's own reviewer calls it immature.** Elliot's 2015 mini-review in Frontiers in Psychology concludes the literature on colour and psychological functioning is "at a nascent stage of development", notes that much earlier work was poorly controlled (it was often impossible to tell which combination of hue, lightness and chroma was manipulated, so confounds were likely), and recommends "patience and prudence" regarding theory, findings and application. A separate critique says colour-in-context theory only vaguely suggests testable hypotheses and the field needs mid-level frameworks. [Strong — expert review] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2015](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/full); [PDF](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2015.00368/pdf); [PMC4383146 critique](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4383146/)
- **Replication failures for "red impairs performance".** A Collabra paper, "Processing the Word Red and Intellectual Performance: Four Replication Attempts", directly replicated Lichtenfeld, Maier, Elliot & Pekrun (2009) in Experiments 1-2 and reports that the original studies gave imprecise effect estimates ranging from large to near zero, with near zero now seeming more likely. Elliot's 2019 "historically based review" (Review of General Psychology) responds that only a precise combination of luminance, chroma and hue should be expected to produce intellectual impairment. [Strong signal that red-performance effects are fragile] — [Collabra replication](https://online.ucpress.edu/collabra/article/6/1/3/113047/Processing-the-Word-Red-and-Intellectual); [Elliot 2019 review](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1037/gpr0000170)
- **Marketing: "Exciting red and competent blue" (Labrecque & Milne, Journal of the Academy of Marketing Science 40:711-727, 2012; online 2011).** Four studies: Study 1 maps hues onto brand-personality dimensions; Study 2 shows saturation and value amplify personality traits; Study 3 shows colour can strategically alter brand personality and purchase intent; Study 4 shows colour influences brand likability and familiarity. Red is linked to excitement and blue to competence. In the paper's own conclusion sophistication is paired with black; the popular "purple = sophistication" reading comes from secondary retellings (one 2011 summary lists purple, pink and black as most sophisticated). A fact-check notes that a famous quotation circulating online is not in the paper. The paper's conclusion is that colour helps when it creates a match between logo colour and the intended personality dimension; a 2026 secondary summary adds that consistency between brand personality and colour predicted preference better than colour alone. [Moderate — four-study peer-reviewed paper, read via abstracts/secondary] — [Springer record](https://colab.ws/articles/10.1007/s11747-010-0245-y); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/251277565_Exciting_red_and_competent_blue_the_importance_of_color_in_marketing); [SCIRP citation](https://www.scirp.org/reference/referencespapers?referenceid=1783142); [insights4print fact-check](https://www.insights4print.ceo/2019/02/color-increases-brand-recognition-by-80-the-real-contents-of-the-loyola-study-revealed/); [11 London summary](https://www.11-london.com/post/colour-and-branding); [deepmarketing 2026 summary](https://www.deepmarketing.it/en/blog/color-science-marketing-evidence-2026)
- A 2005 thesis on ad background colour found the brand advertised on a red background was rated more sophisticated and more exciting than on blue, using Aaker's five brand-personality dimensions. [Weak — unpublished thesis] — [thesis PDF](https://libraryarchives.metro.net/DPGTL/dissertationstheses/2005-impact-ad-background-color.pdf)
- **Colour-emotion associations are largely universal.** Jonauskaite et al. (2020, Psychological Science, "Universal Patterns in Color-Emotion Associations Are Further Shaped by Linguistic and Geographic Proximity") had 4,598 participants from 30 nations speaking 22 native languages associate 20 emotion concepts with 12 colour terms; a pattern-similarity analysis found an average similarity coefficient of r = .88 across groups, with nation still predicting associations beyond the shared pattern and linguistically/geographically closer groups being more similar. The authors suggest design as an application area. Note: the study measures associations, not behaviour. [Strong — large multinational sample] — [PDF (BI repository)](https://biopen.bi.no/bi-xmlui/bitstream/11250/2761614/1/Jonauskaite_etal_2020_PsychScience.pdf); [DOI record](https://isidore.science/document/10.1177/0956797620948810)
- **Colour and trust.** Broeder & Snijder (2019) tested blue and online trust and hypothesised a stronger effect for people from more risk-averse cultures; they found no evidence for the hypothesised moderating effect of culture. Alberts & van der Geest (2011) published "Color Matters: Color as Trustworthiness Cue in Web sites" in Technical Communication, and Cyr (2011) published "Website Design and Trust Across Cultures" (Global Usability, pp. 39-55); only bibliographic details were retrievable for these two. A university communications blog notes colour associations vary from culture to culture. [Weak-to-moderate; primary texts not read] — [Broeder & Snijder 2019 PDF](https://econ.ubbcluj.ro/mid/journal/papers/2-1/Broeder%20and%20Snijder%20-%20MID%202019%202%281%29.pdf); [Alberts & van der Geest 2011 listing](https://makinggood.ac.nz/a/alberts,-w-a,-van-der-geest,-t-m-%282011,-may%29/); [Cyr 2011 dblp record](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/series/hci/Cyr11.html); [St Andrews blog](https://digitalcommunications.wp.st-andrews.ac.uk/2014/06/19/what-the-use-of-colour-tells-us-about-trust/)
- **"Colour increases brand recognition by 80%" is unsupported.** The figure is attributed variously to "the University of Loyola" or "Loyola University, Maryland"; one review says the original study is almost impossible to find in any academic database and that sample size, method and year are missing from nearly every citation; a 2019 trade article set out to reveal "the real contents of the Loyola study"; current aggregators simply cite other aggregators. A separate Reboot survey claimed 78% of respondents recalled a logo's primary colour versus 43% its name, but this is an unsourced marketing survey. [Weak/unsupported] — [reachlink analysis](https://www.reachlink.com/es/consejos/cuidadores-familiares/en-que-se-equivoca-realmente-el-marketing-basado-en-la-psicologia-del-color/); [insights4print 2019](https://www.insights4print.ceo/2019/02/color-increases-brand-recognition-by-80-the-real-contents-of-the-loyola-study-revealed/); [review42 aggregator](https://resources.review42.com/color-psychology-facts/); [Jewellery Focus (Reboot survey)](https://www.jewelleryfocus.co.uk/?p=21345); [LogoLounge](https://www.logolounge.com/news/lendingcolortosuccess)
- **Colourfulness matters less than complexity for first impressions.** Reinecke et al. (CHI 2013) report that colourfulness plays a role in visual appeal "but is not nearly as important as the overall visual complexity of a site", and that age, gender and education level all influence first impressions (details under Key question 2). [Strong] — [Reinecke et al. 2013 PDF](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)
- **Accessibility constraints on colour meaning (primary source).** WCAG 1.4.1 requires that colour is never the only way to convey information ("required fields are red", "error is shown in red"); a hue difference that also has a 3:1 lightness difference counts as an additional cue, but where users must perceive a particular colour (e.g. green outline = valid, red = invalid) an extra indicator is required regardless. WCAG explicitly says this "should not in any way discourage the use of color" when complemented by other cues. [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 1.4.1 Use of Color](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html) (read from [w3c/wcag source](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/20/use-of-color.html))
- **Hue barely affects legibility; red on black is a special hazard.** WCAG's contrast rationale states that for people with colour-vision deficiency "hue and saturation have minimal or no effect on legibility", so contrast is computed on luminance; it adds an advisory to avoid predominantly long-wavelength colours (red) on dark backgrounds because of protanopia. WCAG's flash criterion notes people are "even more sensitive to red flashing than to other colors". [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [Understanding SC 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)
- Two recent papers exist whose titles are directly relevant but whose contents could not be read: "The Influence of Color Stimuli on Adolescents' Emotion Playing Mobile Games" (arXiv, 2024) and "Effects of environmental colours in virtual reality: physiological arousal affected by lightness and hue" (Royal Society Open Science, 2023). [Unread] — [arXiv 2402.19064](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.19064); [RSOS 2023](https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rsos/article/10/10/230432/92153/Effects-of-environmental-colours-in-virtual)

### Inferences
- Red/orange for urgency is defensible on the arousal evidence, but the valence finding means a red element on an evaluative screen (a quiz, a score, a leaderboard demotion zone) is more likely to be read as threat than excitement; keep red for genuinely time-limited or error states and use warm but non-red accents (orange/yellow) for "exciting" prize moments.
- Because colour-emotion associations are highly similar across nations (r = .88), Irish teenagers, parents and teachers can be assumed to share the standard Western mappings used in app conventions (green = success/go, red = stop/error, blue = calm/trust), so the safest design move is to follow those conventions rather than invent new colour meanings.
- Gold for rewards and purple for prestige have no retrieved evidence either way; they are safe as conventions (they do no documented harm) but should not be expected to move behaviour by themselves.
- The Labrecque & Milne "fit" finding suggests a single consistently applied palette that matches the intended personality (e.g. playful but trustworthy) is more valuable than picking any specific "winning" hue; brand-recognition claims should be made without the 80% figure.
- Never encode leaderboard zones, correct/incorrect answers or prize eligibility by colour alone; add icons, labels or position cues (WCAG 1.4.1), which also helps colour-blind students (not quantified in these sources).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed evidence was found on gold-as-reward or purple-as-prestige colour effects in interfaces; the search budget ran out before targeted queries could be run.
- The specific hue-emotion pairs reported by Jonauskaite et al. 2020 (e.g. which colour is most associated with joy, anger, love) could not be retrieved; only the overall similarity statistics were available.
- Cyr, Head & Larios (2010, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies), the most-cited study on colour appeal and trust across cultures, could not be located or read; only Cyr's 2011 chapter was found as a bibliographic record.
- Gnambs, Appel & Batinic (2010) on red in web-based knowledge tests, and the often-quoted HubSpot/Performable "red button beat green by 21%" A/B test, could not be verified (no search budget left); treat both as unverified until checked.
- The sample size of Buechner & Maier 2016 was not retrievable (one snippet mentioned 45 participants but appeared to belong to a different study).
- No primary experiment on brand-colour consistency and recognition memory was found.

## Key question 2: Colourful, flat, "Duolingo-style" interfaces versus plain ones — engagement, perceived childishness or professionalism across audiences (teenagers, parents, teachers)

### Takeaway
Evidence consistently shows that low visual complexity and familiar ("prototypical") layouts win first impressions within tens of milliseconds and that attractive designs are perceived as more usable, but that "flat" styling which removes clickability cues measurably slows people down; colourful illustration itself is not shown to hurt, and Duolingo's published rationale for its bright, rounded style is clarity and production speed rather than tested engagement gains. Direct evidence on teenagers, parents or teachers perceiving bright styles as childish is thin: NN/g's teen research documents impatience and lower reading levels, and only secondary summaries of NN/g advise avoiding a childish tone.

### Cited Findings
- **First impressions form in 50 ms and halo onto usability and credibility.** Lindgaard, Fernandes, Dudek & Brown (2006, Behaviour & Information Technology 25:115-126) ran three studies with very short exposures; ratings given after 50 ms "tallied well" with ratings after longer scrutiny, and coverage links the emotional first impression to later judgements of usability and credibility. A later Tractinsky-group replication reports that "averaged over users, immediate aesthetic impressions of web pages are remarkably consistent". The "50 ms" slogan is a press simplification: the study measured appeal, not usability or purchasing. [Strong — older classic (2006), replicated] — [citation page](https://makinggood.ac.nz/l/lindgaard,-g,-fernandes,-g,-dudek,-c,-brown,-j-%282006%29-attention-web-designers-you-have-50-milliseconds-to-make-a-good-first-impression!-behaviour-it,-25,-115–126); [bit-tech coverage](https://www.bit-tech.net/news/website_hot_or_not/1/); [Tractinsky replication listing](https://www.bgu.ac.il/researcher/noam-tractinsky/publications/349269701/)
- **Simple + familiar beats complex even when familiar.** Tuch, Presslaber, Stöcklin, Opwis & Bargas-Avila (2012, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 70(11):794-811) had participants rate 119 real-website screenshots; study 1 varied exposure between subjects (50 / 500 / 1000 ms) and found visual complexity (VC) and prototypicality (PT) affect aesthetic ratings within the first 50 ms; study 2 (17 / 33 / 50 ms) found effects even at 17 ms, with VC's effect stronger than PT's at the shortest exposures and PT catching up as exposure lengthens (VC is processed at an earlier stage). Websites with low VC and high PT were perceived as most appealing; Google's summary: "if the visual complexity of a website is high, users perceive it as less beautiful, even if the design is familiar." [Strong] — [Google Research record](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/); [paper PDF](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/38315.pdf); [Google Research blog 2012](https://research.google/blog/users-love-simple-and-familiar-designs-why-websites-need-to-make-a-great-first-impression/)
- **Colourfulness and complexity predict appeal; demographics modulate it.** Reinecke, Yeh, Miratrix, Gajos et al. (CHI 2013, pp. 2049-2058) collected ratings of colourfulness, visual complexity and visual appeal for 450 websites (548 volunteers are reported in the summary) and built computational models of perceived complexity and colourfulness; together with demographics (education, age) the models explain nearly half the variance in appeal ratings given after 500 ms exposure. Complexity carries more weight than colourfulness, and "age, gender, and education level all influence their first impressions of websites". The abstract states the first impression "is influential enough to later affect their opinions of a site's usability and trustworthiness." [Strong] — [Harvard PDF](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf); [UW PDF](https://wildlab.cs.washington.edu/Publications_files/Reinecke_CHI2013.pdf); [Harvard record](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml)
- **Complexity and order drive affect, which drives approach behaviour.** Deng & Poole (2010, MIS Quarterly 34(4):711-730, "Affect in Web Interfaces") built a model from environmental psychology, HCI and marketing and tested it in a lab experiment with four complexity levels (number of links, graphics and text blocks on e-commerce home pages); initial pleasantness and arousal responses to a page's complexity and order carry over to subsequent approach behaviour, and the salience of these features depends on users' metamotivational state. A later review summarises it as "greater complexity combined with a highly structured or ordered spatial arrangement increases viewer preferences"; a 2012 Electronic Commerce Research and Applications study found significant effects of complexity and order on preference, moderated by shopping motivation. [Moderate] — [MISQ record](https://misq.org/affect-in-web-interfaces-a-study-of-the-impacts-of-web-page-visual-complexity-and-order.html); [appendices PDF](https://misq.umn.edu/skin/frontend/default/misq/pdf/appendices/2010/DengPooleAppendices.pdf); [2012 ECRA follow-up](https://datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/1567-4223/volumes-and-issues/49/paper-detail/77226)
- **Flat UI with weak signifiers costs time and attention (NN/g, 2017).** NN/g recruited 71 general web users; each saw one version of each of nine sites in a between-subjects design comparing strong versus weak clickability signifiers (weak versions used reduced contrast and/or removed containment). Participants spent 22% more time looking at the weak-signifier pages and made 25% more fixations on them; because tasks were targeted findability tasks, "more time and effort spent looking around the page are not good" (NN/g explicitly rejects the "more engagement" reading). Critics note the study compares a poorly executed flat design with a better non-flat one, so execution quality may drive the result. [Moderate — eye-tracking with n = 71; design critique noted] — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/offbeat/2017/09/05/its-official-users-navigate-flat-ui-designs-22-per-cent-slower/590397); [HTMLGoodies](https://www.htmlgoodies.com/news/study-flat-ui-leads-to-22-slower-navigation/); [critique thread](https://hardforum.com/threads/users-navigate-flat-ui-designs-22-percent-slower.1943274/); [GIGAZINE summary](https://www.gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20170907-flat-ui-elements-attract)
- **Flat icons take about twice as long to find (Burmistrov et al., INTERACT 2015, LNCS 9297:106-114).** With 20 university students already familiar with flat design, visual search for a target word in flat text was associated with higher cognitive load than traditional styling; search for flat icons took about twice as long as for realistic icons with higher cognitive load; identifying clickable objects on flat web pages required more time and produced a significantly greater number of errors. The authors' slide deck reports at least one non-significant time comparison, so the time results are mixed; they recommend replacing flat UIs with interfaces based on decades of HCI principles. [Weak-to-moderate — n = 20] — [paper PDF](https://www.flatisbad.com/publications/Burmistrov-INTERACT15.pdf); [IFIP record](https://dl.ifip.org/IFIP-LNCS-9297/hal-01599895); [slides](https://www.slideshare.net/IvanBurmistrov/flat-design-vs-rich-design-experimental-comparison)
- **Aesthetic-usability effect.** Kurosu & Kashimura (Hitachi, 1995) had 252 participants rate 26 ATM interface variants and found a stronger correlation between aesthetic ratings and perceived ease of use than between aesthetics and actual ease of use (r = 0.59 reported for the Japanese data). Tractinsky (CHI 1997) replicated in Israel with "very high correlations between perceived aesthetics of the interface and a priori perceived ease of use"; Tractinsky, Katz & Ikar (2000) extended it ("What is beautiful is usable"). A Journal of Usability Studies review cautions that only a handful of experiments manipulated aesthetics and usability independently, so "the causal relationship between aesthetics and perceived usability has not been firmly established." NN/g's own article (Kate Moran, "The Aesthetic-Usability Effect", c. 2017) defines it as users' tendency to perceive attractive products as more usable, "even if they aren't actually more effective or efficient". [Strong for perceived usability; weak for actual performance] — [Laws of UX summary](https://lawsofux.com/aesthetic-usability-effect/index.md); [Tractinsky CHI 97](https://chi1997.acm.org/proceedings/paper/nt.html); [Tractinsky 1997 PDF](https://static.aminer.org/pdf/PDF/000/090/567/aesthetics_and_apparent_usability_empirically_assessing_cultural_and_methodological_issues.pdf); [Tractinsky et al. 2000 PDF](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1645430/mod_folder/content/0/Tractinsky%20%282000%29.pdf); [JUX boundary-conditions review](https://uxpajournal.org/?p=4392); [NN/g 2017 UX quiz referencing the article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-quiz-2017/); [UXPin summary](https://www.uxpin.com/studio/blog/aesthetic-usability-effect)
- **Duolingo's published rationale for its art style.** Duolingo's design site says every illustration is built from three rounded primitives (rounded rectangle, circle, rounded triangle) and shadows are pill shapes. Its blog explains that early designs used static, hard-edged shapes on light grey; in 2018 the art team began experimenting with "brighter, rounder, friendlier illustrations appropriate for a younger audience" for the separate Duolingo KIDS app, and this style then moved into the main app alongside a 2018 UI redesign "characterized by vibrant colors and rounded buttons on a white background". Duolingo describes the result as "a minimalistic, playful aesthetic that is quick to produce, clear to understand, and fun to learn with", and notes the simple style was partly chosen because few early designers could illustrate. No engagement metrics for the style are published. [Practitioner — first-party rationale, no data] — [Duolingo design: illustration](https://design.duolingo.com/illustration); [Duolingo blog: Shape language](https://blog.duolingo.com/shape-language-duolingos-art-style/); [Duolingo blog: Reshaping Duo](https://blog.duolingo.com/reshaping-duo/)
- **Duolingo on celebration design.** Its streak-milestone post describes a shareable card with the owl and fire imagery and says the update "proved successful on both measurable and hard-to-measure fronts", without numbers. [Practitioner — unquantified] — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/streak-milestone-design-animation)
- **Third-party reads of Duolingo's look.** A designer's critique argues some users "may mistakingly interpret this design as childlike, patronizing and well... annoying"; a separate student redesign found its test users liked a brighter palette but found the overall style "school-bookish"; an ASO analysis notes Duolingo's store screenshots combine multiple colours more vividly than competitors' solid backgrounds. [Opinion] — [Medium critique](https://medium.com/@caitlin.mac/duolingo-reimagined-25e1f1a80d2); [Medium case study](https://medium.com/@mathishanspach/ux-ui-case-study-redesigning-duolingo-e30316097a9a); [Geeklab ASO breakdown](https://geeklab.app/posts/aso-breakdown-what-duolingo-is-doing-different-from-its-top-competitors/)
- **Teenagers (NN/g, "Teenager's UX: Designing for Teens", Kendrick & Nielsen, 17 March 2019).** Teens are (over)confident in their web abilities but perform worse than adults in usability studies; "lower reading levels, impatience, and undeveloped research skills reduce teens' task success". NN/g's first teen study (2005) tested 38 users aged 13-17 and found a 55% task-success rate versus 66% for adults; the 2019 edition reports many of the same bad habits but improved performance, attributed partly to better design, and has 130 guidelines (the current report page lists 124 guidelines in 312 pages). NN/g's Raluca Budiu says teens are more likely than adults to lose track of their goal and get lost on tempting irrelevant paths. Secondary summaries of NN/g's teen guidance advise avoiding "a condescending or childish tone", prioritising speed, and designing mobile/touch-first because teens are more likely to use a phone than a computer; the word "childish" could not be confirmed in NN/g's own text. [Strong for task performance; weak for the "childish" guidance] — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/); [NN/g summary post](https://x.com/NNgroup/status/1108069645276184576); [NN/g teen report](https://www.nngroup.com/reports/teenagers-on-the-web/); [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/teen-users-vs-other-age-groups/); [secondary summary (gammaux)](https://www.gammaux.com/en/?p=2387); [secondary summary (erikrunyon)](https://erikrunyon.com/page4/)
- **Young adults prefer big images, faces and little text.** Djamasbi, Siegel & Tullis (2010, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 68:307-323) combined an online survey with an eye-tracking study of the most- and least-liked pages and concluded Generation Y (defined as ages 18-31) "may prefer pages that include a main large image, images of celebrities, little text, and a search feature"; their 2011 follow-up found Gen Y and baby boomers report similar aesthetic preferences (both prefer images and little text) but Gen Y "will be more likely than baby boomers to miss key information if a Web page fails to present that information using a limited number of clear focal points that are located above the fold". [Moderate — small lab samples] — [2010 paper PDF](https://users.wpi.edu/~djamasbi/PDFS/2010%20djamasbi%20et%20al-%20IJHCS%20%28journal%20version%29.pdf); [2011 IJEC paper](https://www.ijec-web.org/past-issues/volume-15-number-4-summer-2011/online-viewing-and-aesthetic-preferences-of-generation-y-and-the-baby-boom-generation-testing-user-web-site-experience-through-eye-tracking/); [AMCIS 2008 version](https://aisel.aisnet.org/amcis2008/77)
- **Industry direction.** A 2025 design essay frames Google's "Material 3 Expressive" as "building on the failures of flat design", i.e. the major platform vendor is moving away from strict flatness. [Opinion] — [uxdesign.cc](https://uxdesign.cc/material-3-expressive-building-on-the-failures-of-flat-design-d7a9bb627298)

### Inferences
- The evidence favours a "colourful but simple" position: bright colour and friendly illustration are compatible with low visual complexity (Duolingo's three-shape system is itself a complexity-reduction device), while what demonstrably hurts is (a) cluttered layouts and (b) flat controls that no longer look tappable. For a phone-first arcade, buttons should keep clear containment/contrast signifiers even if the illustration style is flat.
- Because first impressions in under a second halo onto perceived usability and trust (Lindgaard; Reinecke; aesthetic-usability effect), a polished, uncluttered landing page is probably the cheapest trust lever available to a small charity, more so than any specific hue.
- For the three audiences: teens need fast, scannable, low-reading-load screens (NN/g) and probably tolerate playful styling as long as the tone is not patronising; parents and teachers are older (Reinecke found age changes appeal judgements, direction not retrievable) and NN/g's dark-mode and contrast work (Key question 3) suggests prioritising legibility for them. Nothing retrieved shows that a bright palette reduces perceived professionalism for teachers; this remains an assumption to test with a handful of teachers.
- Duolingo's style is a documented production-efficiency choice as much as an engagement choice; a small team can borrow the "few primitives, one palette, white background" discipline without needing Duolingo's experiment data.

### Gaps
- No study was found that measures teachers' or parents' perceptions of "childishness" versus "professionalism" of colourful, gamified interfaces; this is the largest evidence gap for the arcade's audience.
- Reinecke & Gajos (CHI 2014, "Quantifying visual preferences around the world", ~40,000 participants) reportedly found older users and some countries prefer less colourful, simpler sites, and women prefer more colourful sites than men; this could not be verified before the search budget ran out and should be checked before citing.
- Sonderegger & Sauer (2010, Applied Ergonomics) on attractive versus unattractive mobile-phone prototypes and task performance, and Tuch et al. (2012, Computers in Human Behavior, "Is beautiful really usable?") on the direction of the aesthetics-usability link, were not retrievable.
- NN/g's "Children's UX" (2019) guidance on bright colours, animation and sound for younger children, which would help separate "child" from "teen" styling, was not retrieved.
- Duolingo has published no A/B results on its visual style specifically; its claimed metrics for other features are unverified (see Key question 6).

## Key question 3: Readability — line length, mobile font size, contrast ratios, short versus long copy, and how people actually read on screens (F-pattern, scanning, share of words read)

### Takeaway
Decades of eye-tracking and reading research agree that people scan rather than read on screens (NN/g: 79% scanned, ~16% read word by word; on an average page users read at most 28% and more realistically ~20% of the words), that concise, scannable, objective copy improves measured usability dramatically (+124% in NN/g's 1997 study), that comprehension of difficult text halves on phone-sized screens, and that practical readability floors are well defined: roughly 50-75 characters per line (hard limit 80), 16-17 px/pt body text on phones, 4.5:1 luminance contrast for body text (3:1 for large text and UI components), and 24-48 px touch targets.

### Cited Findings
- **People scan.** NN/g eye-tracking across three large studies spanning 13 years found 79% of test users scanned any new page they came across and only 16% read word by word. [Strong — repeated eye-tracking, older classic originating in 1997] — [UX Planet summary of NN/g research](https://uxplanet.org/people-dont-read-online-they-scan-this-is-how-to-write-for-them-80a75069c14e); [mirror of "How people read on the web: the eyetracking evidence"](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/how-people-read-on-the-web-the-eyetracking-evidence)
- **Concise, scannable, objective copy: +124% usability (Morkes & Nielsen, 1997).** Five versions of the same Nebraska tourism site were tested; relative to the promotional control, the concise version scored 58% higher on measured usability, the scannable version 47% higher, the objective version 27% higher, and the version combining all three 124% higher. The study was small and the gains are relative to the control. [Moderate — older classic (1997), small sample] — [Alertbox, 1 Oct 1997 (mirror)](https://paginaspersonales.deusto.es/abaitua/konzeptu/sgml/9710a.html); [article mirror](https://deceptive.design/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web); [NN/g replication files](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/downloadable-files-to-replicate-web-reading-study/)
- **"How Little Do Users Read?" (Nielsen, May 2008).** Using Weinreich et al.'s "Not Quite the Average" browsing dataset (about 45,000 page views per the summary; Nielsen notes it was not eye-tracking) with an average of 593 words per page view, Nielsen estimated users had time to read at most 28% of the words if they spent the whole visit reading, and that "more realistically, users will read about 20% of the text on the average page". A critic argues the analysis lumps together fact-finding visits with full reads. [Moderate — inference from timing data] — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-little-do-users-read/); [original Alertbox PDF](https://faculty.washington.edu/farkas/TC510-Fall2011/NielsenHowLittleDoUsersReadDF.pdf); [critique](https://www.rightreading.com/blog/?p=605)
- **F-shaped pattern (2006) and its 2017 revisit.** NN/g identified the F-pattern in 2006 and revisited it on 12 November 2017 ("F-Shaped Pattern of Reading on the Web: Misunderstood, But Still Relevant (Even on Mobile)"). The 2016-2017 eye-tracking studies behind the revisit ran in Raleigh (46 participants) and San Francisco (105 participants). Key points: the first lines of text receive more gazes than later lines and the first few words on the left of each line receive more fixations than later words; scanning "does not always take the shape of an F"; the pattern persists on phones; and "good design can prevent F-shape scanning". NN/g lists four text-scanning patterns ordered from least to most effective: F-pattern, spotted, layer-cake and commitment; the layer-cake pattern is users scanning only headings/subheadings, the spotted pattern is users picking out specific items "typically a statistic, link, digit, or set of words with a specific format", and a "marking" pattern (eyes stay in one place while scrolling) happens more on mobile. [Strong — repeated eye-tracking] — [NN/g F-pattern revisit](https://nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/); [NN/g text-scanning patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/); [NN/g "How People Read Online" report page](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-people-read-online/); [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/f-pattern-reading-digital-content/); [secondary summary](https://confetti.design/blog/top-4-text-scanning-patterns-to-how-you-read-on-a-website)
- **Numerals stop the scanning eye.** Nielsen's 2007 Alertbox "Show Numbers as Numerals" argues, on the basis of eye-tracking, that web writing should use numerals ("5", not "five") even where print style would not, because scanning users fixate on numerals; a secondary list of eye-tracking lessons repeats "show numbers as numerals". [Moderate — NN/g eye-tracking, read via secondary] — [IntMath summary](https://www.intmath.com/blog/mathematics/writing-numbers-on-the-web-610); [Konigi "23 actionable lessons from eye-tracking studies"](https://mac.konigi.com/blog/23-actionable-lessons-eye-tracking-studies)
- **Mobile halves comprehension of difficult text.** Singh et al. (University of Alberta) ran a Cloze test on the privacy policies of 10 popular sites; reading on an iPhone-sized screen produced comprehension scores only 48% of desktop scores (secondary accounts give raw scores of 39% desktop vs 19% mobile). Nielsen's 2011 write-up ("Mobile Content Is Twice as Difficult") attributes the gap solely to screen size, the only variable that differed. NN/g's 2016 follow-up found a more nuanced picture: short, simple text is understood equally well on phones and computers, but people slow down on difficult text on mobile; NN/g still recommends brevity on mobile. [Moderate] — [NN/g 2011 article](https://nngroup.com/articles/mobile-content-is-twice-as-difficult-2011); [Peachpit summary](https://www.peachpit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=1966021); [Wylie summary incl. 2016 follow-up](https://www.wyliecomm.com/2023/05/reading-on-mobile)
- **Line length: 50-75 characters (Baymard).** Baymard's research article recommends 50-75 characters per line for body text; users have been observed to become fatigued and more likely to skip extremely long lines (100+ characters), long lines "feel intimidating"; it suggests a CSS max-width of about 70ch or 34em adjusted for the typeface. [Practitioner with usability-test observations] — [Baymard line-length article](https://baymard.com/blog/line-length-readability); [Baymard research page](https://baymard.com/research-articles/line-length-readability)
- **Line length experiments.** Dyson & Kipping (1998) found 100-character lines were read faster than 25-character lines with comprehension constant, while 55-character lines were judged easiest to read — participants' judgements did not match their performance. Dyson & Haselgrove (2001, International Journal of Human-Computer Studies 54:585-612) reportedly found a 4-inch line length gave faster reading than 1.7 inches (second-hand). A 2003 Human Factors and Ergonomics Society paper found the narrowest line was perceived as promoting the most concentration and the medium length as optimal. The Team W summary: you read faster with longer lines but prefer shorter ones. [Moderate — older classics (1998-2003)] — [Dyson & Kipping 1998](https://journals.uc.edu/index.php/vl/article/view/5671); [Dyson & Haselgrove 2001 citation (Team W)](https://www.blog.theteamw.com/2009/10/26/100-things-you-should-know-about-people-2-line-length/); [HFES 2003 paper](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/154193120304701112); [Dyson 2004 review PDF](https://stu.westga.edu/~ssynan1/literacy/Dyson.pdf); [HFI newsletter on optimal line length](https://humanfactors.com/newsletters/optimal_line_length.html)
- **WCAG line length and spacing (AAA, primary source).** SC 1.4.8 Visual Presentation requires (as one of its provisions) that lines of text not exceed 80 characters or glyphs (40 for CJK), that line spacing be at least space-and-a-half within paragraphs and paragraph spacing at least 1.5 times line spacing, that text not be fully justified ("rivers of white"), and that text resize to 200% without horizontal scrolling; the rationale is that people with some reading, cognitive or low-vision disabilities lose their place on long lines. [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 1.4.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/visual-presentation.html) (read from [w3c/wcag source](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/20/visual-presentation.html))
- **Print size and reading speed (Legge & Bigelow, 2011, Journal of Vision).** A review of vision science and typography: reading speed increases with print size up to a "critical print size" and then plateaus at maximum reading speed; the "fluent" range spans a factor of 10 in angular x-height from roughly 0.2 to 2 degrees, equivalent to about 4 to 40 points at a 40 cm reading distance; the authors conclude human visual processing, more than economics or art, constrains the print sizes in common use. [Strong — review] — [PMC3428264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3428264); [UMN PDF](https://legge.dl8.umn.edu/sites/legge.psych.umn.edu/files/2020-08/Legge%20Bigelow%202011%20Does%20size%20matter%20for%20reading%3F%20A%20review%20of%20findings%20from%20vision%20science%20and%20typography.pdf)
- **Contrast ratios and why (WCAG, primary source).** SC 1.4.3 requires 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text, where large text is 18 pt or 14 pt bold (about 24 px and 18.5 px CSS). The rationale: 3:1 is the minimum recommended by ISO 9241-3 and ANSI/HFES 100-1988 for standard text and normal vision; 20/40 acuity is associated with a contrast-sensitivity loss of roughly 1.5, so 3 x 1.5 = 4.5:1, and 20/80 acuity implies about 7:1 (the AAA level, SC 1.4.6). Thresholds are not rounded (4.499:1 fails). Thin or unusual fonts can render fainter than their CSS colour, so WCAG encourages thicker fonts or exceeding the ratios. Logos are exempt but brand-guideline low contrast is not. Non-text UI components and meaningful graphics need 3:1 (SC 1.4.11), including the visual cues that show a control exists and its state. Text must be resizable to 200% (SC 1.4.4). [Strong — standard with cited empirical basis] — [Understanding SC 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [Understanding SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html); [Understanding SC 1.4.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/resize-text.html); [W3C WCAG 2.0 Understanding (contrast)](https://www.w3.org/TR/2016/NOTE-UNDERSTANDING-WCAG20-20161007/visual-audio-contrast-contrast.html)
- **Low-contrast text is "illegible, undiscoverable, and inaccessible" (NN/g, Katie Sherwin).** NN/g's piece and companion video argue against the low-contrast grey-text trend and recommend alternatives; a display-industry follow-up found the sites NN/g criticised had only partly reconsidered. [Practitioner — NN/g expert review] — [NN/g video page](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/alternatives-low-contrast-text/); [Display Daily follow-up](https://displaydaily.com/reprising-low-contrast-text-web-pages/)
- **Light mode beats dark mode for normal vision (NN/g, Raluca Budiu, Feb 2020, reviewing Piepenbrock et al.).** Piepenbrock and colleagues (Düsseldorf) tested young adults (18-33) and older adults (60-85) with normal or corrected vision; positive polarity (dark text on light background) performed better on visual-acuity and proofreading tasks regardless of age; a companion Human Factors study found the positive-polarity advantage increased linearly as font size decreased; participants reported no perceived difference even though they performed better in light mode. Dark mode may help some low-vision users, particularly with cataracts. [Strong — peer-reviewed experiments summarised by NN/g; read via mirrors] — [mirror of NN/g article summary](https://gi-radar.de/tl/DC-0313); [Slashdot summary](https://hardware.slashdot.org/story/20/02/16/2041240/dark-mode-vs-light-mode-which-is-better); [FH St. Pölten discussion](https://mfg.fhstp.ac.at/?p=12447)
- **Apple Human Interface Guidelines: type sizes (primary, read via a GitHub mirror of the HIG).** Default/minimum text sizes: iOS and iPadOS 17 pt default, 11 pt minimum; macOS 13/10 pt; tvOS 29/23 pt; visionOS 17/12 pt; watchOS 16/12 pt. The default ("Large") Dynamic Type body style is 17 pt regular with 22 pt leading; accessibility sizes go up to 53 pt body (AX5). Apple advises: "avoid light font weights" (prefer Regular, Medium, Semibold, Bold; avoid Ultralight, Thin, Light); "minimize the number of typefaces"; test game text for legibility on each platform, and if hard to read "consider using a larger type size, increasing contrast ... or using typefaces designed for optimized legibility"; adding visible background shapes behind text helps; avoid tight leading for three or more lines; when users enlarge text, prioritise the content they care about ("in a game, people are often more interested in a character's dialog than in transient hit-damage values"); support Dynamic Type. [Practitioner — platform guidance] — [Apple HIG: Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography) (text read from the [apple-dev-mcp mirror](https://github.com/tmaasen/apple-dev-mcp)); [secondary summary of 17 pt body](https://pangrampangram.com/blogs/journal/guide-for-designing-better-mobile-apps-typography)
- **Material Design 3 type scale (primary, Google's component docs).** Body Large = Regular 16 sp, Body Medium = 14 sp, Body Small = 12 sp, Label Small = Medium 11 sp, Title Medium = Medium 16 sp, Headline Small = 24 sp, Display Large = 57 sp; an "emphasized" scale (heavier weights) is recommended "for showing selection, actions, headlines" to create hierarchy. [Practitioner — platform guidance] — [material-components-android Typography.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Typography.md); [Material 3 typography overview](https://m3.material.io/styles/typography/overview/)
- **Touch targets.** WCAG 2.2 SC 2.5.8 requires targets of at least 24 x 24 CSS px or equivalent spacing (a 24 px circle centred on each undersized target must not intersect another target), independent of zoom; SC 2.5.5 (AAA) asks for 44 x 44 CSS px and recommends larger targets for frequently used controls, irreversible actions, controls near screen edges and sequential tasks. Material's recommended minimum is 48 x 48 dp (the visible icon can be smaller with padding). [Strong — standards/platform guidance] — [Understanding SC 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html) (source: [w3c/wcag](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/22/target-size-minimum.html)); [Understanding SC 2.5.5](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html); [Compose Material 3 minimum interactive size docs](https://kotlinlang.org/api/compose-multiplatform/material3/androidx.compose.material3/minimum-interactive-component-size.html); [Uxcel course note on 48 dp](https://app.uxcel.com/courses/mobile-design/android-app-design-212/minimum-tap-target-size-9094)
- **Practitioner font-size rules of thumb (no primary data).** A web agency recommends 18-26 px base size on large screens and smaller on phones so lines stay a comfortable length, using 21 px / 16 px itself; a student resource cites Smashing Magazine's 13-18 px for desktop; Medium posts recommend 16 px minimum and 30-50 characters per line on mobile without citing primary studies; a games guide recommends a 16 pt minimum for in-game text and user scaling up to 150-200%, and claims "over 30% of players abandon games because of poor interface design" without a source. [Practitioner/weak] — [KickPoint](https://kickpoint.ca/the-readability-formula-making-your-website-easy-to-read/); [NWTC line-length page](https://toleary.bitweb1.nwtc.edu/wfed2/rd/01-line-length/index.html); [Medium "science-backed" recipe](https://medium.com/design-bootcamp/the-perfect-text-readability-recipe-science-backed-typography-for-better-ux-7c8bf190df85); [Outlook Respawn games UI guide](https://respawn.outlookindia.com/gaming/gaming-guides/ui-and-ux-in-games-building-menus-huds-and-feedback-systems)

### Inferences
- For a phone-first site used by teens with below-adult reading performance (NN/g) and halved comprehension of difficult text on small screens (Singh/Nielsen), copy should be very short, front-loaded (first two words of each line carry the F-pattern weight), broken by headings (layer-cake) and studded with numerals (spotted pattern); rules, prize terms and privacy text should be rewritten in plain short sentences rather than relying on anyone reading long paragraphs.
- Converging floors for the arcade's body text: 16-17 px on phones (Apple 17 pt, Material 16 sp), never below 11-12 px for captions, 4.5:1 contrast on all body text and 3:1 on button fills/outlines and icons, 45-75 characters per line (set max-width around 34-36 em), line-height about 1.5, no fully justified text, light-on-dark avoided for body copy (dark mode is a worse reading surface for normal vision, and the penalty grows as text shrinks).
- Parents and teachers are older than the students; the positive-polarity advantage with smaller fonts and the 4.5:1 rationale (age-related contrast-sensitivity loss) argue for erring larger and higher-contrast than a teen-only product would.
- Game screens that need text to be read (instructions, scores, prize rules) should avoid thin weights and put text on solid shapes (Apple's advice), which also keeps contrast predictable over colourful backgrounds.

### Gaps
- NN/g's 2016 mobile-comprehension study (participant numbers, effect sizes) was only available second-hand; the 2018 NN/g "Scrolling and Attention" figures (share of viewing time above the fold) were not retrieved.
- Dyson & Haselgrove 2001's own comprehension results were not retrievable; the "55 characters" figure traces to Dyson & Kipping 1998, not 2001.
- The exact number of page views in the Weinreich dataset used by Nielsen 2008 differs between sources ("about 45,000" in the summary read here); verify against the NN/g article before quoting.
- Font-legibility comparisons on screen (serif vs sans-serif, specific typefaces) were not searched; no claim is made here.
- No study was found quantifying how short versus long prize-rule or instruction copy changes completion rates in games specifically.

## Key question 4: Visual hierarchy and attention — what draws the eye first (size, contrast, motion, faces, numbers), the effect of a single dominant call to action, and the effect of visual clutter on task completion

### Takeaway
Eye-tracking and experimental work agree that motion, large images and faces, numerals, and high-contrast "clickable-looking" elements capture attention first, that a few clear focal points above the fold prevent young users missing key information, and that visual clutter lowers appeal within 50 ms and raises search time, fixations and errors; direct controlled evidence that a single dominant call to action outperforms several was not found, so that remains a practitioner heuristic supported indirectly by the clutter and focal-point findings.

### Cited Findings
- **Motion is the strongest attention grabber in UI and therefore the most distracting.** NN/g: "Moving elements are a powerful tool to attract users' attention. When designing an animation consider its goal, its frequency of occurrence, and its mechanics"; moving UI elements attract attention, "which often means distracting users from accomplishing their primary goals". NN/g eye-tracking also found users "zone out" ad-like elements including animated ads, looking at them mainly when forced to (e.g. when the ad precedes content). [Moderate — NN/g eye-tracking and expert guidance] — [NN/g "Distracting animations" video (referencing "Animation for Attention and Comprehension")](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/distracting-animations/)
- **Faces, big images and few focal points.** Djamasbi et al. (2010; 2011) found young adults favour a large main image and images of celebrities/people with little text, and that Generation Y is more likely than boomers to miss key information unless it is presented "using a limited number of clear focal points that are located above the fold". [Moderate] — [Djamasbi 2010 PDF](https://users.wpi.edu/~djamasbi/PDFS/2010%20djamasbi%20et%20al-%20IJHCS%20%28journal%20version%29.pdf); [Djamasbi 2011 IJEC](https://www.ijec-web.org/past-issues/volume-15-number-4-summer-2011/online-viewing-and-aesthetic-preferences-of-generation-y-and-the-baby-boom-generation-testing-user-web-site-experience-through-eye-tracking/)
- **Numerals and formatted items are what the "spotted" scanning pattern picks out** — "typically a statistic, link, digit, or set of words with a specific format" — and NN/g's eye-tracking led Nielsen to recommend numerals over spelled-out numbers on the web. [Moderate] — [NN/g text-scanning patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/); [IntMath on "Show Numbers as Numerals"](https://www.intmath.com/blog/mathematics/writing-numbers-on-the-web-610)
- **First lines and left-most words get the fixations** (F-pattern): first lines of text receive more gazes than later lines and the first few words on the left of each line receive more fixations; good design (headings, bullets, bold keywords) can prevent the F-pattern. [Strong] — [NN/g F-pattern revisit](https://nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)
- **Clickability signifiers (contrast, containment) steer attention.** In NN/g's flat-UI experiment, removing contrast/containment from clickable elements produced 25% more fixations and 22% more time on targeted findability tasks, interpreted as uncertainty about what is clickable. [Moderate] — [NN/g flat UI](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/flat-ui-less-attention-cause-uncertainty/)
- **Clutter lowers appeal almost instantly and raises search time/errors.** Visual complexity reduces aesthetic ratings within 17-50 ms (Tuch et al. 2012) and is the dominant predictor of appeal in Reinecke et al. 2013; Burmistrov et al. 2015 found search on flat pages took longer with significantly more errors; Deng & Poole 2010 found that complexity must be paired with order (structured spatial arrangement) to produce positive affect and approach behaviour. [Strong for appeal; moderate for task performance] — [Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/); [Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf); [Burmistrov et al. 2015](https://www.flatisbad.com/publications/Burmistrov-INTERACT15.pdf); [Deng & Poole 2010](https://misq.org/affect-in-web-interfaces-a-study-of-the-impacts-of-web-page-visual-complexity-and-order.html)
- **Size, weight and colour are the sanctioned hierarchy levers.** Apple's HIG: "Adjust font weight, size, and color as needed to emphasize important information and help people visualize hierarchy", and "Mixing too many different typefaces can obscure your information hierarchy"; Material's "emphasized" type scale exists "to create hierarchy and [is] recommended for showing selection, actions, headlines". [Practitioner — platform guidance] — [Apple HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography) (via [mirror](https://github.com/tmaasen/apple-dev-mcp)); [Material Typography.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Typography.md)
- **Control visibility and state must meet 3:1 contrast** (WCAG 1.4.11): the visual information needed to identify that a control is present, how to operate it and its state (selected, focused) must have at least 3:1 contrast with adjacent colours; WCAG also encourages delineating the boundary of all controls "to aid people with cognitive disabilities in the recognition of controls". [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)
- **Automatically moving content distracts, especially people with attention deficits.** WCAG 2.2.2 requires a pause/stop/hide mechanism for any moving, blinking or scrolling content that starts automatically and lasts more than five seconds; "five seconds was chosen because it is long enough to get a user's attention, but not so long that a user cannot wait out the distraction". [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html)
- **Academic review of animation and attention.** A 2022 systematic literature review, "Psychological Impact and Influence of Animation on Viewer's Visual Attention and Cognition", exists (title and abstract only were retrievable). [Unread] — [PMC9453061](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9453061/)

### Inferences
- The documented hierarchy of attention capture (motion > large image/face > high-contrast contained element > numerals/bold > body text) means a prize page or game-start screen can be made to "read" in order by giving exactly one element each of these properties: one animated or large element for the main action, numerals for score/rank, and plain text for the rest.
- A single dominant call to action is not directly tested in anything retrieved, but it follows from three findings: young users miss information when there are not "a limited number of clear focal points" above the fold (Djamasbi 2011), extra elements raise complexity which lowers appeal within 50 ms (Tuch), and uncertain signifiers add fixations and time (NN/g). Treat it as a strong heuristic rather than a measured effect.
- Visual clutter is especially costly for this site's teen users, who NN/g found lose track of goals more than adults; each game card, prize banner and donation prompt competes for the same first-second attention budget.
- Any auto-playing attract-mode animation on an arcade page needs a pause control if it runs longer than five seconds (WCAG 2.2.2) and will pull attention away from the donate/play buttons.

### Gaps
- No controlled study comparing one versus several calls to action was retrieved; the choice-overload meta-analysis (Chernev, Böckenholt & Goodman 2015) and practitioner CTA tests could not be checked before the search budget ran out.
- Classic attention-capture experiments (e.g. Franconeri & Simons 2003 on moving/looming stimuli; Cerf et al. on faces in saliency models) and clutter metrics (Rosenholtz et al. 2007 "feature congestion"; Harper et al. 2009 on web-page visual complexity) were not retrievable; the general claims above rest on the HCI studies cited rather than on these.
- NN/g's banner-blindness and "scrolling and attention" articles (percentage of viewing time above the fold) were not retrieved.
- No eye-tracking study of game lobby/arcade screens specifically was found.

## Key question 5: Animation and motion — when micro-animations raise perceived quality and when they reduce usability; reduced-motion considerations

### Takeaway
Platform owners and NN/g agree on the same operating rules: keep UI animations roughly 100-500 ms (about 100 ms for small feedback, 200-300 ms for larger transitions, longer than 400 ms "may feel too slow"), make motion purposeful and brief, avoid animating frequent interactions, let people cancel it, and honour the system reduce-motion setting because scaling, panning, parallax and spinning effects can trigger vestibular symptoms (web.dev cites that over 35% of adults have experienced vestibular dysfunction by age 40). Evidence that animation raises perceived quality is mostly practitioner claim (Duolingo reports success without numbers), while the evidence that gratuitous motion hurts attention and comfort is strong.

### Cited Findings
- **Durations (NN/g, "Executing UX Animations: Duration and Motion Characteristics").** Most animations should run 100-500 ms depending on complexity and travel distance; pick the shortest duration that does not look jarring; simple feedback such as a checkbox or toggle at about 100 ms "feels immediate" and gives the impression of physically handling the control; larger changes such as a modal appearing suit roughly 200-300 ms; longer travel distances need smoother motion, especially for motion-sensitive users. [Practitioner — NN/g guidance] — [NN/g animation duration](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/animation-duration/)
- **Material Design durations and easing (primary).** The Material 3 motion system defines 16 duration tokens from 50 ms (short1) through 100/150/200 ms (short), 250-400 ms (medium), 450-600 ms (long) to 700-1000 ms (extra-long), with the rule "duration should increase as the area/traversal of an animation increases"; container transforms default to 300 ms incoming / 250 ms outgoing with "standard" easing (cubic-bezier 0.2, 0, 0, 1 in M3; FastOutSlowIn in M2). Seven easing curves separate "utility focused" standard motion from "emphasized" M3-styled motion. Older Material guidance: motion "should not cause waiting but should be slow enough for the transition to be understood"; keep transitions short because users see them frequently; mobile transitions centre on about 300 ms, entering ~225 ms, exiting ~195 ms; "Transitions that exceed 400ms may feel too slow"; standard easing with short durations reads as "direct, functional", emphasized easing with long durations as "more stylized". [Practitioner — platform guidance] — [material-components-android Motion.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Motion.md); [Material 3 easing and duration guidance](https://m3.material.io/styles/motion/easing-and-duration/applying-easing-and-duration); [Material 1 duration & easing](https://m1.material.io/motion/duration-easing.html)
- **Apple HIG on motion (primary, via mirror).** "Add motion purposefully ... Don't add motion for the sake of adding motion. Gratuitous or excessive animation can distract people and may make them feel disconnected or physically uncomfortable." "Make motion optional" and never the only channel for important information (supplement with haptics/audio). "Aim for brevity and precision in feedback animations" — "when a game displays a succinct animation that's precisely tied to a successful action, players can instantly get the message without being distracted from their gameplay." "In apps, generally avoid adding motion to UI interactions that occur frequently." "Let people cancel motion ... don't make people wait for an animation to complete." Games should hold 30-60 fps. For visionOS, avoid motion in peripheral vision and oscillation around 0.2 Hz. [Practitioner — platform guidance] — [Apple HIG: Motion](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/motion) (text via [apple-dev-mcp mirror](https://github.com/tmaasen/apple-dev-mcp))
- **Animation is good at attracting attention and that is the risk.** NN/g: moving elements powerfully attract attention; consider goal, frequency and mechanics; distracting animations pull users from their primary goals. [Moderate] — [NN/g distracting animations](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/distracting-animations/)
- **Animation does not automatically aid comprehension.** In learning research, children learning a bicycle pump did no better from animations than from equivalent static graphics; in another study comprehension of causal relations in complex interactive material increased only when attention cues were added to the animation. [Moderate — learning-science studies, adjacent domain] — [TC Columbia PDF](https://www.tc.columbia.edu/faculty/bt2158/faculty-profile/files/nheeffectofanimationoncomprehensionandinterest.pdf); [Computers in Human Behavior cueing study](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/122/paper-detail/72425)
- **Vestibular harm and the reduce-motion preference (WCAG 2.3.3, primary).** "Some users experience distraction or nausea from animated content"; elements moving on scroll and parallax can trigger vestibular (inner-ear) reactions including dizziness, nausea, headaches and "potentially needing bed rest to recover"; remedies are to avoid unnecessary animation, provide an on-site control, or honour the OS reduce-motion feature; a page-flip transition that respects `prefers-reduced-motion` is given as a passing example. [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html) (source: [w3c/wcag](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/21/animation-from-interactions.html))
- **Prevalence and distraction (web.dev, Carie Fisher, 30 Sept 2022).** "By age 40, over 35% of adults will have experienced some form of vestibular dysfunction" (citing the Vestibular Disorders Association); people with ADHD "might be so distracted by your animated elements that they forget why they even went to your website"; an estimated 3% of people with epilepsy are photosensitive; the page recommends asking whether movement is essential and, if not, removing "even micro-movements", providing pause/stop/hide, avoiding infinite loops, testing flashing content with the PEAT tool, and using `prefers-reduced-motion` (web.dev elsewhere recommends animations off by default with opt-in). [Strong for prevalence citation; practitioner for recommendations] — [web.dev Learn Accessibility: Animation and motion](https://web.dev/learn/accessibility/motion) (source: [GitHub](https://github.com/GoogleChrome/web.dev/blob/main/src/site/content/en/learn/accessibility/motion/index.md))
- **Flash thresholds (WCAG 2.3.1, primary).** Content must not flash more than three times per second unless below the general/red flash thresholds; the reference flashing area is 341 x 256 CSS px (about a 10-degree viewport); red flashing has a stricter test. [Strong — standard] — [Understanding SC 2.3.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)
- **Implementation guidance (WebKit, MDN).** WebKit's 2017 post introducing `prefers-reduced-motion` recommends serving alternate animations rather than none, singles out spinning and vortex effects as problems for people with vestibular disorders, and notes Apple.com disabled scaling and background video motion under reduced motion. MDN: the setting signals a preference for an interface that "removes, reduces, or replaces motion-based animations"; "scaling or panning large objects can be vestibular motion triggers"; its example swaps a pulsing scale animation for a slow dissolve; the feature is widely available (since January 2020 per MDN's compatibility data); it is exposed on iOS (Settings > Accessibility > Motion), Android 9+ ("Remove animations"), macOS, Windows and Linux desktops. [Practitioner — browser-vendor guidance] — [WebKit: Responsive Design for Motion](https://webkit.org/blog/7551/responsive-design-for-motion/); [MDN prefers-reduced-motion](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion) (source: [mdn/content](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/css/reference/at-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion/index.md)); [Chrome developers summary](https://goo.gle/prefers-reduced-motion); [Tatiana Mac "no-motion-first"](https://www.tatianamac.com/posts/prefers-reduced-motion)
- **Duolingo's celebration animation** (streak milestone card with fire imagery) is described as successful "on both measurable and hard-to-measure fronts" with no figures. [Practitioner claim] — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/streak-milestone-design-animation)
- **Game "juice" — practitioner essays.** One essay argues excessive juice "can disrupt this flow of information, creating a sense of chaos and confusion"; another praises restraint, citing Celeste's subtle cue of Madeline's hair changing colour to show remaining dashes; a prompt-library guide advises "never let juice obscure gameplay information" and letting players reduce effects. A GDC 2025 talk (Jonathan Bell, Formosa Interactive) covers making UI audio "readable and memorable" using God of War Ragnarok, Helldivers II and Total War: Warhammer III. [Practitioner opinion] — [Wayline: Juice overload](https://www.wayline.io/blog/juice-overload-sensory-feedback-hurts-gameplay); [Wayline: The seductive squeeze](https://www.wayline.io/blog/the-seductive-squeeze-when-juice-in-game-development-becomes-a-crutch); [GameDev.net on GDC 2025 UI audio talk](https://gamedev.net/news/5816-what-was-that-noise-mastering-creative-ui-sound-design-with-intention/)
- A secondary article attributes "a 60% drop in task completion" to excessive movement, but the figure could not be traced to NN/g or any primary study. [Unverified — do not use] — [creditunionwebsolutions motion guide](https://creditunionwebsolutions.com/ux-design/the-art-of-purposeful-motion-a-practical-framework-for-using-animation-and-motion-design-to-guide-attention-provide-feedback-and-elevate-user-experiences/)

### Inferences
- A sensible motion budget for the arcade: ~100 ms for button/toggle feedback, 200-300 ms for card/modal/leaderboard-row transitions, nothing over 400-500 ms except deliberate reward celebrations, and no animation on actions repeated dozens of times per session (NN/g, Material, Apple all converge).
- Reward celebrations (prize won, new personal best, promotion on a leaderboard) are the one place where longer, "emphasized" motion is justified by platform guidance, provided it is tied precisely to the success event, skippable, and replaced by a static or fade-only version under `prefers-reduced-motion`.
- Because a third of adults over 40 (the parents and teachers) report vestibular symptoms, and WCAG lists parallax and scroll-driven motion as triggers, the site should avoid parallax backgrounds, auto-scrolling tickers and large scaling transitions, and should ship a reduced-motion stylesheet from day one rather than as a later fix.
- Any in-game flashing effect (hit flashes, strobing) must stay under three flashes per second and avoid saturated red flashes; this is a legal-style hard constraint, not a taste judgement.

### Gaps
- No quantitative study was retrieved showing micro-animations raise perceived product quality, perceived speed or engagement (e.g. skeleton-screen vs spinner tests, Harrison et al. on progress-bar perception, Hicks et al. 2019 or Kao 2020 on "juiciness" and player experience); these should be checked if numbers are needed.
- NN/g's "The Role of Animation and Motion in UX" (2020) could not be read; only its existence is confirmed.
- The primary Agrawal et al. (2009) prevalence study behind the "35% over 40" figure was not opened; the figure is cited here via web.dev and the Vestibular Disorders Association.

## Key question 6: Evidence specific to leaderboard and prize pages (showing rank and rewards legibly)

### Takeaway
Meta-analytic evidence says gamification produces moderate motivational gains (g ≈ 0.5) and small-to-moderate achievement gains, that K-12 learners benefit more than university students, and that leaderboards work better combined with badges/levels or collaboration than alone and can demotivate some users; Duolingo's documented league design (small weekly cohorts of similar users, promotion/demotion) is the best-known worked example but its engagement figures circulating online are unverified. Nothing retrieved tests the visual presentation of rank or prizes directly, so legibility guidance must be derived from the general findings (numerals, few focal points, 3:1/4.5:1 contrast, never colour alone).

### Cited Findings
- **Meta-analysis of gamification in asynchronous online learning (Treve, 2026, Online Learning Journal).** 28 empirical studies from 2010-2024: moderate positive effect on motivation (Hedges' g = 0.52) and small-to-moderate effect on achievement (g = 0.41); narrative-based gamification had a much stronger influence than points/badges/leaderboards; K-12 learners gained more than higher-education students; publication bias was flagged, though sensitivity analyses held. [Strong — meta-analysis; leaderboard-specific effect size not retrieved] — [OLJ article](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/view/5188); [PDF](https://olj.onlinelearningconsortium.org/index.php/olj/article/download/5188/1617/28002)
- **Combinations beat lone leaderboards.** A systematic review / meta-analysis of gamification elements on e-learning platforms (Nuremberg Institute of Technology, 2024-2025; a 2023 CELDA conference review is cited in the same results) found combining elements boosts motivation, "particularly the combination of badges with leaderboards or level up systems". [Moderate — review] — [Meta-analysis of the Influence of Gamification Elements on Motivation (2024)](https://opus4.kobv.de/opus4-ohm/frontdoor/index/index/year/2024/docId/2476); [Systematic literature review (2025)](https://opus4.kobv.de/opus4-ohm/frontdoor/index/index/year/2025/docId/3144)
- **Competition plus collaboration beats pure competition.** A GaLA 2020 conference paper on leaderboards (Tampere University) cites an earlier meta-analysis finding that competition combined with collaboration "seems to be particularly effective for motivation and behavioral outcomes" compared with competition alone. [Moderate — cited meta-analysis; the GaLA paper's own results were not retrievable] — [GaLA 2020 leaderboard paper PDF](https://trepo.tuni.fi/bitstream/10024/130150/1/GaLA_Conf_2020_paper_leaderboard.pdf)
- **Mixed and negative effects exist.** One review states evidence that gamification enhances learning performance "is mixed"; a self-determination-theory study notes that "negative effects on participants' attitudes and motivation have also been reported in the literature". [Moderate] — [NSF PAR paper](https://par.nsf.gov//servlets/purl/10410064); [Augsburg SDT study PDF](https://opus.bibliothek.uni-augsburg.de/opus4/files/109038/109038.pdf)
- A PMC-hosted meta-analysis lists "leaderboards" and "motivation" among its keywords, but its findings were not retrievable. [Unread] — [PMC8037535](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8037535)
- **Duolingo Leagues (first-party design description).** Weekly leaderboards pair learners "with people who have similar study habits and who are in a similar time zone"; competition spans all courses; top Diamond-league learners can enter a multi-round tournament in some weeks. A third-party deconstruction describes pools of only 20-30 names and similar-pace matching to keep contests winnable. [Practitioner — first-party rationale plus analyst inference] — [Duolingo blog: Leagues](https://blog.duolingo.com/duolingo-leagues-leaderboards/); [Deconstructor of Fun: Leagues](https://duolingo.deconstructoroffun.com/mechanics/leagues)
- **Unverified Duolingo engagement figures.** A Substack post claims leaderboards gave "a 17% increase in overall learning time and triple the number of highly engaged learners"; a loyalty-vendor article claims stronger daily retention and streak-building after Leagues with no data; another vendor cites churn falling from 47% to 28% and a 36% DAU increase with no source. None could be traced to Duolingo. [Weak/unverified — do not quote as fact] — [Substack analysis](https://marishalakhiani.substack.com/p/breaking-down-duolingos-growth-model); [OpenLoyalty](https://www.openloyalty.io/insider/leaderboard-gamification-tactics); [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/blog-gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo)
- **Reward moments as shareable artefacts.** Duolingo's streak-milestone card (owl + fire imagery, designed for sharing) is reported as successful without numbers. [Practitioner] — [Duolingo blog](https://blog.duolingo.com/streak-milestone-design-animation)
- **Legibility rules that apply directly to rank/prize displays (from the general evidence above):** numerals and specifically formatted items are what scanners pick out (spotted pattern; "show numbers as numerals") — [NN/g text-scanning patterns](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/text-scanning-patterns-eyetracking/); young users miss information not presented as a limited number of clear focal points above the fold — [Djamasbi 2011](https://www.ijec-web.org/past-issues/volume-15-number-4-summer-2011/online-viewing-and-aesthetic-preferences-of-generation-y-and-the-baby-boom-generation-testing-user-web-site-experience-through-eye-tracking/); zones or statuses (promotion/demotion, won/not won) must not be conveyed by colour alone — [Understanding SC 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html); row text needs 4.5:1 and icons/badges 3:1 contrast — [Understanding SC 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html), [SC 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html); tappable rows need 24 px minimum / 44-48 px recommended targets — [Understanding SC 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html); auto-updating or scrolling leaderboards need a pause control if they move for more than five seconds — [Understanding SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html); Material recommends its "emphasized" (heavier) type styles for showing selection and actions, i.e. for the viewer's own row — [Material Typography.md](https://github.com/material-components/material-components-android/blob/master/docs/theming/Typography.md). [Strong — standards; moderate — NN/g]
- **Apple's game-specific legibility advice** — test game text on every platform, enlarge or add background shapes behind hard-to-read text, avoid light weights, and when text is enlarged prioritise what players care about — applies to score and prize overlays. [Practitioner] — [Apple HIG Typography](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/typography) (via [mirror](https://github.com/tmaasen/apple-dev-mcp))

### Inferences
- For a school-based charity arcade, the meta-analytic pattern (K-12 benefit larger; combinations and collaboration beat pure competition; some users demotivated) favours small cohort leaderboards (class, year or school-sized, like Duolingo's 20-30-person leagues), paired with badges or a collective goal (e.g. a school-wide donation or play-count target) rather than one global ranking that most students will never top.
- Rank presentation should exploit the spotted-pattern finding: large numerals for rank and score, the viewer's own row visually emphasised (heavier type, contrast-passing highlight plus a label such as "You"), top-3 shown as a compact focal block above the fold, and promotion/demotion zones marked with icons or labels in addition to colour.
- Prize pages should carry one dominant image or illustration of the prize, a short numeral-led summary (e.g. "3 prizes", "closes in 2 days"), and plain-language eligibility text in short lines; motion should be reserved for the moment a prize is won and must be skippable and reduced-motion-safe.
- Because leaderboards can demotivate low-ranked users, consider showing relative progress ("2 places from promotion", personal best) alongside absolute rank; this is an inference from the collaboration/competition evidence rather than a tested display rule.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests leaderboard visual design (absolute vs relative rank display, number of rows shown, highlighting the viewer's own position) on legibility or behaviour; Bai, Hew, Sailer & Jia (2021, Computers & Education, "From top to bottom: how positions on different types of leaderboard may affect ... learning performance, intrinsic motivation, and course engagement"), Landers, Bauer & Callan (2017) on leaderboards and goal setting, and Christy & Fox (2014) on leaderboards and stereotype threat could not be checked before the search budget ran out.
- The GaLA 2020 leaderboard paper's own method and results, and the Treve 2026 leaderboard-specific effect size, were not retrievable.
- The "endowed progress" effect (Nunes & Drèze 2006) relevant to progress-to-prize displays was not verified.
- No typography source on tabular (fixed-width) numerals for aligning scores was retrieved; this is standard practitioner typography advice but is unsourced here.
- Duolingo has published no verifiable metrics for Leagues; all engagement numbers in circulation are second-hand.
