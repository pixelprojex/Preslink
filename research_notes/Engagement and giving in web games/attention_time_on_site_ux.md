# Attention, time on site and UX: what keeps people on consumer websites and web apps (and what makes them leave)

Scope: load time and responsiveness, perceived performance, first impressions and onboarding, copy length and reading level, mobile ergonomics, scroll depth and action placement, micro-interactions/sound/haptics, return triggers and habit formation, and which site categories hold attention longest. Compiled 2026-10-25 for the PresLink Arcade brief (a school charity arcade site in Cork, Ireland, reached mostly from a QR poster on phones, live for a few weeks).

Evidence grading used throughout:
- [STRONG] peer-reviewed experiment/field experiment, systematic review, or replicated finding
- [MODERATE] large observational/vendor dataset with disclosed method, or a single well-run lab study
- [WEAK] small sample, unreplicated, conflicting, or only available via secondary reports
- [PRACTITIONER] industry benchmark, talk or vendor guidance without disclosed method
- [CLASSIC] pre-2010 work that the later literature still relies on

Retrieval note for the report writer: web search worked for this task but the session's egress proxy refused every direct page fetch (nngroup.com, w3.org, michbar.org, viget.com, etc. all returned 403 from the proxy), and the shared search budget ran out before the last ~10 queries. Numbers below are therefore taken from search-result extracts that quote the primary source; where a figure could not be confirmed against the primary text it is marked "not verified this session" or moved to Gaps. Nothing has been invented to fill a gap.

## Key question 1: How much does each 100 ms or 1 s of delay change bounce, conversion or play rate?

### Takeaway
The best-known datasets converge on roughly the same order of magnitude: about 100 ms of extra mobile latency costs in the region of 2–8% of conversions (Akamai 2017: −7% per +100 ms, mostly on phones; Deloitte/Google 2020: +8.4% retail conversions per −0.1 s applied across the whole journey), and bounce probability rises ~32% as mobile load goes from 1 s to 3 s, with 53% of mobile visits abandoning pages that take over 3 s (Google/SOASTA 2017). For web games specifically, Poki's data show "conversion to play" falls with download size (about 80% of US players still waiting at 10 MB, worse on slower-network countries). Most of this is correlational and vendor-published; the strongest causal evidence is Google's 2009 latency experiment (100–400 ms of added delay → 0.2–0.6% fewer searches, persisting after the delay was removed) and Vodafone's A/B test (31% better LCP → +8% sales).

### Cited Findings
- [MODERATE, vendor] 53% of mobile site visitors leave a page that takes longer than three seconds to load; a neural network trained on the data (90% prediction accuracy) estimated that bounce probability rises 113% as load time goes from 1 s to 7 s (Google/SOASTA 2017, "new industry benchmarks for mobile page speed") — [Think with Google](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-gb/marketing-strategies/app-and-mobile/mobile-page-speed-new-industry-benchmarks); [Think with Google PDF](https://www.thinkwithgoogle.com/_qs/documents/1064/WhyMarketersShouldCareAboutMobilePageSpeed.pdf); [AMP blog write-up](https://blog.amp.dev/2017/02/28/new-industry-benchmarks-for-mobile-page-speed/)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Probability of bounce increases 32% as page load time goes from 1 s to 3 s (Google data page) — [Think with Google data](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/data/page-load-time-statistics). (The same Google/SOASTA series is widely quoted as +90% at 5 s, +106% at 6 s and +123% at 10 s; those three figures were not verified this session.)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Akamai "State of Online Retail Performance" (Spring 2017; ~10 billion user visits / 27.7 billion beacons from top retailers in the US, Europe and APAC): a 100 ms delay in load time cut conversion rates by 7%; a 2 s delay increased bounce rates by 103%; 53% of mobile visitors leave a page taking >3 s — [Akamai press release](https://www.akamai.com/fr/newsroom/press-release/akamai-releases-spring-2017-state-of-online-retail-performance-report); [eCommerceNews summary](https://ecommercenews.com.au/story/millisecond-delays-can-hurt-conversion-rates-online-transactions-akamai-report)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Akamai's later 2017 cut (reported by MediaPost): pages loading 100 ms slower (2.8 s vs 2.7 s) saw a 2.4% decrease in conversion overall, but 7.1% on smartphones and 3.8% on tablets — i.e. the headline "7%" is essentially the phone figure — [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/306009/three-seconds-and-youre-out.html)
- [MODERATE, Google-commissioned] Deloitte Digital "Milliseconds Make Millions" (2020; 37 retail, travel, luxury and lead-gen brands in Europe and the US; 30 million user sessions over four weeks): a 0.1 s improvement in mobile site speed was associated with retail conversions +8.4% and average order value +9.2%; travel conversions +10.1% and AOV +1.9%. The gains were only observed when every page in the user journey improved by 0.1 s on all four measured metrics (First Meaningful Paint, Estimated Input Latency, Observed Load, Time To First Byte) — [Deloitte](https://deloitte.com/ie/en/services/consulting/research/milliseconds-make-millions.html); [web.dev case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions); commissioning caveat per [MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/352986/speed-turns-into-a-performance-metric-per-google.html)
- [STRONG, controlled experiment] Google's 2009 "Speed Matters" experiments: injecting 100–400 ms of server-side delay into search results reduced the number of searches per user by 0.2% to 0.6% (averaged over four or six weeks); the 400 ms group did 0.44% fewer searches in the first three weeks and 0.76% fewer in the second three weeks (the effect grew with exposure), and users exposed to 400 ms for six weeks still did 0.21% fewer searches in the five weeks after the delay was removed — [Google Research blog](https://research.google/blog/speed-matters/); [Brutlag's paper (PDF)](https://services.google.com/fh/files/blogs/google_delayexp.pdf); persistence detail via [dri.es summary](https://dri.es/speed-matters)
- [STRONG, A/B test] Vodafone Italy: an A/B test on a paid-traffic landing page with no visual or functional differences, where the optimised variant had 31% better field LCP, produced 8% more sales, a 15% higher lead-to-visit rate and 11% higher cart-to-visit rate — [web.dev Vodafone case study](https://web.dev/case-studies/vodafone)
- [MODERATE, platform data] Poki (web-game portal) "Size Matters: How Loading is Losing You Players" (W3C Workshop on Web Games, 2019): across a sample of 174 desktop games, the share of players who stay after a game starts loading falls with download size — roughly 0.8 (80%) at 10 MB for US players, and lower for Egypt and Brazil at the same size — [W3C slides (PDF)](https://www.w3.org/2018/12/games-workshop/slides/12-size-matters.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER] Poki's developer guidance (via the Defold Poki SDK page): keep the web build under 20 MB because web players decide very quickly whether to stay; Poki's test pipeline looks at playtime, conversion-to-play, file size, loading time, scaling and SDK errors, with "5+ minutes average playtime" and "65%+ conversion to play" cited as benchmarks of a game that works — [Defold Poki best practices](https://defold.com/extension-poki-sdk/best-practices); [GameDev.net summary](https://gamedev.net/news/3701-best-practices-when-building-for-the-web/)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Contentsquare 2025 Digital Experience Benchmark (6,000 sites, 90 billion sessions): one in three visits showed "frustration" (rage clicks, slow-loading pages or excessive hovering); bounce rate rose 0.6% year on year and conversion rate fell 6.1% — [Contentsquare benchmark insights](https://go.contentsquare.com/en/benchmark-insights); [press summary](https://thewisemarketer.com/businesses-pay-more-for-digital-customers-in-2025-but-see-6-1-drop-in-conversions-as-user-frustration-persists/)

### Inferences
- For a QR-poster site opened cold on a phone over school Wi-Fi or 4G, the first load is the whole funnel: budget for <1 s to a usable first screen and <3 s to a playable game, because the 1 s→3 s band is where Google's data shows the first large bounce step (+32%) and >3 s is where half of mobile visitors are gone.
- Treat game bundle size as the primary speed lever (Poki's curve is driven by MB, not server speed): keep each arcade game well under 10 MB, lazy-load everything after the first playable frame, and cache with a service worker so return visits (the second QR scan) are near-instant.
- The Deloitte finding that gains only appeared when every page in the journey improved argues for keeping the whole flow — landing → play → donate — on as few, equally fast pages as possible.
- Measure "conversion to play" (Poki's metric) and LCP/INP in the field; the 65% conversion-to-play benchmark is a useful target for a charity arcade with no acquisition budget.

### Gaps
- No study found that gives a per-second play-rate curve for web games beyond Poki's size-based data; the full Poki deck could not be fetched (proxy refusal), so the exact per-MB slope is unknown.
- HTTP Archive / Web Almanac page-weight benchmarks were not retrieved.
- The Google/SOASTA +90%/+106%/+123% bounce figures for 5/6/10 s are from memory of the same series and were not verified this session.
- No Irish or EU-specific load-time/bounce data was found.

## Key question 2: What do eye-tracking and scroll-depth studies say about where key actions and information should sit on phone screens, and how far people scroll?

### Takeaway
Attention is heavily front-loaded in both time and space: the first 10 seconds decide whether a visitor stays (NN/g on Microsoft's Weibull dwell-time analysis), 55% of pageviews last under 15 seconds (Chartbeat), visual appeal is judged in 50–500 ms (Lindgaard; Reinecke), and 57% of viewing time lands above the fold with 74% in the first two screenfuls (NN/g 2018 eye-tracking, desktop). On phones, grip observation (49% one-handed, 36% cradled) and target-size experiments (≥9.2 mm; NN/g ≥1 cm × 1 cm) plus NN/g's finding that hidden "hamburger" navigation roughly halves discoverability all point the same way: the one action that matters belongs large, visible and thumb-reachable in the first screen, with the rest of the page kept simple.

### Cited Findings
- [STRONG, large dataset] Microsoft Research (Liu, White & Dumais 2010) analysed page-visit durations for 205,873 web pages (over 2 billion dwell times from a browser plug-in) and found dwell time follows a Weibull distribution with "negative aging": pages must pass an initial screening before being examined in detail ("screen-and-glean"), and the effect is stronger for less entertaining pages — [Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/understanding-web-browsing-behaviors-through-weibull-analysis-of-dwell-time/); [paper PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2010/10/dwelltime.pdf)
- [STRONG, derived] NN/g's reading of the same data: the average page visit lasts a little under a minute but the average is not useful; the first 10 seconds of a visit are critical to the stay/leave decision, and only after about 30 seconds does the hazard curve flatten — [NN/g, How long do users stay on web pages](https://nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)
- [MODERATE, vendor dataset, 2014] Chartbeat (Tony Haile) across ~2 billion interactions, mostly news/article pages: 55% of pageviews received less than 15 seconds of engagement; visitors who stayed 15–60 seconds were far more likely to see an ad than those under 15 s (57% vs 37%) — [INMA](https://www.inma.org/blogs/conference/post.cfm/chartbeat-shares-lessons-learned-on-monetising-audience-not-traffic); [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/publishers-looking-to-the-attention-web-for-more-meaningful-metrics/); [Psychology Today summary](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/brain-wise/201509/people-read-only-60-of-an-online-article)
- [MODERATE, eye-tracking] NN/g 2018 "Scrolling and Attention" (120 participants, 130,000+ fixations on 1920×1080 screens): users spent about 57% of page-viewing time above the fold and 74% in the first two screenfuls (to 2160 px); more than 65% of above-fold time was in the top half of the viewport; in 2010 (1024×768 monitors) the above-fold share was 80% — attention still drops sharply after the fold — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed; CLASSIC-adjacent 2006] Lindgaard et al. 2006 (Behaviour & Information Technology 25(2)): visual-appeal ratings of home pages flashed for 50 ms correlated well with ratings after longer scrutiny; Study 3 compared 500 ms with 50 ms exposure — [Nature news](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html); [summary](https://www.handmadeweb.com.au/web-users-judge-sites-in-just-50-milliseconds/)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed] Reinecke et al. CHI 2013: 548 volunteers rated 450 websites; computational models of perceived colorfulness and visual complexity, combined with demographics (education, age), explain about half of the variance in appeal ratings given after a 500 ms view; first impressions also shape later judgments of usability and trustworthiness. A secondary summary reports visual complexity as the strongest driver, with low-to-medium complexity most appealing (not verified against the paper) — [Harvard abstract](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml); [paper PDF](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)
- [MODERATE, observational, 2013] Steven Hoober's field observation of 1,333 phone users in public (780 actually touching the screen): 49% held and used the phone one-handed, 36% "cradled" (one hand holds, other interacts), 15% two-handed; of one-handed users 67% used the right thumb and 33% the left — [Garcia Media summary of Hoober](https://garciamedia.com/?p=7518); [Loop Insight](https://loopinsight.com/2013/12/02/how-users-hold-mobile-devices); caution that the widely quoted "75% use one thumb" is misleading and that phones have grown since 2013 — [Addy Osmani](https://addyosmani.com/blog/touch-friendly-design/)
- [STRONG, controlled study; CLASSIC 2006] Parhi, Karlson & Bederson (MobileHCI 2006): for one-handed thumb use on small touchscreens, targets of 9.2 mm suffice for discrete tasks (buttons, checkboxes) and 9.6 mm for serial tasks (text entry); speed improved with size with no significant difference in error rate between tested sizes — [Microsoft Research abstract](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/?p=155150); [ACM DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/1152215.1152260). NN/g's guidance drawn from it: touch targets at least 1 cm × 1 cm for quick, accurate selection — [NN/g touch target size](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)
- [MODERATE, quantitative usability study] NN/g with WhatUsersDo (179 participants, 6 sites, phones and desktops): hidden navigation (hamburger menus) is less discoverable than visible navigation on both phones and desktops; NN/g's video summary says discoverability is "cut almost in half" by hiding main navigation; a secondary write-up reports a ~20% drop in discoverability and tasks 39% slower on desktop and 15% slower on mobile with hidden navigation; the effect is larger on desktop than on phones — [NN/g article](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/); [NN/g video](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/hamburger-menus/); [Smart Insights](https://www.smartinsights.com/digital-marketing-strategy/are-hamburger-menus-hidden-navigation-good-for-ux/)
- [WEAK, secondary] Singh et al. (University of Alberta, 2011), as cited in Nielsen & Budiu's mobile usability book: reading comprehension reportedly dropped by about half (often quoted as 48%) when readers moved from full-size PC screens to phones; figures are second-hand and contested — [UX Magazine](https://uxmag.com/articles/why-reading-on-mobile-is-uniquely-challenging); [Wylie Communications](https://www.wyliecomm.com/5-obstacles-to-reading-on-smartphones/)

### Inferences
- The first phone screen must carry (a) what this is, (b) who it helps and (c) one big "Play" (or "Play, then donate") button; everything else is secondary. Given ~57–80% of attention above the fold and a 10-second stay/leave window, text below the first screen should be treated as optional.
- Place the primary button in the lower-middle of the first viewport (thumb-reachable for one-handed and cradled users, which together were 85% of Hoober's sample) but above the browser's bottom chrome; use a fixed bottom bar for Play/Donate/Leaderboard rather than a hamburger menu.
- Make every tap target ≥10 mm (~44–48 CSS px; the common platform guidance of 48 dp Material / 44 pt Apple HIG was not retrieved this session) with generous spacing, because the Parhi study's sizes are for one-handed thumb use, which is the posture a QR-poster user is most likely to be in.
- Keep visual complexity low (few elements, one accent colour, big type): Reinecke's and NN/g's data say simplicity drives the sub-second appeal judgment that precedes any reading.
- Since NN/g's fold percentages are desktop figures, do not assume phone users scroll less or more; instead instrument scroll depth on the live site.

### Gaps
- No phone-specific eye-tracking scroll-depth percentages were found (NN/g's 2018 study is desktop; its 2016 mobile reading article could not be fetched).
- Chartbeat's often-cited claim that ~66% of article attention occurs below the fold could not be searched (budget exhausted) and is not included as a finding.
- NN/g 2016 "Reading Content on Mobile Devices" (Moran) reportedly found no significant comprehension difference between phone and desktop for the same text, contradicting the Alberta figure; the article was blocked by the proxy, so this is unverified and is noted here only as a lead.
- Hoober's data predates large phones; no 2020s replication was found.

## Key question 3: What onboarding and "time to first fun" findings exist for games and apps?

### Takeaway
The best-documented onboarding result is Duolingo's delayed sign-up A/B test (letting people learn before creating an account raised DAU by ~20%, with later "soft wall" refinements adding 8.2%), though it is self-reported by the company. Industry benchmarks set expectations: median mobile-game Day-1 retention is only ~22% (top quartile ~27–30%, top decile ~40%), Day-7 under 4%, with sessions of ~5–6 minutes and ~4 sessions a day; web portals (Poki) treat 65%+ "conversion to play" and 5+ minutes playtime as signs a game works. No published seconds-based "time to first fun" threshold was found; the hyper-casual guidance (one control, minimal tutorial, hook within the first session) is practitioner opinion.

### Cited Findings
- [MODERATE, company-reported A/B test] Duolingo: moving the sign-up screen several steps later (users could complete lessons without an account) produced roughly a 20% increase in DAUs; later refinements to when and how the sign-up "soft walls" appear added another 8.2% to DAUs about three years later — [First Round Review interview with Gina Gotthilf](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker); mechanics (skippable soft walls; progress lost after ~3 skips unless signed up) per [Heartcore](https://heartcore.substack.com/p/heartcore-consumer-insights--7eb). One secondary source attributes the 20% to next-day retention rather than DAU; the primary interview says DAU — [StriveCloud](https://www.strivecloud.io/blog/blog-gamification-examples-boost-user-retention-duolingo)
- [MODERATE, vendor benchmark] GameAnalytics 2025 Mobile Gaming Benchmarks (2024 data, ~11,600 apps): top-25% Day-1 retention 26.5–27.7% (down from 28–29% in 2023), bottom-25% 10–11.5%; iOS top quartile 31–33% vs Android 25–27%; median Day-7 3.4–3.9%; 75% of projects have Day-28 retention below 3%; average session length median 5–6 minutes, top quartile 8–9 minutes; players average 4 sessions/day (midcore 6–7) — [GameAnalytics 2025 report PDF](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2025-GameAnalytics-Mobile-Gaming-Benchmarks.pdf); [GameDev Reports summary](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-gaming-benchmarks)
- [MODERATE, vendor benchmark] GameAnalytics 2026 edition (2025 data, 16,262 titles with ≥1,000 MAU, sponsored by PWN Games): median Day-1 retention ~22%, top quartile just over 30%, top decile ~40%, top 1% 64–68%; median Day-7 below 4%; top 1% reach 13–15% Day-30; top-quartile session length 5.2 minutes, top 1% ~22 minutes (sample definitions differ from the 2025 edition, so not directly comparable) — [GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-and-pc-game); [report PDF](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/2026-01-27-2026-mobile-pc-benchmarks_compressed.pdf)
- [PRACTITIONER] Supersonic (hyper-casual publisher): tutorials should teach how to play "which they need to know to start having fun"; use a single control to keep onboarding simple; hyper-casual games have ~7 days to hook players; one source puts hyper-casual Day-7 retention at 5.9% and Day-30 at 1.38% — [Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/learn/blog/6-ways-to-boost-your-games-retention-from-d1-d7/); [Supersonic](https://supersonic.com/?p=78461); [Segwise benchmarks](https://segwise.ai/blog/mobile-gaming-app-user-retention-strategies.md)
- [PRACTITIONER] Unity on playable ads: still playing at second 14 ("S14") means the user has progressed through the tutorial/early onboarding, so S14 is used as a proxy for onboarding success — [Unity blog](https://unity.com/blog/create-retention-playable-ads)
- [PRACTITIONER] Poki: web players decide very quickly whether to stay; benchmarks of 65%+ conversion to play and 5+ minutes average playtime; build under 20 MB — [Defold Poki best practices](https://defold.com/extension-poki-sdk/best-practices)
- [MODERATE, platform data] Poki W3C 2019: conversion-to-play falls with download size (~80% at 10 MB, US; lower in Egypt/Brazil; 174 games) — [W3C slides](https://www.w3.org/2018/12/games-workshop/slides/12-size-matters.pdf)
- [MODERATE, large dataset] Microsoft/NN/g dwell-time analysis: the "screen-and-glean" pattern is stronger for less entertaining pages, i.e. entertaining content is screened less harshly — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2010/10/dwelltime.pdf)

### Inferences
- Never gate play behind an account or form. For a few-week school campaign there is no business case for sign-up at all; collect a nickname only when a player wants to post a score, after they have played (Duolingo's "soft wall after value" pattern).
- Design for a first session that reaches the core loop within one tap and under ~10–15 seconds (Unity's S14 proxy; NN/g's 10-second window), with one control (tap or swipe) and the tutorial embedded in the first level rather than as screens.
- Expect low organic return: even well-made mobile games keep only ~1 in 5 players to Day 1 and <1 in 25 to Day 7. Return visits in a short campaign will have to come from external prompts (posters, class announcements, email, deadlines) rather than habit; see Key question 6.
- Track Poki-style metrics (conversion to play, median session length, sessions per visitor) rather than retention curves designed for long-lived apps.

### Gaps
- No Supercell, King or Voodoo talk giving a numeric "time to first fun" was retrievable; the search budget ran out before a targeted GDC search, and such figures appear to be practitioner lore rather than published studies.
- No facebook/instant-games or browser-game Day-1 retention benchmarks were found; all retention benchmarks above are native mobile apps.
- The Duolingo numbers are company-reported with no published experiment write-up.

## Key question 4: What evidence is there on short copy, numbers and verbs, and on plain-language rewrites, for comprehension and task completion?

### Takeaway
Scanning, not reading, is the default: 79% of users scanned new pages and only 16% read word by word in Nielsen & Morkes' 1997 study, and rewriting a page to be concise, scannable and objective raised measured usability by 124% (concise alone +58%, scannable +47%) — a classic, single-site result. Plain-language rewrites of real documents have measurable effects on action (a US veterans' letter went from 43% to 65% response). Numerals ("23" not "twenty-three") attract fixations when people scan. Reading-level matters for the audience: 21% of Irish adults score at or below Level 1 literacy (PIAAC 2023), and lower-literacy readers answered only 46% of comprehension questions on an unedited pharma ad versus 82% for high-literacy readers; NN/g recommends a 6th-grade reading level for landing pages. Evidence specifically on "verbs" in copy is practitioner opinion, not measured.

### Cited Findings
- [CLASSIC, usability test, 1997] Nielsen & Morkes: 79% of test users always scanned any new page; 16% read word by word. On a single tourism site, a concise version scored 58% higher on measured usability, a scannable version 47% higher, an objective (non-promotional) version 27% higher, and a version combining all three 124% higher than the control — [Nielsen's 1997 Alertbox (mirror)](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web); [UX Myths summary](https://uxmyths.com/post/647473628/myth-people-read-on-the-web). Small sample, one site; percentages should not be read as general effect sizes.
- [CLASSIC, eye-tracking, 2007] NN/g: "numerals stop the wandering eye and attract fixations" even inside dense text; "23" beats "twenty-three" for scanning users looking for facts — [NN/g eyetracking topic listing](https://www.nngroup.com/topic/eyetracking/?page=2); [IntMath discussion](https://www.intmath.com/blog/mathematics/writing-numbers-on-the-web-610) (original article at nngroup.com/articles/web-writing-show-numbers-as-numerals/, not fetched)
- [MODERATE, documented case studies] Kimble, "Writing for Dollars, Writing to Please" (2012; 2nd ed.), summarising 50 studies of plain-language rewrites: e.g. the US Veterans Benefits Administration letter whose response rate rose from about 43% to 65% after a plain-language rewrite, saving about $4.4 million in staff time — [PlainLanguage.gov, "The Bottom Line"](https://PlainLanguage.Gov/resources/articles/the-bottom-line/); [Kimble in Michigan Bar Journal](https://www.michbar.org/file/barjournal/article/documents/pdf4article2972.pdf); [book](https://cap-press.com/books/isbn/9781531024543/Writing-for-Dollars-Writing-to-Please-Second-Edition)
- [MODERATE, survey] Trudeau, "The Public Speaks: An Empirical Study of Legal Communication" (Scribes Journal of Legal Writing 14, 2012; survey run in 2011, 376 respondents) is the only US empirical study of the public's preference for plain language; a 2017 international follow-up ("The Public Speaks, Again", with Cawthorne) confirms the preference — [Michigan Bar Journal PDF](https://www.michbar.org/file/barjournal/article/documents/pdf4article2079.pdf); [UALR](https://ualr.edu/law/?p=23246). (The specific percentages — commonly quoted as ~80% preferring plain language, rising with the reader's education and with the complexity of the topic — could not be verified this session because the PDF was blocked.)
- [WEAK, secondary report of NN/g eye-tracking] Testing an off-the-shelf pharmaceutical ad, NN/g's lower-literacy group answered 46% of comprehension questions correctly versus 82% for the highly literate group; NN/g then rewrote with shorter sentences, shorter words and explanatory graphics (post-rewrite scores not found) — [Wylie Communications](https://freewritingtips.wyliecomm.com/2020-09-10/)
- [CLASSIC, 2005] Nielsen, "Lower-Literacy Users: Writing for a Broad Consumer Audience": recommends a 6th-grade reading level on the homepage, key category pages and landing pages; lower-literacy users "plow" through text rather than scan — [ATJ Library record](https://atjlibrary.org/node/441/article-lower-literacy-users-writing-broad-consumer-audience-nielsen-2005); [contemporary summary](https://thoughtcrumbs.com/2005/03/low-literacy-web-users-plow-through.shtml)
- [WEAK, slide deck] Summers & Summers (2005): a revised site achieved a 93% task-success rate for lower-literacy web users versus 68% on the original — [health-literacy slides (archived)](https://cybercemetery.unt.edu/oilspill/20130219033544mp_/http://www.howto.gov/sites/default/files/health-literacy-and-the-Web-slides.pdf)
- [STRONG, national survey] PIAAC 2023 (CSO fieldwork June 2022–Sept 2023): 21% of adults in Ireland scored at or below Level 1 in literacy (OECD average 26%), not statistically different from 2012; 5% were at the lowest level (OECD 9%) — [PIAAC results factsheet](https://a.storyblok.com/f/120607/x/0b5935b5c6/piaac-results-factsheet-pdf.pdf); [CSO key findings](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-piaac/programmefortheinternationalassessmentofadultcompetenciespiaac2023/keyfindings); [NALA](https://www.nala.ie/adult-literacy-needs-have-risen-in-ireland)
- [MODERATE, large dataset] Chartbeat: 55% of pageviews get under 15 seconds of engagement (see Key question 2) — [INMA](https://www.inma.org/blogs/conference/post.cfm/chartbeat-shares-lessons-learned-on-monetising-audience-not-traffic)

### Inferences
- Write every screen so it can be understood by scanning in under 10 seconds: a headline with a number ("€1,240 raised for X"), one verb-led button ("Play"), one line on the cause, and nothing promotional. The 1997 data say promotional tone itself lowers measured usability.
- Target roughly a 10–12-year-old reading age (NN/g's 6th-grade guidance) given that a fifth of Irish adults are at or below Level 1 literacy and the audience includes pupils, parents and grandparents.
- Put numerals everywhere they occur (scores, amounts raised, days left, players today): they are what scanning eyes land on.
- Keep the donation explanation to a short plain-language paragraph with a concrete amount-to-outcome mapping; plain-language rewrites change behaviour (43%→65% response), not just comprehension.
- Claims that "verbs convert better" in CTAs are practitioner opinion; A/B test button wording on the live site instead.

### Gaps
- The often-cited NN/g 2008 finding that users read on average ~20–28% of the words on a page (Weinreich et al.) could not be searched before the budget ran out; not included as a finding.
- No controlled study on copy length specifically for mobile game/charity landing pages was found; Medium's "7-minute optimal read" and similar content-marketing claims were not retrieved and are weak in any case.
- Trudeau's percentages, the post-rewrite scores in NN/g's lower-literacy study, and the NN/g 2016 mobile comprehension article are unverified (proxy blocked the sources).
- No evidence found on "verbs" vs nouns in button copy beyond practitioner advice.

## Key question 5: What evidence is there on micro-interactions, sound and haptics for perceived quality and retention, and on the "aesthetic-usability effect"?

### Takeaway
The aesthetic-usability effect is real for first impressions (Kurosu & Kashimura 1995: r = 0.59 between aesthetics and apparent usability; Tractinsky 2000: aesthetics affected post-use usability ratings while actual usability did not) but the best-controlled modern test (Tuch et al. 2012, n = 80, 2×2) found the arrow mostly runs the other way: aesthetics had no effect on perceived usability, whereas poor usability lowered perceived aesthetics via frustration. For feedback: tactile feedback brought touchscreen typing close to physical-keyboard performance (Hoggan et al. 2008), "juicy" haptic embellishments improved enjoyment, aesthetic appeal and immersion in games (CHI 2021), and juiciness research shows redundant visual feedback helps appeal but can be overdone. Perceived-performance tricks are weaker than assumed: in Viget's 136-person test, skeleton screens were perceived as slower than spinners or blank screens. No study linking micro-interactions or sound to retention was found.

### Cited Findings
- [CLASSIC, 1995] Kurosu & Kashimura (CHI '95; 26 ATM layouts): "apparent usability" correlated r = 0.59 with aesthetic ratings, while correlations with inherent (measured) usability factors were mostly negligible — [CHI '95 proceedings](https://chi1995.chistatic.hosting.acm.org/proceedings/shortppr/mk_bdy.htm); [Monk & Lelos review PDF](https://www-users.york.ac.uk/~am1/HOIT07MonkLelos.pdf)
- [CLASSIC, 1997/2000] Tractinsky's 1997 Israeli replication found very high correlations between perceived aesthetics and a-priori perceived ease of use; Tractinsky, Shoval-Katz & Ikar 2000 ("What is beautiful is usable", computerised ATM) found the degree of aesthetics affected post-use perceptions of both aesthetics and usability, whereas the degree of actual usability had no such effect — [CHI 97 paper](https://chi1997.acm.org/proceedings/paper/nt.html); [Tractinsky research page](https://www.ise.bgu.ac.il/faculty/noam/research/aesthetics.html); [2000 paper PDF](https://mycourses.aalto.fi/pluginfile.php/1645430/mod_folder/content/0/Tractinsky%20%282000%29.pdf)
- [STRONG, lab experiment] Tuch, Roth, Hornbæk, Opwis & Bargas-Avila 2012 (Computers in Human Behavior 28(5)): 80 participants used one of four online-shop versions (aesthetics × usability, each high/low) and rated them before and after use with four validated instruments. Aesthetics had no effect on perceived usability; usability did affect post-use perceived aesthetics; affect (frustration) mediated the effect — i.e. "what is beautiful is usable" can reverse to "what is usable is beautiful" under a strong usability manipulation — [Google Research record](https://research.google/pubs/is-beautiful-really-usable-toward-understanding-the-relation-between-usability-aesthetics-and-affect-in-hci/); [Google Research blog](https://ai.googleblog.com/2012/05/is-beautiful-usable-what-is-influence.html); [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2012/05/Aesthetics-Usability)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed] Lindgaard 2006 and Reinecke 2013 (see Key question 2) establish that visual appeal is judged in 50–500 ms and that visual complexity/colorfulness predict it — [Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html); [Reinecke CHI 2013](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2013/reinecke13predicting.shtml)
- [STRONG, lab experiment; CLASSIC 2008] Hoggan, Brewster & Johnston (CHI 2008): adding tactile feedback (30 ms vibration for correct input, 500 ms for errors) to a touchscreen keyboard significantly improved speed and accuracy of finger-based text entry, bringing it close to a physical keyboard, in static and mobile settings; a 2007 study by the same lab found fewer benefits when tested on an underground train — [Glasgow eprints](https://eprints.gla.ac.uk/43811); [University of Glasgow news](https://www.gla.ac.uk/news/archiveofnews/2008/july/headline_85408_en.html); [DOI record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F1357054.1357300)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed] "Juicy game design" (Hicks, Gerling et al., CHI PLAY 2019): visual embellishments ("juiciness" — redundant, non-functional feedback to a single action) are assumed by industry and academia to improve player experience but empirical evidence was lacking; the paper tests this directly — [ACM record](https://unpaywall.org/10.1145%2F3311350.3347171); earlier reflections — [figshare talk](https://figshare.com/articles/Kieran_Hicks_Kathrin_Gerling_Patrick_Dickinson_-_What_makes_a_Game_Reflections_on_Juiciness_and_Player_Experience_/5147011). (Exact effect sizes not retrievable this session.)
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed] "Juicy Haptic Design" (CHI 2021): vibrotactile embellishments in games can enhance enjoyability, aesthetic appeal, immersion and meaning — [University of Waterloo Haptic Experience Lab](https://uwaterloo.ca/haptic-experience-lab/projects/juicy-haptic-design-vibrotactile-embellishments-can-improve)
- [MODERATE, qualitative] "Juicy Audio" (Hicks, Rogers, Gerling, Nacke, CHI PLAY 2024): interviews with audio designers find juicy-design principles are applied intentionally to sound to improve feedback and experience — [Staffordshire eprints PDF](https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/8527/1/3677084.pdf)
- [WEAK, small practitioner study] Viget tested 136 people on three loading treatments: mean perceived wait was 2.82 s for a skeleton screen, 2.41 s for a spinner and 2.29 s for a blank screen; the skeleton group was least likely to say content loaded quickly (59% vs 74% for spinners) and slowest on the follow-up task (10.54 s vs ~9.5 s) — [Viget](https://viget.com/articles/a-bone-to-pick-with-skeleton-screens). A separate small study found skeletons perceived as slightly shorter than blank screens or spinners, with left-to-right shimmer feeling faster than pulsing; samples too small to be conclusive — [UX Collective](https://uxdesign.cc/what-you-should-know-about-skeleton-screens-a820c45a571a). Evidence is mixed.
- [CLASSIC, 2007] Harrison, Amento, Kuznetsov & Bell (UIST 2007, "Rethinking the Progress Bar"): human time perception is non-linear, so progress-bar pacing changes perceived duration even when real duration is unchanged; nine bar behaviours were tested and users were most tolerant of slow/negative progress at the start of an operation; later studies conflict on slow-to-fast vs fast-to-slow — [Chris Harrison project page](https://chrisharrison.net/index.php/Research/ProgressBars); [CMU KiltHub](https://kilthub.cmu.edu/articles/journal_contribution/Rethinking_the_Progress_Bar/6470396/1); [2022 arXiv review](https://web3.arxiv.org/pdf/2211.13909)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Contentsquare's "frustration" metric (rage clicks, slow pages, excessive hovering) affected 1 in 3 visits in 2025 — [Contentsquare](https://go.contentsquare.com/en/benchmark-insights)

### Inferences
- Polish buys the first impression (sub-second appeal judgment) but does not rescue a game that feels broken: by Tuch et al., a laggy or confusing control will make the whole site look worse. Prioritise responsive input (no perceptible lag on tap) over decoration.
- Cheap juice that the evidence supports: immediate visual + sound + short vibration on every scoring action (navigator.vibrate on Android browsers; iOS Safari has no vibration API, so audio and visual must carry it). Keep it redundant and brief; the juiciness literature warns that too much embellishment is counter-productive.
- Do not assume skeleton screens make loads feel faster; a fast real first paint or a simple progress indicator that moves early is better supported.
- Sound should be opt-in or mute-able (classroom/public contexts); there is no retention evidence for sound, only experience ratings.

### Gaps
- No study was found linking micro-interactions, sound or haptics to retention or return visits (only to in-session experience ratings).
- Effect sizes from the CHI PLAY 2019 juiciness paper and the CHI 2021 haptics paper were not retrievable.
- Nielsen's classic response-time limits (0.1 s feels instant, 1 s keeps flow, 10 s loses attention) and the "Doherty threshold" (400 ms) were not retrieved this session (nngroup.com blocked), so they are not cited as findings; they are consistent with the Google 2009 and Akamai data above.
- No web-specific evidence on the Vibration API's effect on perceived quality was found.

## Key question 6: What return triggers work best for a short campaign (weeks): email, "closes on date" deadlines, live counters, notifications, QR posters and physical prompts?

### Takeaway
For a campaign measured in weeks, habits will not form (median 66 days to automaticity, range 18–254, in Lally 2010), so returns must be prompted. The field evidence favours: goal-proximity/progress displays (donations rise as a campaign nears its goal — Cryder et al. 2013; Kickstarter support is U-shaped with a surge near the deadline — Kuppuswamy & Bayus 2017), short or no deadlines rather than long ones (a one-month deadline produced the fewest responses in Knowles et al. 2022), social information about what others gave (Shang & Croson 2009, for new donors only), and physical point-of-decision prompts (a 50% relative increase in stair use across 16 studies — Soler et al. 2010, the closest rigorous analogue to posters in a school). Reminders work but have costs (Damgaard & Gravert 2018: more donations and more unsubscribes; higher push frequency raised uninstalls in Wohllebe et al. 2021). Channel benchmarks are sobering: nonprofit fundraising email clicks ~0.5%, mobile push CTR ~2%, web-push opt-in low single digits to ~15%. The famous Ariely & Wertenbroch self-imposed-deadline result has failed a 2026 replication.

### Cited Findings
- [STRONG, peer-reviewed; CLASSIC 2002 — now contested] Ariely & Wertenbroch (Psychological Science 13, 2002): people self-impose costly deadlines, and self-set deadlines improve performance, but less than evenly spaced externally imposed deadlines — [Duke record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/810365); [paper PDF](https://erationality.media.mit.edu/papers/dan/eRational/Dynamic%20preferences/deadlines.pdf). A direct replication of Study 2 by Hyndman & Bisin (Psychological Science, online July 2026) found deadline changes had a negligible effect on all performance metrics; Bisin & Hyndman 2020 found performance worse with deadlines — [replication](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/09567976261460772); [preprint](https://bpb-us-e1.wpmucdn.com/wp.nyu.edu/dist/c/16384/files/2025/09/Ariely_Replication-1.pdf)
- [STRONG, field experiment] Knowles, Servátka, Sullivan & Genç, "Procrastination and the non-monotonic effect of deadlines on task completion" (Economic Inquiry 60(2), 2022): survey invitations offering a charitable donation gave recipients one week, one month or no deadline; responses were lowest with the one-month deadline and highest with no deadline, with the no-deadline and one-week groups producing many early responses (a month lets people delay and forget) — [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/procrastination-and-the-non-monotonic-effect-of-deadlines-on-task/); [MPRA working paper](https://mpra.ub.uni-muenchen.de/109484/). (Exact response rates not retrieved.)
- [STRONG, field experiment] Damgaard & Gravert, "The hidden costs of nudging" (Journal of Public Economics 157, 2018): reminders to a charity's mailing list increased donations but also increased unsubscriptions; ignoring these costs overstates donor welfare effects by a factor of ten. Working-paper estimates: non-givers lose about $2.35 per reminder versus a net benefit to the charity of $0.18 per reminder; counting lost future donations, the reminder reduced lifetime gains from the solicitation by about one-third; a second treatment promising fewer future solicitations reduced unsubscribes — [working paper abstract](https://swopec.hhs.se/gunwpe/abs/gunwpe0650.htm); [working paper PDF](https://repec.econ.au.dk/repec/afn/wp/16/wp16_03.pdf); [Chronicle of Philanthropy](https://www.philanthropy.com/news/follow-up-pitches-can-hurt-fundraising-in-the-long-run/)
- [STRONG, field + lab] Cryder, Loewenstein & Seltman, "Goal gradient in helping behavior" (JESP 49, 2013): contribution rates on a micro-lending site rose as recipients approached their funding goals (Study 1); funds close to their goals drew more donations than funds far from them in a large field experiment (Study 2); mediation showed perceived impact of late-stage gifts explains the effect (Study 3) — [CMU PDF](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/GoalGradient.pdf); [WUSTL record](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/goal-gradient-in-helping-behavior/)
- [STRONG, large observational] Kuppuswamy & Bayus (Journal of Business Venturing 32(1), 2017; 25,058 Kickstarter projects over two years): backer support rises as a project nears its goal and drops once the goal is reached; the effect is stronger near the deadline, for small goals, and when early support was limited; earlier versions document a U-shaped pattern with more backing in the first and last weeks than the middle, across successful and unsuccessful projects — [IDEAS/RePEc record](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jbvent/v32y2017i1p72-89.html); [2013 working-paper summary](https://yannigroth.com/2013/02/24/the-dynamics-of-backer-support-in-crowdfunding-findings-from-kickstarter)
- [STRONG, field experiment; CLASSIC-adjacent 2009] Shang & Croson (Economic Journal 2009): telling public-radio callers what another donor had just given raised contributions; the most effective social-information level was the 90th–95th percentile of previous gifts; the effect held for new members but not renewing members. A later replication found no effect, mostly among renewing donors — [paper PDF](https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~jandreon/PhilanthropyAndFundraising/Volume%202/27%20Shang%20Croson%202009.pdf); [Knowledge@Wharton](https://knowledge.wharton.upenn.edu/article/raising-the-bar-the-role-of-social-information-in-charitable-giving/); [replication](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/S0193-230620150000018002)
- [STRONG, systematic review] Soler et al. 2010 (Am J Prev Med 38(2S)), Community Guide update on point-of-decision prompts (signs at the choice point): prompts alone increased stair use by a median 2.4 percentage points, a relative increase of 50% (interquartile 0.83–6.7 points; 21 study arms; "strong evidence") — [The Community Guide](https://www.thecommunityguide.org/findings/physical-activity-point-decision-prompts-encourage-use-stairs); [review PDF](https://origin.thecommunityguide.org/media/pdf/pa-ajpm-evrev-podp.pdf)
- [MODERATE, small unreplicated study] Lally et al. 2010 (European Journal of Social Psychology; 96 participants, 84 days): median 66 days for a new daily behaviour to reach its automaticity plateau, range 18–254 days; the 66-day figure rests on the 39 participants with good curve fits and the 254-day upper bound is a statistical extrapolation beyond the 84-day study; the study has not been replicated — [BPS Research Digest](https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit); [The Behavioral Scientist](https://www.thebehavioralscientist.com/articles/how-long-to-form-a-habit); [planned replication (PCI RR)](https://rr.peercommunityin.org/download/t_recommendations.reply_pdf.8382af6b73bdd0f7.6d616e757363726970745f7072657072696e742e706466.pdf)
- [STRONG, diary studies] Wood, Quinn & Kashy 2002: in hourly diary studies, about 43% of reported behaviours were performed almost daily in the same context, i.e. habitual — [paper PDF](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wendy-wood/wp-content/uploads/sites/183/2023/10/Wood.Quinn_.Kashy_.2002_Habits_in_everyday_life.pdf); [APA interview with Wood](https://www.apa.org/research/action/speaking-of-psychology/behavioral-habits)
- [STRONG, field experiment] Wohllebe et al. 2021 (Innovative Marketing): 17,500 retail-app users, five push frequencies over seven weeks — uninstalls rose with the frequency of non-personalised pushes and direct open rates fell — [Business Perspectives](https://www.businessperspectives.org/index.php/journals/innovative-marketing/issue-382/mobile-apps-in-retail-effect-of-push-notification-frequency-on-app-user-behavior). Vendor data: retention drops when users receive more than 3 notifications on install day — [MoEngage report PDF](https://info.moengage.com/hubfs/Retention%20Benchmark%20reports/e-commerce-app-india-report_final.pdf)
- [MODERATE, vendor benchmarks] Mobile push: average CTR ≈2.25% across industries (CleverTap 2025, via Pushwoosh), range 0.5–7.4%; action-game CTR 0.82% Android / 0.46% iOS; opt-in rates (Batch 2025) Android 67% (down from 85% after Android 13's permission prompt), iOS 56%, overall 61% — [Pushwoosh benchmarks](https://www.pushwoosh.com/blog/push-notification-benchmarks/); [Business of Apps](https://www.businessofapps.com/?p=101648); [Airship 2025 benchmarks PDF](https://growth.airship.com/rs/313-QPJ-195/images/Airship-2025-Push-Notification-Benchmarks-EN.pdf?version=0)
- [WEAK, vendor, dated] Web push: PushOwl (90 Shopify Plus stores, 3+ years old) average opt-in 2.7% (range 2.3–5%) and CTR 3.68% — [PushOwl](https://docs.pushowl.com/en/articles/6361107-web-push-notification-benchmarks); Gravitec claims 10–15% opt-in with well-placed prompts (unsourced) — [Sleeknote roundup](https://sleeknote.com/?p=138964)
- [MODERATE, sector benchmark] M+R Benchmarks 2025 (2024 data): nonprofit fundraising email click-through 0.48% (flat year on year); $58 raised per 1,000 fundraising messages (−10%); advocacy email CTR 2.3%, response rate 1.4% — [M+R email](https://mrbenchmarks.com/email); [M+R 2025 email messaging](https://2025.mrbenchmarks.com/email-messaging.html). Nonprofit open rates ~25–29% (inflated by Apple Mail Privacy Protection) — [Bloomerang](https://bloomerang.com/blog/email-marketing-for-nonprofits); 28.59% open / 3.29% click per Neon One via [Nonprofit Tech for Good](https://www.nptechforgood.com/2025/06/04/2025-email-marketing-statistics-for-nonprofits)
- [MODERATE, company A/B test] Duolingo's delayed sign-up (+20% DAU) shows that removing friction before value is itself a retention lever — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)

### Inferences
- Do not plan on habit: a few-week campaign is shorter than the median time to automaticity. Design for prompted returns instead, and make each return instant (cached PWA, no login).
- Physical prompts at the point of decision are the best-evidenced channel available to a school: posters with the QR code at corridors, canteen queues, the front desk and the school gate (where phones come out) are the direct analogue of stair-prompt signs that produced a 50% relative lift. Change the poster's headline figure weekly to make it a fresh prompt.
- Show a live progress bar/goal thermometer and "€X to go" prominently; the goal-gradient evidence (Cryder; Kickstarter) says giving accelerates near the goal, so set a reachable goal and, if it is reached early, announce a stretch goal rather than leaving a full bar (support drops once a goal is hit).
- Use a visible "closes on <date>" and a final-week push; the crowdfunding U-shape and Knowles et al. favour short, salient deadlines over long ones. Keep deadlines near-term (days, not a month).
- Show social information from the top of the distribution ("most parents gave €10–€20") to first-time donors, per Shang & Croson; avoid averages that anchor low.
- Email the school community sparingly (launch, mid-point, last 48 hours): each extra reminder brings diminishing donations and real annoyance costs (Damgaard & Gravert), and sector click rates are ~0.5% anyway.
- Skip web push for this campaign: opt-in rates are low single digits on the web, iOS requires installing the site to the home screen first, and over-frequent pushes raise abandonment; a class WhatsApp/announcement or poster refresh is a cheaper, better-evidenced trigger.
- Give returning players a reason: leaderboards that reset weekly and a per-class score counter convert the goal-gradient and social-information findings into play rather than only giving.

### Gaps
- Searches on countdown timers/urgency field experiments and on QR-code scan statistics could not be run (budget exhausted), so there is no cited evidence on live countdowns or QR adoption rates.
- No study was found on physical prompts (posters) in schools specifically; the stair-prompt review is an analogue, not a direct test.
- BJ Fogg's Behavior Model (B = MAP: behaviour needs motivation, ability and a prompt at the same moment; behaviormodel.org) could not be retrieved this session and is cited only as a lead.
- No evidence was found on "live counters" (visitor/donor counters) as distinct from goal thermometers.
- Duolingo's own streak-effect figures were not retrievable.

## Key question 7 (objective coverage): Which kinds of sites hold attention longest and why?

### Takeaway
Per-visit attention is short everywhere on the open web (Chartbeat: 55% of news pageviews under 15 s; Similarweb: most industries under 2 minutes per visit, with financial services the longest at 2:18), whereas interactive, self-paced products hold people far longer per session: mobile games average 5–6 minutes per session (top quartile 8–9) at ~4 sessions a day, and web-game portals aim for 5+ minutes of playtime. The mechanism in the dwell-time literature is that entertaining content is screened less harshly ("screen-and-glean" is weaker for entertaining pages), and that time-on-page is only retained once a visitor passes the first 10–30 seconds. Data on social and learning apps' daily minutes could not be retrieved this session.

### Cited Findings
- [MODERATE, vendor] Similarweb industry averages for visit duration: financial services 2:18 and pharma/medical devices 2:16; all other major industries under two minutes; note Similarweb counts bounces as 0-second visits in the average — [Similarweb blog](https://similarweb.com/corp/blog/session-duration); [metric definition](https://support.similarweb.com/hc/en-us/articles/115000501485)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Chartbeat: 55% of pageviews (mostly news/articles) under 15 s; 15–60 s visitors much more likely to engage with on-page elements (57% vs 37% ad viewability) — [INMA](https://www.inma.org/blogs/conference/post.cfm/chartbeat-shares-lessons-learned-on-monetising-audience-not-traffic); [journalism.co.uk](https://www.journalism.co.uk/publishers-looking-to-the-attention-web-for-more-meaningful-metrics/)
- [MODERATE, vendor] GameAnalytics: mobile-game session length median 5–6 min, top quartile 8–9 min (2024 data); ~4 sessions/day, midcore 6–7 — [GameAnalytics 2025 PDF](https://investgame.net/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/2025-GameAnalytics-Mobile-Gaming-Benchmarks.pdf); 2025-data edition: top-quartile 5.2 min, top 1% ~22 min — [GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-and-pc-game)
- [PRACTITIONER] Poki benchmark of 5+ minutes average playtime for a successful web game — [Defold Poki guide](https://defold.com/extension-poki-sdk/best-practices)
- [STRONG, large dataset] Liu et al. 2010: "negative aging" (initial screening) is stronger for less entertaining pages; after screening, dwell times lengthen — [Microsoft Research PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2010/10/dwelltime.pdf)
- [MODERATE, vendor] Contentsquare 2026 benchmark: time on site fell 7% and content consumption 10% year on year across 6,000+ sites, i.e. web attention per visit is still shrinking — [Contentsquare 2025/2026 press](https://contentsquare.com/fr-fr/press/2025-digital-experience-benchmarks/)
- [MODERATE, company-reported] Duolingo's play-before-sign-up and soft-wall approach (+20% and +8.2% DAU) illustrates that learning apps earn repeat visits by making the first interaction the product itself — [First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)

### Inferences
- A charity arcade should borrow the game-session model (5-minute sessions, several short visits) rather than the news-page model (15 seconds, one visit): the game itself is the attention engine, and the cause/donation message should ride on the game's session time rather than compete with it on the landing page.
- Because web attention per visit is short and falling, the arcade's value proposition is the thing that is different — playable in one tap — and the site should get out of the way to let that happen.
- Report playtime per visitor and return visits rather than page views; Chartbeat's and NN/g's work shows page views say nothing about attention.

### Gaps
- Searches for daily time-spent by app category (social, video, games, learning; e.g. Sensor Tower/data.ai figures for TikTok/YouTube/Duolingo) could not be run before the budget ran out; no cited numbers for social or learning apps.
- Similarweb's per-category table (news, games, social) was not visible in results; only the two longest industries were quoted.
- No study found that directly compares why categories differ; the "screen-and-glean" mechanism is an inference from the dwell-time literature.
