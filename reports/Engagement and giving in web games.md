# Speed, fairness and honesty drive arcade giving

PresLink Arcade will raise the most money by being fast to load, instant to respond, visibly fair, frictionless to pay through and plain about where each euro goes, not by importing the engagement machinery of free-to-play games. The research notes converge on that answer from six directions. The first load and the first ten seconds decide whether a QR scan becomes a play: **53% of mobile visitors abandon pages that take over 3 seconds**, and visual appeal is judged within 50–500 ms, before a word is read. The stop-the-light loop sits in the sub-genre with the **highest recorded Day-1 retention in hyper-casual games (44% for top "timing" titles)**, and its retention depends on feedback landing within roughly 100 ms of the tap and on near-misses being shown as honest, actionable information rather than engineered "so close" moments. Colour choices move behaviour very little, whereas clutter, low contrast, small touch targets and dense text measurably cost attention, trust and task success. Leaderboards energise the top and must be designed for the middle: a reachable reference point raised contributions **530% among people below it** in one field experiment, while unreachable absolute boards demotivated the bottom two-thirds of classrooms. Giving responds to a seeded, reachable goal, to high-but-plausible social information, to wallet-first checkout and to a concrete "€2 does X" link, while deadlines mostly change when people give rather than whether. For an audience that is mostly under 18, Irish, EU and UK regulators converge on no streaks, no pressure to spend, visible caps and high-privacy defaults, and Irish gambling law makes "pure skill" the pivotal design property, because a paid game that is even partly chance would bar under-18s outright. The feature most at odds with all of this is the **"most plays" spot prize**, which rewards spending. Two of the six research strands (colour and visual design; reward loops and game feel) were cut short by a usage limit, and no strand could open primary pages directly, so figures are flagged where they rest on search-result extracts or memory.

## How far to trust the numbers in this report

Every research strand reports the same retrieval problem: the session's proxy refused direct page fetches from nngroup.com, w3.org, ico.org.uk, irishstatutebook.ie, eur-lex and most academic hosts, and the shared search budget ran out before final verification passes. Most figures therefore come from search-result extracts that quote the primary source, and the notes say so item by item. The colour strand could not run targeted searches on gold-as-reward, purple-as-prestige, teachers' perceptions of "childish" styling or the Reinecke & Gajos 2014 cross-national preference study, and the reward-loops strand lost search after about 28 queries, so several of its classic references (endowed progress, peak-end, round-number goals, RAIL) carry DOIs supplied from memory rather than confirmed this session. Where a number below is marked "not verified" or "recalled", treat it as a lead to check before it appears in committee minutes or on the site. Where the notes graded a finding STRONG (replicated experiments, meta-analyses, large field experiments) or MODERATE (a single good experiment or large dataset), this report leans on it; PRACTITIONER and vendor claims are named as such. Nothing in the legal sections is legal advice.

## The first load and the first ten seconds decide whether a scan becomes a play

For a site reached by scanning a poster on a phone in a corridor, the landing load is the whole funnel, and the evidence on load time is unusually consistent in direction even though most of it is vendor-published. Google's 2017 mobile benchmarks found **bounce probability rises 32% as load time goes from 1 s to 3 s** and that 53% of mobile visitors leave a page taking over 3 s ([Think with Google](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/data/page-load-time-statistics); [Think with Google benchmarks](https://thinkwithgoogle.com/intl/en-gb/marketing-strategies/app-and-mobile/mobile-page-speed-new-industry-benchmarks)). Akamai's 2017 retail dataset put the cost of a 100 ms delay at **7.1% of conversions on smartphones** against 2.4% overall ([MediaPost](https://www.mediapost.com/publications/article/306009/three-seconds-and-youre-out.html); [Akamai](https://www.akamai.com/fr/newsroom/press-release/akamai-releases-spring-2017-state-of-online-retail-performance-report)), and Deloitte's Google-commissioned study of 30 million sessions associated a 0.1 s mobile speed gain with **8.4% more retail conversions**, but only when every page in the journey improved ([web.dev](https://web.dev/case-studies/milliseconds-make-millions); [Deloitte](https://deloitte.com/ie/en/services/consulting/research/milliseconds-make-millions)). The causal anchors are smaller but firmer: Google's 2009 experiments injecting 100–400 ms of delay cut searches per user by 0.2–0.6%, with the effect growing over weeks and persisting after the delay was removed ([Google Research](https://research.google/blog/speed-matters/)), and a Vodafone A/B test with no visual change but 31% better LCP produced 8% more sales ([web.dev Vodafone](https://web.dev/case-studies/vodafone)). For web games the lever is download size rather than server speed: Poki's data across 174 games show "conversion to play" falling with megabytes, at roughly **80% of US players still waiting at 10 MB** and worse on slower networks ([W3C slides](https://www.w3.org/2018/12/games-workshop/slides/12-size-matters.pdf)), and Poki's working benchmarks for a game that "works" are 65%+ conversion to play and 5+ minutes of playtime with the build kept under 20 MB ([Defold Poki guide](https://defold.com/extension-poki-sdk/best-practices)). The arcade is a single page with one game, so these targets are reachable; the point is to measure conversion to play and LCP in the field rather than assume them, because the Deloitte finding implies the landing, play and pay screens must all be fast, not just the first one.

Once the page is up, attention is front-loaded in time and space. Microsoft's analysis of over two billion dwell times found page visits follow a Weibull "screen-and-glean" curve: pages must pass an initial screening before being examined, and that screening is harsher for less entertaining content ([Microsoft Research](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/understanding-web-browsing-behaviors-through-weibull-analysis-of-dwell-time/)), which NN/g reads as **the first 10 seconds deciding stay-or-leave** ([NN/g](https://nngroup.com/articles/how-long-do-users-stay-on-web-pages/)). Chartbeat's two billion interactions put 55% of pageviews under 15 seconds ([INMA](https://www.inma.org/blogs/conference/post.cfm/chartbeat-shares-lessons-learned-on-monetising-audience-not-traffic)). Appeal is judged even faster: home pages flashed for 50 ms drew ratings that matched longer scrutiny ([Nature](https://www.nature.com/news/2006/060109/full/news060109-13.html)), and in a 450-site study, computed visual complexity and colourfulness plus demographics explained about half the variance in appeal after a 500 ms view, with complexity mattering far more than colour and the first impression haloing onto later judgements of usability and trust ([Reinecke et al. 2013](https://kgajos.seas.harvard.edu/papers/reinecke13aesthetics.pdf)). On desktop, NN/g's 2018 eye-tracking placed 57% of viewing time above the fold and 74% within two screenfuls ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/)); no phone-specific scroll percentages were retrieved, so the site should instrument its own scroll depth rather than assume. The practical reading is that the first phone screen must carry what this is, who it helps and one large "Play · €2" button, with everything else treated as optional.

Where that button sits matters because of how phones are held. Hoober's 2013 field observation of 1,333 users found **49% one-handed and 36% cradled**, so 85% interact with a thumb ([Garcia Media summary](https://garciamedia.com/?p=7518)), a dataset that predates large phones and has no 2020s replication. The classic thumb-target experiment found 9.2 mm sufficient for discrete taps ([Parhi, Karlson & Bederson](https://dx.doi.org/10.1145/1152215.1152260)), NN/g recommends at least 1 cm square ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/touch-target-size/)), and the standards floor is 24 px (WCAG 2.5.8) with 44 px at AAA and 48 dp in Material ([WCAG 2.5.8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/target-size-minimum.html); [WCAG 2.5.5](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/target-size-enhanced.html)). Hiding navigation behind a hamburger roughly halves discoverability in NN/g's 179-participant study ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/hamburger-menus/)), which supports the site's existing visible tab bar (Play, Board, About, You) over any collapsed menu. The onboarding evidence points the same way as the site's no-sign-up design: Duolingo's company-reported test of letting people use the product before creating an account raised DAU by about 20%, with a later refinement adding 8.2% ([First Round Review](https://review.firstround.com/the-tenets-of-a-b-testing-from-duolingos-master-growth-hacker)), and forced account creation is one of the top abandonment reasons in Baymard's checkout surveys (see the giving section). Asking for initials on the first tap and an email only after the first play is already the "soft wall after value" pattern.

Copy has to survive scanning by a mixed-literacy audience. In Nielsen and Morkes' 1997 study **79% of users scanned and 16% read word by word**, and a concise, scannable, objective rewrite scored 124% higher on measured usability than the promotional control (one site, small sample, so the percentage is an illustration, not an effect size) ([Alertbox mirror](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web)). Numerals stop the scanning eye where words do not ([IntMath on NN/g](https://www.intmath.com/blog/mathematics/writing-numbers-on-the-web-610)), and plain-language rewrites change behaviour, not just comprehension: a US veterans' letter went from a 43% to a 65% response rate ([PlainLanguage.gov](https://PlainLanguage.Gov/resources/articles/the-bottom-line/)). The audience argument is specific to Ireland: PIAAC 2023 found **21% of Irish adults at or below Level 1 literacy** ([CSO](https://www.cso.ie/en/releasesandpublications/ep/p-piaac/programmefortheinternationalassessmentofadultcompetenciespiaac2023/keyfindings)), NN/g's long-standing advice is a sixth-grade reading level on landing pages ([ATJ Library record](https://atjlibrary.org/node/441/article-lower-literacy-users-writing-broad-consumer-audience-nielsen-2005)), and NN/g's teen research found teens perform worse than adults in usability tests because of lower reading levels and impatience ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/usability-of-websites-for-teenagers/)). The often-quoted claim that phone screens halve comprehension comes from a Cloze test of privacy policies and is contested; NN/g's 2016 follow-up found short, simple text understood equally well on phones, with only difficult text slowing readers (second-hand, unverified) ([Wylie summary](https://www.wyliecomm.com/2023/05/reading-on-mobile)). Either way the rule is the same: the parents' page, the prize terms and the scoring explanation should be written in short lines at a 10–12-year-old reading age, led by numerals.
