# Let skill, not phones, pick the winners

The follow-up research leaves the earlier report's direction intact but moves the arcade's main risk from how the game looks to what its score actually measures. Verification weakened colour psychology again, since red's apparent effects on test performance lose all evidential value once publication bias is corrected, and confirmed that legibility, simple structure and cues that do not depend on colour are what count. The site's new tiles, muted pastels with letters in a deep shade of each hue, now clear WCAG's 4.5:1 contrast minimum easily at **6.5–7.6:1** (*computed here*), but because all eight share almost the same lightness they are hard for anyone to tell apart and close to identical for colour-blind players, who are about **1 in 12 Irish schoolboys**; that is acceptable while initials carry identity, but it blunts the colour picker and leaves the swatches faint. The bigger finding concerns fairness. Phones differ by **35–140 ms** in the combined delay of screen and touch, and the follow-up's score model, applied here to the live scoring curve, shows 40 ms of extra phone lag costing a practised player **150–230 points** of median score, enough to erase the gap between practised and typical players. Because the build scores each tap at the frame that was on screen, the curve's millisecond-sharp top is finer than a phone can display: on the model's assumptions every thousand plays yield roughly 5–75 lights scoring 999, so "the earlier one wins" turns the best-light prize into a race for the first lucky millisecond, while the new first-play prize, played without practice, partly ranks phones. The site's recent changes (the same lights for everyone each day on a 1 ms grid, a first-play prize, €20 and €100 spending limits, no play counts, a top-20 board, pastel tiles and a child-appropriate email note) settle most of the earlier report's spending and contrast concerns, though the best-score boards still reward volume within the caps. The next steps are cheap: free unscored practice that measures each phone's lag and shows "12 ms late", a best-light rule no millisecond lottery can decide, one player per person, accurate wording about phones and spending, and a last contrast pass on the prize bands, grey text and moving light.

> **How to read the evidence.** Neither follow-up could open most web pages: fetching failed and the network proxy reached only GitHub. Standards, design-system code, MDN's browser data and game source code were therefore read in full from GitHub, while papers, NN/g, Baymard, WebAIM and industry figures come from search-result extracts of abstracts and publisher pages. A finding below is at that extract level unless marked otherwise: *full text* means the primary source was read directly, *second-hand* means it was seen only in press, blogs or summaries, *unverified* means it could not be confirmed, and *vendor* marks unaudited industry data. *Computed here* marks arithmetic or simulation done during this research, by the follow-up researchers or for this report against the site's current code: contrast ratios, colour-blindness simulations (Machado 2009 matrices with CIEDE2000 colour differences) and a score model whose timing spreads (15, 30 and 45 ms) and phone lags (0 or 40 ms) are assumptions, not measurements. The checks run for this report reproduced the follow-up's contrast figures exactly, its colour differences to within about 0.3 and its score table to within 2 points. Statements about the site come from its current code. Legal points are general information, not legal advice.

## Verified evidence keeps the direction but reopens three fixes

Read against the site as it now stands, the follow-ups confirm most of the earlier report and strengthen its cautions, and they reopen three fixes the site has already made (the 1 ms grid, the first-play prize and the pastel tiles), each because a detail the earlier work lacked changes how the fix behaves.

| Earlier report | What the follow-ups add or change | On the site now |
|---|---|---|
| Colour psychology is weak; drop the "80% brand recognition" claim | Weaker still: red's effects on test scores lose evidential value after bias correction. Also drop "red button +21%" and "older users prefer less colour" | Nothing to change |
| Initials should reach 4.5:1 on all eight tiles | Bold initials set at 12–13 px count as normal text needing 4.5:1; only 2 of the 8 original colours passed | Done with pastels at 6.5–7.6:1, but the tiles now merge for colour-blind players |
| No study tests whether teachers and parents find bright styling childish | Still none; trust follows simple structure, consistency and a sturdy payment step | Pastels are a defensible taste call, not an evidence-driven one |
| Unknown where "juice" (layered visual and sound effects) stops helping | Moderate juice beats both none and extreme | Confetti only at 850+, skipped under reduced motion |
| Present near-misses as diagnostic, never engineered | The one skill-game experiment found near-misses bored players and did not raise the urge to continue | Early/late words shown; the milliseconds are not |

The colour strand's checks mostly confirm the human-computer interaction evidence and cut the colour psychology further. Lindgaard's "50 milliseconds" study holds as a design, across three studies with 500 ms and 50 ms exposures, though its often-quoted correlation remains *unverified* ([HFI](https://www.humanfactors.com/newsletters/an_exploration_of_relations_between_visual_appeal.html)); a 2011 follow-up found judgements of a homepage's trustworthiness and usability "largely driven by visual appeal", though processed "in a qualitatively different manner" from appeal itself ([Lindgaard et al. 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/1959022.1959023)). Visual complexity lowers appeal within 17 ms ([Tuch et al. 2012](https://research.google/pubs/pub38315/)), and five experiments on real sites found structure had a "manifold and greater impact" than colour, whose factors mainly changed how colourful a site looked ([Seckler et al. 2015](https://edoc.unibas.ch/29197)). Usability changes how beautiful a site looks after use, not the reverse ([Tuch et al., CHB 2012](https://research.google/pubs/is-beautiful-really-usable-toward-understanding-the-relation-between-usability-aesthetics-and-affect-in-hci/)), and among 60 adolescents aged 14–17 a more attractive phone design gave faster task completion and higher perceived usability ([Sonderegger & Sauer 2010](https://sonar.ch/documents/302271/files/Sonderegger_Sauer2010.pdf)). Hue effects shrink: a meta-analysis of 67 effect sizes found red's apparent harm to test performance concentrated in early studies, with **"no evidential value" remaining after correction for publication bias** ([Gnambs 2020](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/s13423-020-01772-1)), and red's boost to attractiveness is small and possibly inflated (d = 0.26 for men rating women) ([Lehmann et al. 2018](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1474704918802412)). Three claims should go. The "80% brand recognition" figure is still credited to an untraceable "Loyola" study ([Colorcom](https://www.colorcom.com/research/why-color-matters/)); the "red button beat green by 21%" test is *unverified* and confounded, because the page was dominated by green ([Uxcel](https://app.uxcel.com/courses/color-psychology-for-designers/testing-color-combinations-148/testing-for-conversions-8936)); and the idea that older users prefer less colourful sites runs the wrong way. Reinecke and Gajos's 2.4 million ratings show women and less-educated people preferring more colour, and secondary summaries say people over 40 preferred *more* colour, not less (age finding *unverified* against the paper) ([Reinecke & Gajos 2014](https://eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2014/reinecke14visual.shtml); [Weinschenk](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/26009934-100-more-things-183-what-people-find-visually-appealing-depends-on-age?tab=book)).

The reward strand's corrections make the earlier advice more conservative. Duolingo's own blog confirms that allowing two streak freezes instead of one raised daily active learners by **0.38%**, and that separating the streak from the daily goal raised day-14 retention by **3.3%**, both company tests without method detail ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/how-duolingo-streak-builds-habit); [Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/improving-the-streak)): real but small effects on a huge free product, and not transferable to paid play by minors. The widely quoted "leagues gave 17% more learning time" appears only *second-hand* ([Elite Game Developers](https://elitegamedevelopers.substack.com/p/investing-in-language-learning-apps)). More useful is Duolingo's admission that league points pulled learners off the core lessons until it rebalanced them, which it says added 1.1–1.8 million minutes of core learning a day: whatever a ranking counts is what people chase ([Duolingo](https://blog.duolingo.com/time-spent-learning-well)). The "timing games keep 44% on day one" figure is a 2020 snapshot of the top 5% of hyper-casual games (*vendor*) ([GameAnalytics](https://www.gameanalytics.com/blog/the-metrics-behind-hyper-casual-games-industry-report)), and later editions changed their samples, so year-on-year comparisons mislead ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/gameanalytics-mobile-and-pc-game)). The only direct near-miss experiment in a skill-based video game, with 40 players, found near-misses left them "more bored and less excited" than big wins and no support for an urge to keep playing ([Finserås et al. 2021](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11469-019-00070-9)), which weakens, without overturning, the Candy Crush finding behind the earlier advice to soften the paid replay. Juice follows an inverted U: in the largest test, medium and high juiciness beat none and extreme on every measure (*second-hand*) ([Improbable Research on Kao 2020](https://improbable.com/tag/rpg/)), and in a preliminary study players rated a juicier version higher but played it worse ([Juul & Begy 2016](https://adk.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/good-feedback-for-bad-players-a-preliminary-study-of-juicy-interf)). The 2024 "juicy audio" paper cited in the earlier notes is an interview study of 12 sound designers, not a player experiment ([Hicks et al. 2024](https://eprints.staffs.ac.uk/8527)). Two earlier gaps closed. In experiments with 10,000 and 70,000 players, people "in almost all cases" played longer when the game was easier ([Lomas et al. 2013](https://www.stat.cmu.edu/~brian/463-663/project-part-one/Optimizing%20Challenge.pdf)). Web vibration works in Chrome and Samsung Internet on Android, not at all in Safari on iPhones, and silently does nothing in Firefox for Android (*full text*) ([MDN browser data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Navigator.json)), so on iPhones the arcade's feel rests on sound and visuals alone.

## Pastel tiles read clearly but merge for colour-blind players

### Contrast is now solved with room to spare

WCAG 2.2 settles what the initials need. "Large" text starts at 14 point bold, about 18.5 px, so the 12–13 px bold initials on the boards are normal text needing **4.5:1**; ratios are not rounded, and brand colours earn no exemption ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html); [WCAG 2.2 definition](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/#dfn-large-scale)). The tile itself needs no ratio against the page because the letters carry the meaning, though a selected or focused state must reach 3:1 and a change of hue alone "is likely to fail" the rule on using colour ([W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)), and a dark outline or halo round letters is an accepted way to pass ([W3C G18](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/general/G18)). Apple's checker is more lenient, accepting any bold text at 3:1 ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). The alternative APCA method is stricter for small, heavy text, but it is beta software outside WCAG, and an April 2026 review reports that the WCAG 3 draft still lists its contrast method as "yet to be determined" ([APCA](https://github.com/Myndex/SAPC-APCA/blob/master/documentation/APCA_in_a_Nutshell.md); [Roselli](https://adrianroselli.com/2026/04/wcag3-contrast-as-of-april-2026.html)). Against that bar, only 2 of the original 8 tile colours passed with white letters, purple at 5.74:1 and indigo at 5.54:1, with green as low as 2.09:1 (*computed here*); that palette was essentially Duolingo's brand set ([Duolingo](https://design.duolingo.com/identity/color)). Such failures are common: WebAIM found low-contrast text on 83.9% of the top million home pages in 2026 (*second-hand*) ([Priority Pixels on WebAIM](https://prioritypixels.co.uk/insights/web-accessibility-is-getting-worse-according-to-the-2026-webaim-million-report/)), and only about 30% of mobile sites passed Lighthouse's contrast check in 2025 ([Web Almanac](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/accessibility)).

At the site owner's request, the tiles are now eight muted pastels (pale green, blue, purple, orange, red, pink, teal and indigo) with letters in a deep shade of the same hue. **Every tile now clears 4.5:1, at 6.5–7.6:1, and four reach 7:1** (*computed here*), which settles contrast with room to spare and follows one of the routes the follow-up suggested, dark letters on light tiles. APCA's verdict is cooler: the tiles score Lc 72–75, short of its levels for 13 px text at weight 800, though close to its level for short "spot" text at weight 700. The follow-up's minor advice therefore stands as optional polish: weight 700 rather than 800 on the small tiles, and 14 px or more where the layout allows ([apca-w3](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-w3/blob/master/src/apca-w3.js)).

### Equal lightness leaves colour-blind boys nothing to go on

The cost of the pastels lands on how easily the colours can be told apart, and colour-blindness makes that cost concrete. In a random sample of 1,626 Irish schoolchildren, **8.3% of boys** (95% CI 6.6–10.3) and 1.8% of girls had a colour-vision deficiency; the green-weak deutan type (4.8%) outnumbered the red-weak protan type (1.7%), and Traveller boys reached 21.0% ([Harrington et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32285548/)). The girls' figure is high next to the European 0.4% and should be checked against the full text ([Birch 2012](https://doi.org/10.1364/JOSAA.29.000313)). Most affected boys have milder, partial forms rather than full colour-blindness ([Colblindor](https://www.color-blindness.com/?p=143)). At 8.3%, a class of 30 boys has about a **93% chance** of including at least one colour-blind pupil, and every 400 boys include about 33 (*computed here*), before counting the fathers and male teachers who play.

The pastels share almost the same lightness (CIELAB L\* 89–92) and little colour intensity (chroma 8–12), so hue is the only thing separating them, and hue is exactly what colour-blindness removes. Simulating the eight tile backgrounds (*computed here*; Machado matrices as implemented in [colorspacious](https://github.com/njsmith/colorspacious/blob/master/colorspacious/cvd.py), CIEDE2000 differences), the closest pair for normal vision, purple and indigo, is 4.3 apart, against 12.5 in the original bright palette. Under full green-blindness (deuteranopia), blue and purple fall to 0.5, and pink and teal, blue and indigo, and purple and indigo to about 1, all but identical; under the commonest form, moderate deuteranomaly, purple and indigo fall to 1.4 and blue and indigo to 1.9. The deep letter colours fare little better, since a deuteranope sees the green and red letters only 0.9 apart. On the page, the tiles sit at 1.23–1.32:1 against white and 1.09–1.17:1 against the green tint of the player's own row, so the tile shape itself almost disappears. As the follow-up concluded for the original palette, this still satisfies WCAG's rule on using colour, because the initials, the "You" tag and the highlighted row carry identity ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). But colour has gone from a weak secondary cue to almost none, for everyone and especially for colour-blind boys, which rules out any feature that relies on telling tiles apart, such as colour teams, "find your colour" or prize states shown by tile colour.

The colour picker suffers most. Its pastel swatches sit at 1.23–1.32:1 against the white card and their faint edge at about 1.5:1, neighbouring options are as close as the tiles, and only the selected ring passes, at 17:1 (*computed here*); the recent changes did give each swatch a 44 px tap area. W3C treats a colour-only control as arguably essential but encourages a second identifying cue, and the follow-up proposed a border of at least 3:1 or initials inside each swatch ([W3C 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)). Two fixes within the pastel style would restore much of what was lost. Drawing each swatch as a miniature tile, with the player's initials in that hue's deep ink and a thin outline in the same ink, makes every option preview itself and reach 8–10:1 against white. Varying the depth of the tiles restores distinctiveness for colour-blind players, because lightness is the one difference colour-blindness preserves; the follow-up's best colour-blind-safe set worked by alternating mid and deep tiles. As an illustration only (*computed here*, untested with players), giving three tiles a mid tint and two a deeper, greyer one while keeping today's letter colours lifts the worst colour-blind pair from about 0.5 to 4.0–5.6 and the worst normal-vision pair from 4.3 to 8.2, with every tile still at 4.58:1 or better.

The game's hit-and-miss feedback needs no change. A hit is a green marker, a ring, a green verdict and a sound; a miss is a white marker and grey words; so the difference rides on lightness, wording and sound, which survive colour-blindness. The site's green and red differ in luminance by only 1.29:1 and fall from 68.5 to 8.5 apart under deuteranopia (*computed here*), so a red-versus-green miss cue would be a mistake. Where a second colour is wanted, blue against orange with a tick or cross holds up in the same simulation (*computed here*), even though Apple names blue and orange among risky pairings ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)), and game-accessibility guidance likewise asks that nothing essential rely on colour alone ([Game Accessibility Guidelines](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/)).

The moving light matters most, because it is the one graphic the whole game depends on, and it is visible against most of the track only through its translucent ring and drop shadow. The fixes are simple (*computed here*): dark ink on the bright prize bands gives 7.0–8.2:1, as the Best light band already shows at 6.04:1; a grey of #6e6e73 reaches 5.07:1; and a solid 2 px outline in the site's ink colour gives the light 5.5:1 or more against every part of the track.

## Choice and calm structure, not hue, carry identity and trust

Letting players choose their colour is sound motivational design, but a modest lever. A meta-analysis of 41 studies found choice raised intrinsic motivation, effort, performance and perceived competence, most strongly when the choice did not affect the task (as a tile colour does not), when no reward followed it, and for children rather than adults; effect sizes were not retrieved ([Patall et al. 2008](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18298272/)). In games, a felt sense of autonomy and competence predicts enjoyment and future play ([Ryan, Rigby & Przybylski 2006](https://repository.essex.ac.uk/7228/)), and players who created an avatar rather than taking a premade one identified with it more, which raised immersion, effort, enjoyment and time played ([Birk et al. 2016](https://doi.org/10.1145/2858036.2858062)). Every such study involved far richer customisation than one of eight colours, so PresLink's effect is plausible but small and untested. The IKEA effect is the wrong frame, because effort breeds attachment only when the effortful task is completed ([Norton et al. 2012](https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/12136084)). Apple also suggests letting people "customize color schemes such as chart colors or game characters" as a help for colour-blind users ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)), which works only if the options differ in lightness, the property the pastels lack. Any benefit from recognising one's own colour depends on seeing it wherever one appears. The site already shows it on the boards, the prize leads and the You tab, lets players change it at any time and defaults it from the initials, but pastel tiles that fade into white rows make it far less visible. The choice should stay free of any link to payment, consistent with Patall's finding that choice works best with no reward attached.

No study yet tests bright against plain styling with teachers or parents, so the earlier report's largest colour gap stays open. The adjacent evidence points one way: trust follows structure, consistency and a sturdy payment step, not hue. Appeal drives trust judgements (Lindgaard 2011, above), and appeal depends more on symmetry and simplicity than on colour; Seckler's highest-rated sites combined low complexity with a blue hue, medium brightness and medium-to-high saturation ([Seckler et al. 2015](https://edoc.unibas.ch/29197)). On charity sites, stronger appeals and human images made the site look more consistent, which raised its perceived warmth and competence and so people's intention to donate (217 participants) ([Kwak et al. 2023](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/intr-11-2021-0847/full/html)), and a two-study paper found that when people give their own money, the content and their trust in the organisation outweigh the site's looks ([GI 2020](https://dl.gi.de/handle/20.500.12116/34268)). Payment trust is decided at the card fields: in a 2025 survey of 1,026 US adults, **19% had abandoned a checkout in the previous three months because they did not trust a site with their card**, and reassuring copy and a visually "robust" card area make the same form feel safer ([Baymard](https://baymard.com/blog/perceived-security-of-payment-form)). In CNN's account of NN/g's study, teenagers wanted sites "simple, but not childish" and disliked glitzy, blinking graphics ([CNN on NN/g](https://money.cnn.com/2005/01/31/technology/personaltech/Teenstudy/index.htm)), and in one informal test most 9–11-year-olds preferred a muted palette as more mature (weak evidence) ([Lullabot](https://lullabot.com/articles/ux-kids-personal-journey)). The move to pastels is therefore a defensible taste call that the evidence neither requires nor rules out: Seckler's best-rated colour settings sit nearer the old saturated palette, but in the same studies structure outweighed colour. What the evidence does tie to trust is consistency, and the site now mixes pastel tiles with the bright Duolingo colours of the prize bands, which also fail contrast; bringing the bands into the pastel-and-deep-ink style would solve both problems (an inference).

System preferences get more attention online than the data justify. No representative figure exists for dark-mode use: one university library's analytics found about 38% of visitors with dark mode on ([NCSU Libraries](https://www.lib.ncsu.edu/node/68686)), reader polls claiming over 80% overstate it ([Android Authority](https://www.androidauthority.com/dark-mode-survey-results-3682352/)), and the only Reduce Motion figure found, about 3.5%, comes from a talk with no stated source ([Ravenhall](https://noti.st/allisonravenhall/g5pGSf/present)). About half of websites now honour the reduced-motion setting but only about 13% offer a dark scheme ([Web Almanac](https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2025/accessibility)). Apple's advice for apps and games is to respond to Reduce Motion by "reducing automatic and repetitive animations, including zooming, scaling, and peripheral motion", and to check contrast in both appearances if dark mode is offered ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)); Material adds that saturated colours "vibrate" on dark backgrounds ([Material](https://m2.material.io/design/color/dark-theme)). The site now does what the follow-up asked: under reduced motion the hit ring, score pop, best-score bump and badge pops stay still, confetti and count-ups are skipped, and the moving light stays, as WCAG's exception for essential motion allows. A few small zooms remain (the countdown numerals growing from 40% to 112%, the game screen's entrance and the cabinet's shake) and could join the same rule. Dark mode stays optional, since dark text on a light background reads better for normal vision (earlier report) and pastel tiles would need separate dark versions.

## Phone lag outweighs skill at the top of the live curve

### Timing games score in bands and calibrate outside play

Shipped timing games score in bands of timing error, often scaled by difficulty. osu! awards its top grade within ±(80 − 6 × difficulty) ms, ±50 ms at medium difficulty, and its mania mode has a fixed ±16 ms top window (*full text*) ([osu! wiki](https://github.com/ppy/osu-wiki/blob/master/wiki/Beatmap/Overall_difficulty/en.md)); StepMania's default bands run ±22.5, 45, 90, 135 and 180 ms (*full text*) ([StepMania](https://github.com/stepmania/stepmania/blob/5_1-new/src/Player.cpp)); and Arcaea's top grade sits near ±25 ms, with an option that counts early and late hits (*second-hand*) ([Moegirl](https://mobile.moegirl.org.cn/en/Arcaea)). Friday Night Funkin' instead uses a continuous S-shaped curve for points, flat near zero error and steep around 55 ms, and keeps bands only as labels (*full text*) ([Funkin source](https://github.com/FunkinCrew/Funkin/blob/main/source/funkin/play/scoring/Scoring.hx)). Lab studies put practised adults' timing precision at roughly 5–22 ms (standard deviation), "much poorer" when the interception point is fixed in advance, as it is in stop-the-light ([Brenner & Smeets 2015](https://personal.fbw.vu.nl/~jsmeets/2015/Temporal_precision-JOV15.pdf)), and adults beat children at timing a response to a moving target ([Figueiredo & Barreiros 2013](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2013/46655/46655.pdf)). The follow-up's design rule for PresLink follows: a transparent, continuous points curve with word labels as an overlay; a small perfect zone no narrower than about ±3 ms; a floor; a slope set from pilot data so the median first paid play after practice lands near 650–750; perfect plays rare enough to expect only 0–3 in a campaign; tie-breaks published in advance; and the direction of every error shown ("12 ms late").

Phone latency is the variable that breaks this design. A robot measuring 26 popular smartphones found total screen-plus-touch latency of **35 to 140 ms**, variability the authors warn "could be misattributed as individual or group differences" ([Nicosia et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35953659/)). Across about 60,000 online testers, mobile users, especially on Android, were slower than computer users after controlling for age, education and vocabulary ([Passell et al. 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33954913/)), and browser-based reaction tests on touchscreens consistently overestimate response times, with device differences that "may affect reliability for individual differences" ([Pronk et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7280355/)). In a stop-the-light game that lag lands as a constant "late" bias on every tap, bigger than the differences in precision between ordinary players. Browsers help only partly: Chrome stamps a touch with the operating system's own input time to 0.1 ms, while Safari and Firefox round to 1 ms (Firefox to 16.7 ms with fingerprinting protection on), and whether Safari uses the system's time was not confirmed (*full text*) ([Chromium](https://github.com/chromium/chromium/blob/main/third_party/blink/renderer/core/dom/events/event.h); [MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/api/event/timestamp/index.md)). People partly adapt to a constant delay when they get feedback ([Stetson et al. 2006](https://beam.vt.edu/content/dam/fbri_vtc_vt_edu/publications/montague-publications/1-s2.0-S0896627306006271-main.pdf)), but one study found children aged 8–11 did not adjust where adults did ([Pisa Vision Lab 2014](https://www.pisavisionlab.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/2014_ChildrenDoNotRecalibrate.pdf)). Rhythm games deal with the problem outside scored play, through osu!'s universal offset and offset wizard (*full text*) ([osu! wiki](https://github.com/ppy/osu-wiki/blob/master/wiki/Client/Options/en.md)), Rock Band's sensor or tap-along calibration ([Harmonix](https://www.harmonixmusic.com/blog/how-to-calibrate)) and Guitar Hero Live's calibration during play ([Activision](https://support.activision.com/uk/en/guitar-hero-live/articles/advanced-calibration-for-a-guitar-hero-live-controller)).

### The live curve magnifies phone lag

The live curve makes the arcade unusually sensitive to that lag. Each light scores 50 + 300 × (1 − d) + 649 × e^(−d ÷ 0.05), minus up to 12 points over the first 3 ms, kept between 50 and 999, where d is the error as a fraction of the one-second sweep (`site/app.js`, migration `0022`). That top is harsher than any of the four curves the follow-up modelled: **1 ms of error costs 20 points, 5 ms costs 75 and 10 ms costs 133** (*computed here*), where the follow-up had already flagged an exponential curve losing 61 points at 5 ms as harsh near zero because it magnifies device noise for the best players. Running the follow-up's own model on the live curve gives these medians (*computed here*; the replication matched the follow-up's published table to within 2 points):

| Player's timing spread (assumed) | Median three-light average, phone lag 0 → 40 ms (follow-up's model) | Same, with the build's frame-by-frame scoring on a 60 Hz screen |
|---|---|---|
| Practised, 15 ms | 855 → 626 | 834 → 684 |
| Typical, 30 ms | 759 → 635 | 750 → 675 |
| Novice, 45 ms | 685 → 617 | 681 → 639 |

Two readings follow. With no extra lag, typical and novice first plays land around 680–760, inside or just above the follow-up's 650–750 target, so the earlier report's worry that first plays might feel too low is not borne out and the slope needs no urgent change, though a pilot should confirm the timing spreads, which are assumptions. But **40 ms of extra lag costs a practised player 150–230 points and a typical player 75–125**, and brings the practised and typical medians within about 10 points of each other: on a laggy phone the curve can barely see skill. The build already removes part of the lag by timing each tap from the operating system's touch timestamp and allowing one frame (16 ms) for the display, and those are the right first steps. No fixed allowance can absorb phone-to-phone differences of the size Nicosia measured, however, so the terms' line that "phones differ very slightly in how fast they register a tap" understates the problem.

### The screen's frame rate turns 999 into a lottery

The millisecond grid also meets a second limit, the screen. To make the light stop "where it was seen", the build scores each tap at the last frame drawn at least 16 ms before the touch (`site/app.js`), so the scored time is always the time of a frame: 16.7 ms apart on a 60 Hz screen and 8.3 ms apart at 120 Hz. The curve's 1 ms resolution is finer than any phone can display. **A flawless tap therefore scores anywhere from 795 to 999 on a 60 Hz screen** (median 889), and from 889 to 999 at 120 Hz (median 936), depending only on where the frames happen to fall (*computed here*). A 999 needs a frame to land on the mark's exact millisecond. On the model's assumptions about 1.3% of a typical player's lights and 2.2–2.5% of a practised player's score 999, so every thousand plays should produce roughly 40–75 lights at 999, or 5–22 on laggy phones, while a 999 average across all three lights stays rarer than 1 in 50,000 plays (*computed here*). The follow-up's rule of 0–3 perfects per campaign is met for whole plays and broken for single lights. Because the best-light prize breaks ties by "the earlier one wins", it will in practice go to whoever logs the first lucky millisecond: a race that rewards playing early and often, decided within a margin that phones, not players, control. That brings back in a new form the "partly chance" problem the 1 ms grid was meant to remove, and the README and code comments saying a 999 "keeps the top of the board to one person" describe the old random marks, not the current build.

The follow-up's fairness steps, in order of value, answer both problems. First come free, unscored practice-and-calibration lights before a device's first paid play, available at any time and each showing "x ms early/late" so players can adapt, which matters most for younger players. Second is a per-device offset in the manner of osu!: the median signed error of 5–10 calibration taps, capped at about ±80 ms, stored per device and browser, subtracted when scoring and published ("we adjust for your phone's delay"). A player who taps late on purpose during calibration gains nothing, since the offset only moves their own target; what remains is consistency, which the follow-up calls "a defensible and publishable definition of skill". Third are touch timestamps, a clock-driven light and server re-scoring, which the build already has. Fourth, no prize should hinge on a perfect zone a few milliseconds wide or on an uncalibrated first play. Fifth, logging only the operating system and browser family with each score lets the committee check, before awarding prizes, whether one platform dominates the top. Sixth, timing should come from visuals alone, because audio output delay varies by device ([MDN](https://github.com/mdn/content/blob/main/files/en-us/web/api/audiocontext/outputlatency/index.md)). For the frame problem, the calculation above suggests two options (an inference): make the top band at least one 60 Hz frame wide, so any error within about ±8 ms scores the top, or keep the curve and break best-light ties by a skill measure, such as the tied players' second-best light, rather than by time.

## Self-referenced, spend-neutral rewards suit a €2 game with child players

### Personal bests motivate more players than rank does

Comparing players with their own past has the better evidence. Cyclists racing a recording of their own earlier ride gained performance, intrinsic motivation and flow over four weeks compared with a non-competitive version, and the authors warn that competing against others "can negatively impact the intrinsic motivation" of less able people ([Race Yourselves, CHI 2020](https://researchportal.bath.ac.uk/en/publications/race-yourselves-a-longitudinal-exploration-of-self-competition-be/)). Feedback after good attempts helped adults learn a throwing task ([Chiviacowsky & Wulf 2007](https://www.sponet.de/sponet/Record/4012854)), though a study of 40 children found no difference ([RBEFE](https://revistas.usp.br/rbefe/en/article/view/16652)). Progress framing works best on whichever quantity is smaller, what remains when close to a goal and what is done when far from it ([Koo & Fishbach 2012](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-663827.html)), and Olympic bronze medallists looked happier than silver medallists because they compared themselves with fourth place ([Kill Screen on Medvec et al.](https://www.killscreen.com/bronze-medalists-happier-silver/)). Praise for effort beat praise for intelligence on persistence and enjoyment after failure ([Mueller & Dweck 1998](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf)), and inflated praise made children with low self-esteem less willing to take on a challenge (*second-hand*) ([BPS on Brummelman et al. 2014](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/inflated-praise-your-children-incredibly-bad-idea)). A person's position on a leaderboard strongly colours how they rate the board and the app around it, while extraverts like boards whatever their rank ([Jia et al. 2017](https://scholarworks.iupui.edu/bitstream/1805/17645/1/Jia_2018_designing.pdf)), so for most players a personal-best track does most of the motivating.

The result screen already does much of this. It opens with "New best" and a distinct sound on a personal best, otherwise with "Up 3 places" or a title; it shows the three lights, the player's best and the points needed for the next place; and confetti fires only for averages of 850 or more and never under reduced motion. Three gaps remain against the follow-up. There is no "need 905 on this light to beat your best" line during the third light, which the follow-up would show only when reachable (need = 3 × best − (light 1 + light 2) + 1, at most 999 and not far above the player's best single light), switching otherwise to progress made ("two lights averaging 812"). Celebration is tied to an absolute 850, so a newcomer's personal best of 640 gets a heading and a sound but no confetti while a strong player's ordinary 860 gets confetti, where the follow-up's tiers run from a calm summary below the player's own median to the largest effect for a first 900 or a top-10 entry, each kept to about 1.5 seconds and skippable. And the words inflate or misattribute: every light from 850 up is called "Perfect" when only 999 is, a typical play near 750 is titled "Superb", and a poor light is "Unlucky" in a game whose terms say "nothing in the score is random". Measurement-based praise of the process ("12 ms late", "all three within 30 ms: steady timing") fits both the praise evidence and the skill framing the legal position depends on; the coaching line already says "a touch late" and only needs the number.

### Rewards money cannot buy work when announced and earned

Rewards that money cannot buy have good field evidence, with a sharp caveat about how they are given. Randomly assigned, purely symbolic awards raised new Wikipedia editors' retention for a year ([Gallus 2017, via IGL](https://innovationgrowthlab.org/node/901)). But in a randomised trial with 15,329 students in grades 6–12, surprise awards for past perfect attendance **reduced** later attendance, most among low-performing students, apparently because they signalled the behaviour was unusual: 82% of recipients assumed they had missed fewer days than their classmates, against 27% of students who got no award ([Robinson et al. 2021](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/demotivating-effect-and-unintended-message-awards-0)). A dominant star makes rivals ease off, as PGA golfers did when Tiger Woods played ([Kellogg Insight on Brown 2011](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/golf_lessons)); gamers respect people less for buying an advantage than for buying cosmetic items ([Evers et al. 2015](https://www.ijis.net/ijis10_1/ijis10_1_evers_et_al_pre.html)); groups contribute more toward specific, challenging goals ([Beenen et al. 2004](https://presnick.people.si.umich.edu/papers/cscw04)); and donations accelerate as visible goals approach ([Cryder et al. 2013](https://www.cmu.edu/dietrich/sds/docs/loewenstein/GoalGradient.pdf)). Spelunky's daily challenge, with the same cave for everyone and one ranked attempt a day, shows the cleanest way to stop money multiplying a player's chances ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-24-hour-ticket-examining-daily-runs-)).

Measured against this, the site's changes are the right ones, with three loose ends. The first-play prize is Spelunky's single ranked attempt, the cleanest spend-neutral design the follow-up found, but it is fair only after practice and calibration: on the model above, 40 ms of lag costs a typical first-timer 75–125 points, as much as or more than the 70–75 points that separate typical from novice players. It also assumes one player per person, whereas anyone can register new initials and buy a fresh "first play" for €2, along with fresh spending limits, since the limits apply per player; the committee can enforce one player per person when winners collect, if the rules say so. Hiding play counts follows the evidence that bought status earns disrespect, the top-20 board with the player's own row limits the public display of low ranks (though only a periodic reset, such as a weekly board, would blunt the superstar effect), and the caps are hard limits, which the earlier report's evidence favoured over invitations to self-limit. The three main boards still rank each player's best score, though, and a player's best rises with the number of plays. Computed here, the expected best of a day's 14 plays (two 7-play bundles) sits about 1.7 play-to-play standard deviations above the player's average, and the best of the campaign's 70 plays about 2.4: **roughly +85 to +135 points for a €20 day and +120 to +190 for the €100 cap**, before any gain from practice, taking either the follow-up's assumed 50-point spread or the roughly 80 points the live curve gives a typical player under the model. The caps limit the advantage without removing it, so the terms' line "No prize depends on how much anyone spends" is true of the prize rules but not of the odds. The follow-up's options for a fully spend-neutral board are the best of each player's first five plays, a weekly best with one prize per person, or "most improved" measured against the better of the first two plays.

Free practice is where fairness and fundraising stop pulling against each other. The case that demos cost sales comes from confounded 2013 data on €40–60 console games, where trailer-only titles sold about twice as many units as those with a demo (*second-hand*) ([MCV](https://mcvuk.com/game-demos-cut-sales-by-half-study-claims/)), whereas in a panel of 711 Google Play apps a well-rated free version went with higher sales of the paid version ([Liu, Au & Choi 2014](https://www.jmis-web.org/articles/1219)). A practice light cannot replace a place on the board, so the follow-up expects little cost to sales and some gain in trust, untested. The fairness case is firm: without practice, a first paid play buys the phone's calibration and the player's first feedback. The follow-up's rules are that practice be unlimited, labelled "Practice: not scored", never shown on boards and never upsold ("you'd have scored 940, pay €2 to make it count" is exactly the pressure nudge to avoid with under-18s), with at least three practice lights on a device before its first paid play, a number to test in a pilot. Practice, calibration, published scoring and early/late feedback all reduce chance-like variation that depends on the device, which supports describing the arcade as a game of skill; but the follow-up still could not retrieve how the Gambling Regulation Act 2024 defines a "game", so the solicitor's view remains the gating step ([Law Reform Commission](https://revisedacts.lawreform.ie/eli/2024/act/35/section/2/revised/en/html)).

## Conclusion

The follow-ups change the question the arcade must answer. The earlier report asked whether the design pushed children to spend, and the site has largely settled that with hard caps, no play counts and a prize money cannot buy. The open question now is whether the score measures the player. In a timing game the instruments (the phone's lag, the screen's frame rate and the grid the marks sit on) are part of every score, and a curve sharp enough to separate the best players near zero error is also sharp enough to rank their phones. The 1 ms grid shows the trap neatly: it removed one source of chance and exposed a larger one hidden inside each frame. The answer is not a gentler curve but measurement: calibrate each phone, show each error in milliseconds, and keep prizes away from distinctions finer than the hardware can resolve.

Colour has followed the opposite arc. Contrast is solved, and calm pastels make colour a quieter, more grown-up cue, which suits a charity asking parents for money. The price is that colour now distinguishes almost nothing, least of all for the roughly one boy in twelve who is colour-blind; that is tolerable while initials carry identity, and fixable within the style by varying depth and showing the letter ink. Across both strands the earlier pattern holds: the levers with the best evidence (honest measurement, progress against one's own best, symbolic awards announced in advance and free practice) are also the ones a school charity can use without asking children to spend.

## Recommendations for PresLink Arcade

Items run in priority order within each group, with evidence strength in brackets. The earlier report's first step, a written legal view on whether a paid prize game open to under-18s falls outside Irish gaming and lottery law, remains the gating item and is not repeated here.

### Do now

1. **Add free, unscored practice that calibrates each phone.** Require at least three practice lights, labelled "Practice: not scored" and each showing "x ms early/late", before a device's first paid play, and keep practice available at any time, off every board and free of upsell. Store the median signed error of 5–10 calibration taps as a per-device offset capped at ±80 ms, subtract it when scoring and say so ([Nicosia et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35953659/); [osu! wiki](https://github.com/ppy/osu-wiki/blob/master/wiki/Client/Options/en.md)). [strong for the latency; practitioner for the method; *computed here* for the size]
2. **Stop exact-millisecond 999s from deciding the best-light prize.** Under "earlier wins" the prize goes to the first lucky millisecond. Publish a skill-based tie-break instead (the tied players' second-best, then third-best light), make 999 a pre-announced "Bullseye" badge rather than the decider, and consider counting only each player's first five plays so the prize is also spend-neutral. Correct the README and code comments that still describe random marks. [*computed here*; follow-up guidance]
3. **Make "one player per person" a rule and check it at collection.** State that only a person's first player counts for the first-play prize and that the spending limits apply per person, and check each winner's code, school email or payment email when prizes are collected. [inference from the code]
4. **Correct two lines in the terms.** Replace "Phones differ very slightly in how fast they register a tap" with an accurate line (phones differ by up to about a tenth of a second; practice measures and adjusts for yours), and turn "No prize depends on how much anyone spends" into "No prize counts spending; more plays give more chances at a best score, which is why spending is capped". [strong; *computed here*]
5. **Finish the contrast pass.** Put dark ink on the Everyone, Teachers and First play prize bands (7.0–8.2:1) or bring them into the pastel style; fix the bronze rank badge (dark ink on its orange gives 5.55:1); use a grey no lighter than #6e6e73 (5.07:1) for the miss verdict, result heading and board detail lines; give the moving light a solid 2 px dark outline (5.5:1 or better on every part of the track); and draw each colour swatch as a miniature tile with the player's initials in its ink. [standard; *computed here*]
6. **Reword verdicts in measured, process terms.** Show the error in milliseconds on every light, reserve "Perfect" for 999, drop "Unlucky", and stop calling a typical play "Superb" ([Mueller & Dweck](https://www.columbia.edu/cu/psychology/courses/3615/Readings/Mueller_Dweck.pdf); [BPS on Brummelman](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/inflated-praise-your-children-incredibly-bad-idea)). [moderate]

### Consider

1. **A spend-neutral main board.** Make one board the best of each player's first five plays, or a weekly best with one prize per person, so that a single €2 play can compete; otherwise the best-score boards keep a volume edge of roughly 85–190 points within the caps (*computed here*) ([Game Developer on Spelunky](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/the-24-hour-ticket-examining-daily-runs-)). [*computed here*; practitioner]
2. **Personal-best tension and tiered celebration.** Show "Need 905 on this light to beat your best" only when it is reachable, otherwise progress made, and tie confetti to a new personal best, a first 900 or a top-10 entry rather than a flat 850, kept to about 1.5 seconds, skippable, and absent for below-median plays ([Koo & Fishbach](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/jconrs/doi10.1086-663827.html); [Improbable Research on Kao](https://improbable.com/tag/rpg/)). [moderate]
3. **Colour that works as a cue within the pastel style.** Give the look-alike tiles different depths (blue, purple and indigo; pink and teal; green and orange) and a thin ink outline, then check the result with two or three colour-blind pupils; the illustrative depth-varied set above lifts the worst colour-blind pair from about 0.5 to 4–5.6 (*computed here*). [*computed here*; untested]
4. **Symbolic awards announced in advance, and a goal that counts people.** Announce badges for accomplishments, such as "Steady hand: all three lights within 40 ms", show them on the player's card and never give thank-you awards for playing often; add a whole-school milestone that counts people rather than euros, such as a staff-room final for the teachers once 200 different people have played ([Gallus via IGL](https://innovationgrowthlab.org/node/901); [Robinson et al.](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/demotivating-effect-and-unintended-message-awards-0); [Beenen et al.](https://presnick.people.si.umich.edu/papers/cscw04)). [strong field evidence for the award rules]
5. **A pilot week and a check before prizes are awarded.** Collect practice-light errors on real phones from students, teachers and parents to confirm the timing spreads behind the 650–750 target and set the offset cap; record only the operating system and browser family with each score and check that no platform dominates the winners; and consider a flat top band about one 60 Hz frame wide, so screen refresh rate cannot rank the best players. [model; follow-up]
6. **Finish the motion and theming details.** Add the countdown zoom, game-screen entrance and cabinet shake to the reduced-motion rule, and add dark mode only if players ask for it, with dark versions of the pastels and contrast checked in both themes ([Apple HIG](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). [practitioner]

### Avoid

1. **Prizes decided by an uncalibrated first play, an exact-millisecond 999 or a time-based tie-break among common scores.** [*computed here*]
2. **Meaning carried by colour alone**, such as red-versus-green hit and miss cues, red flashes on a miss, colour teams, "find your colour" or prize states shown by tile colour ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG21/Understanding/use-of-color.html)). [standard]
3. **Upsells from practice or at the limits**, such as "you'd have scored 940, pay €2 to make it count", a "Back tomorrow" invitation at the daily spending limit, or any colour, badge or tile tied to payment. [regulatory guidance; inference]
4. **Surprise "thanks for playing so much" awards and fanfare for below-median plays**, which signal the wrong norm and disguise losses as wins ([Robinson et al.](https://www.hks.harvard.edu/publications/demotivating-effect-and-unintended-message-awards-0)). [strong]
5. **Haptics as a necessary channel**, since iPhones get none ([MDN browser data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/Navigator.json)). [full text]
6. **Figures that failed verification**: "80% brand recognition", "red button +21%", "older users prefer less colour", "leagues gave 17% more learning", and retention comparisons across GameAnalytics editions. [verification]
